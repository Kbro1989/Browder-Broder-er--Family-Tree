Origins of the South Carolina Browder Family

Posted byjmarkbrowderFebruary 23, 2025February 23, 2025Posted inUncategorizedTags:ancestry, family-history, genealogy, history, slavery

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div.

Feb. 23, 2025

Based on what records that we do have, it is clear that the South Carolina Browder line starts with a John Browder (c1731-c1769) who married someone named Amelia (d. c1785). They are found in Bladen County, North Carolina first. In 1763, the Bladen County, North Carolina tax list names Henry “Bosswill” (Boswell) together with “Jno.” Browder (John Browder who married Amelia). It also names David “Braveboy” (Brayboy?). So, John Browder is placed there are early as 1763. What John’s relationship was with Henry Boswell is not known, but the fact that they are listed together in the same tax list demonstrates that they held property together. Perhaps it was a business.

I do not know yet who Henry Boswell actually was. But he is a clue in that he could well have been a relative of Amelia.

Another clue comes in the form of the Nov. 16, 1776 will of Thomas Sanders (Bladen Co., NC Deed Book 1738-1779, p. 5):

Thomas Sanders of Bladen County, NC states that he was very weak & sick, and bequeaths to his “bloved wife, Emilea…my children viz: Elizabeth Boswell, Elisabeth Ellis, Amelie Morley, Thomas Browder & Elay Sanders, my daughter in law.”

Wit: Duncan Morris, Senvill Hardwick.

One has to understand (as if this would be a real question) that Thomas Browder was a son of John Browder, not Thomas Sanders, even though Sanders calls him his “son” in his will. So, why did Sanders call Thomas his son? Because Amelia married Thomas Sanders after the death of John Browder. Thomas Browder was already an adult by that time since he is found in the land records, so he was born about 1749.

One has to presume that the above Elizabeth Boswell either married a Boswell or had the second name of Boswell but was not yet married. But an Elisabeth Ellis is also named in Sander’s will so that it becomes obvious that one was his daughter and one was his step-daughter. Since Elisabeth Ellis is named second, it seems that we can conclude that Elizabeth “Boswell” was a sister of Thomas Browder and, therefore, a daughter of John Browder and Amelia.

It is known that Henry Boswell (b. c1740), married an Elizabeth and some state that she was Elizabeth Sanders, based on this will. I am going to suggest that it is the other way around and that Thomas Sander’s daughter, Elisabeth, married an Ellis and that John Browder’s daughter, Elizabeth, married Henry Boswell because we see a direct relationship between John Browder and Henry Boswell early on, but do not see the same thing concerning Thomas Sanders. It seems obvious, thus far, that Henry Boswell was a son-in-law to John Browder, Boswell having married John’s daughter, Elizabeth.

Now, by 1770 Thomas Browder, son of John Browder and Amelia, is found in the land records for Bladen County, North Carolina. He purchased some land that year from Dennis Lennon.

We don’t know who Thomas Browder (b. c1749) married. But we can continue to trace him because in 1771 Thomas Browder sold some land in Bladen County, North Carolina to his half-brother, Christopher Sanders, son of the above Thomas Sanders.

On Apr. 27, 1778, Christopher Sanders, son of Thomas Sanders, of Bladen County, North Carolina, wrote his will. In it he mentions his daughter, Amelia Sanders, among others.

On Nov. 11, 1779, Thomas Browder, son of John Browder and Amelia, received a land grant of 200 acres in Bladen County, North Carolina. So, he was clearly still alive.

Amelia died about 1785 as shown by the fact that she deeded to her son, Thomas Browder, her property in Bladen County, North Carolina.

“AMELIA SANDERS TO THOS BROWDER NO 472

Bladen County, NC

Deed Book 1, page 391

To all to whom these presents Shall come I Amelia Sanders do send greetings Know ye that I the said Amelia Sanders of Bladen County and providence of No Carolina being very sick and weak of body but of perfect mind Memory do hereby for and in consideration of love and affection I have and do bear my beloved Son Thomas Browder have given and granted and do hereby give grant and bequeath unto my said beloved Son all my real personal Estate that is to say all my Money goods Bonds accompts my Cattle Hogs and Sheep Poultry and Negroes and all this property to me belonging not here mentioned with the condition of his letting my beloved daughter Lucy Ellis have one half of the pewter and with allowing me a sufficient Mentenance as long as please God let me live I hereby make over to my Son Thomas Browder as my free will and Gift as above Mentioned together with a full power to dispose of as he thinks proper given under my hand this twenty fourth day of Octr 1785

Signed Sealed & delivered       Amelia her ++++ mark Sanders

in presents of us

Wm Brown

Nancy Brogdon

Bladen August 1785 This Deed was proved by William Brown and ordered to be Registered

John White C. C.”

Further, the 1790 census of Bladen County, North Carolina lists Thomas Browder.

On June 22, 1795, Thomas Browder deeded his property to Johathan Meares (Deed Book 7, page 266). This deed was proven in September of 1799.

It becomes obvious that Thomas Browder and his family moved to Williamsburg, South Carolina about 1799 both because the above deed was proven in that year, and because of the fact that on December 11, 1807, Thomas Browder, Jr. petitioned the court in Williamsburg to be allowed to sell whatever property his father had to pay off debts.

“Petition of Thomas Browder [Jr.] for Thomas Browder Senr

Estates Box 4, Envelope 7

Probate Office, Williamsburg, SC:

Petition of Thomas Browder humbly sheweth That the estate of Thomas Browder Senr Decd is in same measure perishable and that the heirs are some of them minors – That the said estate is considerably in debt. I therefore wish you to grant me an order to sell the said property at public vendue and such terms as you think proper

And your petition will pray ~

Witness: Thomas (his + mark) Browder

J.W. Murray

11th Decr 1807”

“page 2 of Thomas Browder Senr Estate file, South Carolina, Williamsburg District) By David McClary Esquire Ordinary:

To. Thomas Browder

Whereas Thomas Browder Senr late of Said district decd lately died Intestate having whilst he lived and at the time of his death divers good rights and credits within the district aforesaid by means whereof the full deposition and power of granting the Administration of all and Singular the goods rights & Credits of the said decd and also Crediting the Accounts calculations and recomings of the Said Administration and a final dismission of the Same to me is Manifestly Known to Belong I desiring that the goods rights and Credits of the Said decd may be well and truly Administerd converted and disposed of; do herby grant unto the Said Thomas Browder in whose Fidellity in this behalf I very much confide full power by the tenor of these presents to Administer the goods rights & credits of the Said decd, which to him in his lifetime and at the time of his death did belong, and to ask _vy recover and reserve the Same, and to pay the debts in which the decd Stood Obliged, So far forth askes goods rights & credits will intend According to there rate and order of Law, being first Sworn on The Holy Evangelists of Almighty God to make a true and perfect Inventory Thereof and to exhibit the Same into the Ordinary Office of Said district in order to be recorded on or before the Thirty first day of October now next issuing and to render a Just and true Account calculation and recoving of the Said admin unto required; and I ordain dispute and constitute you the Said Thomas Browder Administration of all the good rights and credits of the Said deceased.

In testimony whereof I have hereunto Set my hand and Seal the Thirty first day of July Anno Domini 1807 and in the Thirty Second Year of American Independence

Ordinary Office,

Recorded Book B

Page 30”

Now, everyone understands that Arthur Browder (c1770-c1825) came from Bladen County, North Carolina (yet, he shows up in none of the records there, indicating that he was probably a minor at least almost until he moved to South Carolina). Since the only line shown in the records there is the one detailed above, it becomes obvious that Arthur Browder was a son of Thomas Browder, Sr. and a brother of Thomas Browder, Jr. So, the earliest Browders in the area of Williamsburg, SC would have been descended from either Arthur Browder or Thomas Browder, Jr. Sadly, I have yet to be able to detail the family of Thomas Browder, Jr. But that of Arthur Browder has been done.

The one remaining question is exactly where John Browder (b. c1731) who married Amelia came from. Previously I had connected him to John Browder II of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, who married Mary. I now have to accept that as a mistake because the local tax records are just too clear that John had an entirely different son named John who married Dianna and remained in Dinwiddie County until his death. Without going into extensive detail, the only real possibility is that John’s father was Edmund Browder (b. c1716), son of John Browder and Elizabeth Jones. This Edmund Browder (I have tried to point out, to little avail) was actually the father of the Edmund Browder who later moved, with his family, to Chatham County, North Carolina and who wrote his will there in 1785. People have, over the years, conflated two Edmund Browders into only one. Edmund Browder, Jr. is the one who married Ann Deloney. His father, Edmund, also married someone named Ann, whose maiden name is not known. In effect, the two brothers, Edmund Browder, Jr. and John Browder (both sons of Edmund Browder, Sr. and Ann) both moved at some point to North Carolina, being the first of the Browder family to do so. They moved to different counties, but that was by no means unusual.

Regardless, Arthur Browder was apparently still a minor in 1790 since he is not listed in that census of Bladen County, North Carolina as Thomas was. Thus, his birth date is estimated as about 1770. But he would have married Charlotte about 1792 in any case because I have been able to determine that their first son, Hardy Browder, was born in Bladen County, North Carolina about 1794. For me, this clearly indicates the possibility that Charlotte’s maiden name may well have been Hardy.

Hardy Browder did have a daughter named Charlotte (b. c1829), who married Henry Parker, and a son named Arthur (c1835-1860). Hardy Browder died in Pike County, Alabama.

This is not all that could be done here, but is all that I am going to do at this time.
