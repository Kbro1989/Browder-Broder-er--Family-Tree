Isham Richard Browder and Mary Ann Harris and Their Children

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (Updated on May 9, 2025)

Isham Richard Browder (1801-abt. Oct. 1885) and Mary Ann Harris (August 25, 1800-November 20, 1874) were both born in, and brought up as neighbors in, Rockingham County, North Carolina on Wolf Island Creek.  Mary Ann was the daughter of Levin Harris (c1775-1834) and Elizabeth (c1785-1843) (although some dispute this) of Rockingham County who also resided on Wolf Island Creek.  Richard was the son of Isham “Coleman” Browder (c1774-1832) and Martha (c1774-c1811) Browder, obviously named after both of his grandfathers, Thomas Isham Browder (c1732-1784) and Richard Browder (c1719-c1807).  Isham Richard Browder, then, was the recipient of the combined heritages of both the Arthur Browder (c1715-c1738) and Richard Browder lineages.

It is obvious from the extant records that Richard Browder never actually used his first name, Isham, except one time after moving to Tennessee, and he probably never used it prior to the move either.  In fact, on the one document that refers to him as Isham Richard Browder (shown later) from McNairy County, the name Isham has been marked through with a line.  The reason for this is uncertain, but it is certain that this was not a mistake on the part of the clerk because the clerk knew Richard Browder well.  In fact, we will see that Richard Browder wished to separate himself from the legacy of the Isham and Randolph families and to begin a new legacy of his own.  This is also probably the very reason that he and his family moved to McNairy County, Tennessee as soon as all other members of his immediate family had either already moved away or had passed on, they, having waited until Elizabeth Harris passed away before moving, she, being the last ancestor.

Richard Browder did not continue the legacy handed down to him intact, but, instead, built his own, based upon what little was left, which he, in turn, hoped to pass down to future generations.  He rejected the rather hurtful legacy of previous Browder and related family generations as demonstrated not only by the fact that he moved away, but also the fact that he willed property to all of his children, contrary to that which had been done before.

It seems reasonable to assume that Richard, knowing early on that there would be no real inheritance for him other than the land already deeded to him by his father, began as a young man to make a legacy of his own which was different, in many ways, from that of his father and grandfather.  I submit that Richard knew that issues concerning property would arise, perhaps having been told by his own father.  That some other family members seem to have, in my opinion, ignored this knowledge, which they were probably told also, and found themselves unprepared for it speaks volumes for the human tendency to hope in the face of certainty, rather than to prepare for the future.  Certain others seemingly hoped that things would not turn out the way they did.  This was, until research revealed it, our family’s forgotten chapter, which changed everything.

So, Richard Browder apparently did not want to be called “Isham”, because he was putting the past behind him.  He also apparently did not want other family members to contact him after the move to Tennessee.  That is why he, I believe, did not even tell other family members, except for the Whittemores, who remained there, that he and his family had moved from Rockingham County, North Carolina.  His family was the last of the Browders to move from Rockingham County.  For all anyone else in the Browder family would have known, he and his family were still there.  Some others outside of the family, left behind in Rockingham County, also obviously knew where the Richard Browder had family moved to.  But, again, it seems that other family members who had already moved away did not know.  This is the reason that, from this point on, I will refer to him mainly only as Richard Browder, as he obviously wished to be remembered.

It is well-known from several sources that the early McNairy County Browders were very private – in the lingo of the time “curious.”  They didn’t believe in other people knowing about their family business.  And, no doubt, they did not want to talk about the situation they had left behind in Rockingham County, North Carolina.  But, it seems that things certainly didn’t turn out the way Richard might have hoped after all once they made their move to McNairy County, Tennessee either.  The truth is, sometimes one can do all of the right things, but all still will not turn out as one hopes in the end.  But things were generally better after the move, and it probably was a good decision.

Surely it is true that the earliest McNairy County Browders knew of their history and descent, but later Browders knew absolutely nothing about either Isham Browder (Sr. or Jr.).  I can attest to this fact because of interviews I had with various family members over many years, beginning in the late 1970s.  No one left alive had ever heard of Isham Browder until my great-uncle, Herman E. Browder, uncovered the evidence by his own research and the help of a certified genealogist.  That is apparently the way Richard Browder wanted it to be.  He did not want the past to ever catch up with what he was trying to build in McNairy County.  He wanted to start completely over, and this is exactly what he and his family did, with minor exceptions.  And all of this while the family simply transplanted themselves to a place where many others from Rockingham County also settled.

Even Delia Frances Browder, daughter of Lee Roy Browder (my great-great grandfather), the repository of so much information about the Browder family heritage, apparently had no idea who Richard Browder’s parents were.  If she had, she would surely have told Herman E. Browder and, probably also, my own grandfather, Frank Browder.  Frank Browder knew her well, but when I used to talk with him about the Browder family history, he only knew a little about John Browder (Lee Roy’s father) and only knew about Richard Browder because Herman had told him about his existence.  Frank explained that when he was a child he knew that older family members would sometimes discuss the family heritage, but, as a child, one did not ask questions, and he was often told to go away and play somewhere.  “A child was to be seen, but not heard”, he would say.  Obviously, he did as he was told to do.  But, when I asked him about the family, he did his best to remember any little thing that he might have ever known.  If he had not, I would now know so very much less than I do.  I absolutely treasure my conversations with my Browder grandparents about both my Browder and Sanders family heritage.

Now, that this may actually have been meant as a temporary move has some evidence to support it.  Other family branches in McNairy County have always understood that the family had originally planned to move on farther, into Texas.  But this final move would never take place for reasons no one knew, although it was said that they delayed due to sickness in the family or livestock, and simply never renewed the effort after that point.

In any event, Herman, my grandfather’s brother, is the one who discovered our descent from the Isham Browders of North Carolina.  No doubt, many times he took the opportunity to talk with Delia F. Browder, daughter of Lee Roy Browder.  If she had known about either Isham Browder, she would have told him.  She could only go as far back as to Richard Browder, but was still able to state the fact that the McNairy County, Tennessee Browders came from North Carolina.  That is all she knew!  And Richard was her own great-grandfather!  How much had been forgotten in such a short time!  Richard Browder had completed his mission to get away from the earlier Browder heritage very effectively.  He perpetuated a complete rejection of the divided family who had painfully fought one another, through the legal system, over inheritance.  The pain of that family disagreement would follow these Browders only in that they, apparently, had no further contact with other family members who had moved elsewhere.

To get back to the basic knowledge of who they actually were, Richard Browder and Mary Ann Harris were married on January 19, 1819 in Rockingham County, North Carolina (Rockingham County Marriage Bonds Book [RCMBB], p. 27).  The bondsman was James Delap (remember that Richard’s grandfather, Capt. Isham Browder, died near Delap’s shop. James must have been a son or grandson of whoever owned that shop) and the witness was William Ferrel.  Richard was born in 1801 by my best estimate, and died in or about October of 1885, aged about 85 years, a nice, long life, especially in comparison to the three prior generations of his male Browder ancestors.

Mary Ann Harris was born on August 25, 1800 in Rockingham County, North Carolina.  She died on November 20, 1874, at 74 years of age, both evidenced on her headstone.  Both died in McNairy County, Tennessee and are buried beside one another in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery, which is located across the present-day Highway 142, relatively close to the location where their home once stood.  Richard obviously ensured that Mary Ann had a headstone, as she passed away before he did, but none has been found for him, although it is said by some that he once had one, which has since been destroyed.  Still, after diligent search, no evidence has been found for the existence of his headstone. Only a large rock seems to have marked his grave.

The Richard Browder home itself is described as having been a white, two-story wood frame home, similar to most others in the area at that time (like that of Fielding Hurst, which still stands).

Now, unfortunately, the only known notation directly referring to Richard Browder in any extant publication comes from Reminiscences by Gen. Marcus J. Wright, first published in 1882, in which he stated the following:

“Richard Browder settled at an early date on Oxford Creek, where he now lives at an advanced age.  He has always been a good citizen, and highly respected by all his neighbors” (p. 84).

One wishes that more would have been written about him, because his story is obviously quite interesting.  But, again, he was a very private person and probably would not have told anyone much if they had even interviewed him.  Perhaps Wright tried, but Richard may have been unwilling to assist.

Helen Sides-Dye wrote that. “Thomas Jefferson took the oath as our third President March 4, 1801.  With their background as Virginia planters, it is only natural to assume that the BROWDERS were supporters of Thomas Jefferson. . . .  The Jeffersonians of the South were the landed aristocracy, and the records indicate that ownership of land was very important to the BROWDERS.” (Sides-Dye, p. 106). Of course, Helen did not know of the family relationships here.

Certainly, it is true that land ownership was very important to Richard Browder.  This did not change, even though Richard seems to have worked hard to repudiate the past of his ancestors.  Richard Browder, in fact, owned much property in McNairy County and was one of the larger land owners, although he was not “rich”.  But his closest associates were among those who owned the most land in the county and who were rich, by the standards of the day and that area.

So, the legacy did continue on in this respect.  The early McNairy County Browders were intent upon perpetuating land ownership, and it is completely clear that Richard Browder had intended to pass reasonable sections of land down to all of his children (including the girls).  The change, therefore, was that the property would not go only to one male child, as had been the standard with reference to both Thomas Isham Browder and Isham C. Browder.  And slave ownership was still practiced by Richard Browder but was clearly less important than it had been to either his father or his grandfather.  In fact, Richard only owned four slaves, as far as is known, while his grandfather had owned several, which Richard’s father had utilized.  So, essentially, Richard invested more in land than in slaves, which was probably a good thing, as things turned out.

Now, although there seems to be no extant provable documentation to confirm this (I have literally scoured what records are still available, but so many have been destroyed), it seems obvious to me that Richard Browder was an American soldier during the War of 1812.  I base this primarily on his known relationships.  Because of his young age, Richard Browder would have enlisted no earlier than 1814, and more likely in 1815.  Richard could have taken part in the Battle of Horseshoe Bend in Mississippi Territory (as Benjamin Wright of McNairy County did), which took place on March 27, 1814.  During this battle, Maj. Gen. Andrew Jackson and his forces defeated the Creek Indians.  Richard would likely have met his good friend, John Green Randolph (records show that he did serve), while serving during this war.  They would likely, then, have both fought still later under Maj. Gen. Andrew Jackson during the Battle of New Orleans in January of 1815, the final battle of the war.  It is known that Randolph did participate in this battle.

Also, just as John G. Randolph is known to have done, Richard Browder likely continued serving beyond the end of the war, still under Gen. Andrew Jackson, during the Indian Wars.  If so, Richard likely served during the First Seminole War in Florida from 1816 to 1818, after which he obviously returned home to North Carolina.  Richard Browder, therefore, would have served in the army for about three years, a standard enlistment.  And the very next year we know that Richard Browder married Mary Ann Harris and began a family.  So, the timeline fits.

This war service was only Richard’s first move toward differentiating himself from his ancestors.  Yes, his grandfather had served as a Captain in the Militia during the American Revolutionary War, and Richard would have been keenly aware of this.   But Richard’s service was different.  He likely served as a Private and, if he rose in rank, he probably did not rise very high in rank.  In other words, he served as a common soldier rather than as an officer.  And it seems obvious that he served in more areas of the country, and for a longer period, in total, than his ancestors had.  The only similarity would have been that the United States was once again at war with Great Britain.

According to the censuses of the time, both Richard and Mary Ann Browder could read and write.  According to the 1820 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina, the couple had one son under ten years of age at that time and they owned no slaves.  This first son was John Browder, my great-great-great grandfather.  The Richard Browder family at that time resided close to Richard’s father, Isham “Coleman” Browder, who still had another son, and a daughter residing with him in 1820.  William Bethell, a family friend, also resided fairly close to this family during that time.

Richard and Mary Ann Browder had, by this time, established themselves a short distance away from the old home place where his grandmother, Talitha Browder, still resided close to Elizabeth Browder, widow of Augustine Claiborne Browder. 

While still in Rockingham County, North Carolina, Richard Browder also performed several real estate transactions, other than the ones in which his father, Isham, gave him property.  First, on January 26, 1826 he deeded 168 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Clement Whittmore, Jr. (RCDB Z, p. 310).  Later, on September 23, 1835, he witnessed the sale of her one-sixth share of 689 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, by Anne Elizabeth “Eliza” Browder, daughter of Augustine C. Browder, et. al., to Luke Palmer (RCDB 2nd G, p. 5).  This is probably Luke Palmer’s initial purchase of land in the area.

By 1830, according to the census of Rockingham County, North Carolina, Richard and Mary Ann Browder had two male children under five years of age, one between five and ten years of age, and one between ten and fifteen years of age.  They also had one female child under five years of age and one between five and ten years of age.  In that year the family’s closest neighbors were Nathaniel Roach, Isaiah Hancock, Daniel Carroll, Elizabeth Browder, Walter Martin, John Meador, William Meador, John C. Cox, John J. Wright, Levin Harris and an uncertain Browder who appears to have the name Anderson Browder (but the original is very difficult to read).

In 1834, Richard Browder, still in Rockingham County, received 581, and also 94, acres of land (RCDB 2nd N, pp. 342 and 352), situated on Wolf Island Creek and Lickfork Creek in Rockingham County, North Carolina, as an “inheritance” from Levin Harris, through Richard’s wife, Mary Ann.  It is known that Levin Harris died by May of 1834, leaving his widow, Elizabeth. I state this even though the documents do not specify that it was an “inheritance”. The proximity of Levin’s death and these transactions make it obvious.  Levin Harris wrote a will that covered everyone else.

Later, on February 10, 1835, Richard Browder stood as bondsman for the marriage of Claiborn Harris (c1815-Oct. 1849) and Minerva Settle (daughter of Benjamin Settle and Mary Reid/Reed) in Rockingham County, North Carolina.  Claiborne was a son of John Harris, Jr. (c1776-c1841) [brother of Levin Harris] and Ellender “Nellie” Atkins (c1777-c1850), daughter of Daniel Atkins, of Rockingham County.

Still later, on August 23, 1836, Clement Whittmore and his wife, Martha, sold 173 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Richard Browder (RCDB 2G, p. 194).

By 1840, according to the Rockingham County census, Richard and Mary Ann Browder had one male child in each of the following age groups; 20-29, 15-19, 10-14, 5-9, and under 5 years of age.  They also had one female child, aged fifteen to nineteen, one female child between ten and fourteen, and two female children under five years of age.  This family still did not own any slaves during this time period, but by this time their family was complete, and all of their children were born in Rockingham County, North Carolina.  At that time, they did not reside far from the Walter Martin, Andy Martin, E. R. Harris, John Harris [Jr.] and John Meador families.  They also resided close to three Carroll families, Larry Carroll, William Carroll and Daniel Carroll.  Several other Martin families also resided close by at that time.

Between 1842 to 1847, and following the death of her husband, John Harris, Jr., Nellie-Atkins-Harris moved with her children to McNairy County, Tennessee.  According to the 1850 census, she was residing with her daughter, Martha (Nov. 22, 1816-Sept. 30, 1890), wife of Abram Young, close to Monteray (now Michie), McNairy County, Tennessee.  Next door was the home of her son, Caleb Harris (c1806-bef. 1860), in which also resided two of Caleb’s brothers, John (July 7, 1835-Jul. 5, 1911) and William (b. c1818). 

This has led some researchers to conclude that Mary Ann was a daughter of John, Jr. and Nellie Harris since these families resided so close by in McNairy County, but they are clearly incorrect, in my view, and according to the records as I see them.  This family could very well have moved to McNairy County at about the same time that the Richard Browder family moved there, but there is no other causal connection.

Be that as it may, during the American Civil War, Federal troops came so close to the home of Nellie-Atkins-Harris, that she and her children fled the area into Mississippi to avoid them.  This, obviously, would have taken place just prior to the Battle of Shiloh, since Federal troops did advance through that area on their way to that historic battle.  Thereafter, this Harris family remained in Marshall County, Mississippi.  

However, it is important to also note here that federal troops also came very close to the Richard Browder family, and the Battle of Shiloh took place not very far from their property.  Yet, they did not flee or move to Mississippi.  One might think that, if there was a strong family relationship between these families, that the Browders would have also moved along with this Harris family. 

The following is the marriage license of Claiborn Harris, showing the signature of Richard Browder.

NOTE:  Those who believe that Mary Ann Harris was the daughter of John Harris, Jr. and, therefore, a sister of Claiborn Harris, also cite this marriage record as part of their “proof”.  Of course, this record is proof of absolutely nothing other than the fact that these families knew, and were likely related to, one another (and they were).  And, in fact, these other researchers should note that, although these families both moved to McNairy County, Tennessee and resided fairly close to one another, there is absolutely NO evidence at all that they associated with one another in any way whatsoever after these moves took place.  If they were such close family units, why might this be the case?  In any case, it is neat to have Richard Browder’s signature here.

Now, in 1842 Jeremiah Mize deeded some land (acreage unknown) on Lickfork Creek to Richard Browder (RCDB 2nd M, p. 12).  And Richard Browder sold the 94 acres of the land he had previously received from Levin Harris, which bordered Richard’s own property, to Luke Palmer on August 28, 1842 (excepting about 30 acres of said land of the interest of Ira Harris, and about 60 acres of the fee simple of Elizabeth Harris, Levin’s widow), all of which was situated on Lickfork Creek, for $300.00 (RCDB 2nd N, p 353).  Then Richard Browder sold the rest of the property of Levin Harris, on August 28, 1843 (RCDB 2nd N, pp. 342-43) of 581 acres, which was situated on Wolf Island Creek, for $2,486.00.  Both deeds were finally proven during the May term of 1844.  Richard Browder had once owned at least 1,331 acres of land in Rockingham County altogether.

After May (probably in June) of 1844, the Richard Browder family began the move, across the Appalachian Mountains through the Cumberland Gap, to McNairy County, Tennessee, eventually settling on Oxford Creek, sometime later in 1844.  So, the Richard Browder family moved from Rockingham County, North Carolina to McNairy County, Tennessee almost exactly 60 years after the death of his grandfather, Capt. Thomas Isham Browder. 

According to family tradition, Richard Browder first actually walked across the Appalachian Mountains, saw the land of Tennessee, and then returned to bring his family to Tennessee.  One should not find the idea that Richard walked all that way too far-fetched, since it is known that several other pioneers did just that, including William T. Anderson (b. 1804), who did so at the age of seventeen, walking all the way to Madison County, Tennessee (Mitchell, p. 133).  Perhaps Richard Browder did this during his return journey from Florida to North Carolina after the Seminole War and maybe along with Anderson.  Indeed, it is not impossible at all that Richard Browder could have been a part of the Natchez Trace Expedition of 1818, along with Jonathan Crook (an ancestor through my mother) and his brother Jeremiah Crook, together with expedition leader David “Davy” Crockett, which “discovered” West Tennessee.  The Browder and Crook families were neighbors and associates early on in Virginia.

There may be some corroboration to this belief in that there is also the suggestion, again from family tradition, that Richard traveled to McNairy County (possibly along with the John Green Randolph family) about 1827, at which time Richard staked out his original claim.  Afterward he is said to have returned to Rockingham County, to then return to McNairy County with his family in 1844.  So, in all probability, Richard Browder himself made two or three trips in all to West Tennessee, settling in McNairy County during his final trip.  This would not have been unusual either, as others are also known to have done similar things.

It does seem obvious, at least, according to family tradition and extant records combined, that the family’s move was planned well in advance of the time in which it took place and the only thing they were waiting for was the demise of Elizabeth Harris.  And if Richard Browder first traveled to McNairy County along with his friend John G. Randolph, just as his distant cousin Isham Browder of the American Revolutionary War traveled to Ohio, where he had been granted lands for his Revolutionary War service, along with Nathaniel Fitz Randolph, the “coincidence” here is just too sweet to ignore!  The Browder family and the Randolph families were working together yet again in both cases. 

It is also true that today the former properties of Richard Browder, and that of John G. Randolph, which Richard later purchased, are still known to be some of the best farmland in McNairy county.  They probably both produced cotton on this land and the Browder and Randolph families would have taken their cotton bales to Dayton (later known as Henderson Station) to be sent to market, once this railroad depot town was established. 

It is documented that John Green Randolph, along with his family, did arrive in McNairy County prior to 1830.  The two, Richard Browder and John Green Randolph, eventually owned large, adjoining, tracts of property.  So, they were “neighbors” in McNairy County, although John G. Randolph actually resided some distance away in the town of Montezuma.

In fact, the State of Tennessee granted Richard Browder two initial known tracts of land in McNairy County, Tennessee.  Both were issued on May 18, 1844.  The first tract was for 443 acres (Grant #649/TGB 1-A, p. 649) and the second was for 25 acres (Grant #5369/TGB 6-A, p. 746).  Curiously, the second tract for 25 acres is repeated in the records.  Both tracts were located on Oxford Creek and were formerly lands that had belonged to John Oxford.  John Oxford was granted 20 acres of land by the State of Tennessee in the 9th Surveyor’s District, Range 11, Section 2 on June 22, 1826 by virtue of his occupant claim to said land.  This grant began at the north-east corner of #1071 and ran east 45 poles, then south (McNairy County, Tennessee Land Grants, 1823-1851, #426).  Then, Jacob Oxford was granted a total of 200 acres of land, in parcels of 25 acres each (certificate #s 3165, 3166, 3167, 3168, 3169, 3170, 3171 and 3172), all located in the 9th Surveyor’s District, Range 4, Section 1, bordering the lands of William Petty (ibid, #506).  Then, on June 30, 1826 the State of Tennessee granted a reserve of 50 acres as an occupant claim to John Oxford, located in the 9th Surveyor’s District, Range 4, Section 2, at the north-east corner of #426, running east 90 poles and then south (ibid, #111).

Later, Jacob Oxford purchased two acres of land in the 9th surveyor’s district, third range and third section, in McNairy County, Tennessee from William Garrett, on September 9, 1827 (McCDB A, p. 15).  This land was part of a 285 acre grant.  And Jacob Oxford also purchased 182 acres of land in the 9th surveyor’s district, range 3, section 3 (Grant #205) in McNairy County, from William West, Jr. of South Carolina, on August 1, 1828 (McCDB A, p. 45).  Both of these tracts were located on what would become Oxford Creek.  Later, Jacob Oxford sold 92 acres of land to Abel U. Murry on August 19, 1828.  This was the southern section of the land originally granted to William West, Jr. (McCDB A. p. 46).  Jacob Oxford also sold 93 acres of land to John Ham on June 22, 1829 (McCDB A, p. 60).  He also sold 106 acres of land to John Norwood on June 22, 1829 (McCDB A, p. 61).  This land was located next to that of William Petty.  Jacob Oxford also sold 95 acres of land to Lany Moore on April 14, 1829 (McCDB A, p. 62).  So, the above lands of Jacob Oxford are not the lands that Richard Browder later acquired.  Jacob and John Oxford may have been brothers with adjoining tracts of land.

The Richard Browder family is known to have settled on the north branch of Oxford Creek.  It is also believed that Richard Browder was among those who met in December of 1844 in McNairy County, Tennessee as a church body, formed to appoint a building committee.  This committee approved the building of a log structure, located on the Purdy-Stantonville Road, which was called Clear Creek Baptist Church.  The actual records have been missing for years (some say that they were taken by a family who moved to Texas) so it cannot be proven that Richard Browder took part in this.  However, once Clear Creek Church was established, it was at that early time the closest church to the family.  And this family certainly were Baptists at that time.  I know this from my earliest interviews with family members.  Their faith is one thing that was remembered well about this family.

This church had actually first been organized in 1828, being the oldest Baptist Church in McNairy County.  It is obvious from extant evidence that the family of Thomas Saunders, Sr. was instrumental in its establishment in 1828.  However, in 1841/42, a split ensued between the Missionary and Primitive Baptists, which led to the founding of the Clear Creek Church (when the name Clear Creek was actually adopted) of 1844.  The church of 1844 was actually, then, the second church building, after which a third building was erected on land donated by Roy Luttrell, which was finished in 1847.

Again, the Richard Browder family were Baptist by the time they settled in McNairy County, although the family is also known to have attended a Methodist Church in the area.  No one I interviewed was certain which Methodist Church they may have attended, but it seems likely to me that it may well have been Shiloh Methodist Church since so many of their neighbors and descendants are known to have often attended that church.  But, no doubt, Richard himself became a Baptist due to the influence of his wife’s family, who had obviously been early Baptists back in North Carolina. But one must remember that his grandmother, Talitha, was also a member of Wolf Island Baptist Church as probably were Richard’s parents.

Richard Browder also purchased a small family of slaves at Purdy from Brown & Hampton on August 8, 1845 (McCDB C, p. 18), not long after arriving in McNairy County, Tennessee.  This slave family included a mother and three children.  This is the first surviving record mentioning Richard Browder in McNairy County, Tennessee.  And this is the first (and last) time that Richard Browder is known to have actually purchased slaves on his own.  He is not known to have done so back in North Carolina except in a single instance.  In addition, it is known that the descendants of this family retained the surname “Browder” but moved elsewhere some years ago.  It is said that they moved to California.

So, the transition from North Carolina to Tennessee also marked a transition from non-slave ownership to slave ownership for this particular household.  And this, even though the two previous generations of the Browder family had been slave owners all of their lives.  Why this was the case may always remain an open question.  It may have something to do with which Baptist sect they adhered to at a given time.  Perhaps they switched from the Primitive Baptist faith to the Missionary Baptist faith.

Randolph, from whom Richard Browder purchased it.

Thus, the Richard Browder family, and their descendants, remained in Tennessee and did not follow his sister’s family on to Texas (she, having died in Mississippi in any case).  It was only a few years after their arrival in McNairy County, but they elected to stay rather than to move on.

All in all, according to the extant land grants and deeds and Richard Browder’s will combined, Richard had owned at least 1,542 acres of land altogether in McNairy County, Tennessee.  He also made reference to 155 acres of land he gave to Eleanor Browder-Willis in his will, which must be included in this total.  There can be little doubt that her land was located at the far southern end of the 443 acre tract (because descendants of this Willis family continue to reside in this area even today) and that Richard gave some of his other daughters land from this property as well, even though he never states this.

Richard’s total land holdings included the acreage purchased from John G. Randolph of 640 acres, plus that granted to him by the State of Tennessee of 134 acres in 1849, as well as the tract of 443 acres, the 25 acre tract and, finally, the original 300 acre homestead tract.  This is more than Richard had owned in Rockingham County, North Carolina, altogether.

Again, through his will, Richard Browder gave Franklin Browder most, if not all, of his original homestead land, while his brothers received roughly equal amounts of about 100 acres apiece, more or less, of the land purchased from John G. Randolph.  And John Browder received another 100 acres of land, totaling 200 acres altogether for him.  Benjamin F. Sanders actually acquired more than anyone else because of all of his purchases from Richard, as well as from his children.  It seems that Richard was somewhat annoyed with the fact that Benjamin F. Sanders had obtained so much of it by various means, as exemplified by Richard’s will.

No doubt, Richard Browder had wanted his family to, more or less, stay together, and that is why he arranged the separate plots in the way that he did.  In fact, John Browder’s land was central among all of the plots, and Franklin Browder’s land was next to the land that Richard wanted his daughters to have.  It seems, therefore, that Richard had intended for his eldest and youngest sons to function as mediators with the other family members.  He had no desire for family members to fight, or sue, over property like previous generations had done.  The eldest and youngest brothers were obviously supposed to enforce the peace between other family members, and it seems that they accomplished this, for the most part.

Now, if we put all of the evidence together, then the following picture can be drawn.  Richard Browder was indeed a young soldier during the War of 1812, and knew both John Green Randolph and Joseph Carroll from his service during that time.  Once Richard Browder and John G. Randolph left the army, they traveled together into Tennessee.  Richard himself came all the way to McNairy County to see the land.  But why would he do this?  After all, he would not have received any land grants directly from his service in the war.  So, what land would it have been that he had traveled to see?  The answer has to lie with the American Revolutionary War.  Former soldiers were granted lands in the west for their service in that war, especially in McNairy County, Tennessee, which had been set aside for exactly this purpose.  Richard traveled to see land that he already knew about.  There was a plot of 100 acres of land which Richard later gave to his son, John, that is not accounted for in the extant records (it was probably in the lost McNairy County deed book), all of which are detailed here.  It had to be this plot of land that Richard had traveled to see and finalize his claim to.  After Richard did this, he left said land in the care of his friend, John G. Randolph, and returned to Rockingham County, to return with his family some years later.

Richard’s “claim” was already there, and he knew about it.  There can be little doubt that this was a grant that had been earned by his grandfather, Capt. Thomas Isham Browder, for his service during the American Revolutionary War.  But, both he and his son died before they could make any moves toward securing it.  Indeed, both may have planned, during their lifetimes, to actually settle there with their families.  But this never happened due to circumstances beyond their control.  So, it became Richard’s responsibility to secure the grant.  No one had bothered to sue for this piece of land.  Perhaps other family members overlooked it, if they even knew about it.  But it becomes obvious that it was because of this piece of land that Richard Browder moved his family to McNairy County, Tennessee. THIS is the reason they moved to McNairy County, Tennessee, not because of other relatives.

Now, it was also during the lifetimes of the first generations of Browders in McNairy County that the Browder Schoolhouse was built.  In fact, it seems likely that Browder Schoolhouse, and Browder Chapel, were one and the same building, contrary to what I had believed during my early research.  The reason that this may be true is because it is known that weddings did take place at Browder Schoolhouse, and that schoolhouses during that era often doubled as churches/chapels.  And, just like others during that era, it would have been a one room structure.  In fact, those who remember it state that it was a white, wood frame, structure just like others in the area.  But, as memories fade, the schoolhouse and the chapel were not remembered as having been one and the same building.  Thus, just a bit of confusion among later generations.  However, I can safely state that Ms. Pauline “Polly” Hockaday-Reynolds recounted to me that the Browder Schoolhouse also had a large stage inside of it.  She also recalled that when she was growing up, even the old blackboards were still on the walls.  So, the building survived for some time.

But the Browder Schoolhouse eventually fell into disuse, and was later replaced by a new one called Oxford Creek Schoolhouse, which was located “just over the ridge” from Browder Schoolhouse in the same area.  In fact, it is known that children attending Oxford Creek Schoolhouse in later years would take field trips to see the old Browder Schoolhouse building.  This information was provided to me by Roy and Mansel Michael, who used to go to school at Oxford Creek Schoolhouse.  Mansel took my brother, Tracy, and I, throughout the woods in that area on April 29, 1989, showing us the former locations of the school buildings, etc.  He stated that the Browder Schoolhouse was located back in the woods, walking from the intersection of McNairy Central Rd. and Cox Lane.  We walked quite a good way, but never quite found the location that day.  He also showed us that the Oxford Schoolhouse was located across the road from the house on Cox Lane that my father built, down in a field there.  We didn’t try to walk to it.  We learned a lot from Mansel Michael during that and later interviews.  But a good bit of new housing has been built in this area since that time, and some of the locations have been built over or destroyed by these housing developments.

It seems obvious, at any rate, that the earliest members of the McNairy County Browder family built, and taught at, the Browder Schoolhouse, since several sons of Richard and Mary Ann Browder were apparently educated at Purdy Male and Female Academy.  It seems virtually certain to me that John Browder, at least, did teach school there.  The schoolhouse was obviously located on his land.

The Browder Schoolhouse was actually located at what is today just north of HWY 142, behind the home of Jack Martin, close to the Browder cemetery there.  When exactly it was built and started is unknown.  However, it could not have been started prior to the time when Richard Browder purchased the land from John G. Randolph in 1848 because it was clearly located on that tract of land.  By 1916 it was no longer shown on area maps, while Oxford Creek Schoolhouse was shown.  And, a list of schools in McNairy County, by district, published in the McNairy County Independent on November 26, 1906, does not list Browder School, but does list Oxford School.  So, it is safe to assume that the Browder Schoolhouse building was no longer used for classes by late 1906 (Mitchell, History of Schools, p. 9). 

Pauline Hockaday-Reynolds, again, has stated to me that she knows that the building was still around as late as the 1930s.  She also said that she knows that it was the Browder Schoolhouse, not the Oxford Creek Schoolhouse, because that is what her mother and others told her growing up.  And she is the one who finally was able to confirm its actual location for me.  In any case, it is certain that the building was around, and it was still being used, as late as 1893 (the year that my ancestor, John Browder, died).

It is not known exactly what happened to the Browder Schoolhouse building, or exactly when its demise occurred.  However, that area used to be listed on maps as “Browder Schoolhouse Hollow” and is in the area of Oxford Creek where the Browder family resided.  The schoolhouse was actually located somewhat south of the main hollow area.  Nancy Wardlow-Kennedy, who resided very close by, also confirmed what Mansel Michael had told me years, after he took us around that area.

Returning to the division of land, it seems to me that the determining factor was Richard Browder’s desire to treat all of his children more or less equally.  Previous Browder generations had tended to give all, or most, of their lands to only one son.  Certainly, both Isham Browders in North Carolina did this, even though they easily had enough to divide between all of their children.  However, Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia (c1819-c1807) seems to have divided his land more or less equally, thus initiating a relatively new trend within the family.  Richard Browder of McNairy County, Tennessee obviously followed this trend, set down by his grandfather and namesake.  By doing so, he clearly “broke the mold” of some previous generations.  Remember, Richard Browder clearly did not want to continue the legacy of his Isham Browder ancestors. He instead chose to be like his grandfather, Richard Browder.

But the divided-up land was simply unable to support several large, individual families, who initially had to take care of about 100 acres or more apiece, suddenly without the assistance of slave labor.  So, the majority of these families eventually sold the lands they were given.  Even so, they continued to have difficulty making ends meet.

John, Isham, A. J., and Franklin Browder, along with B. F. Sanders, seem to have done the best toward keeping what they had been given during their generation.  And in some instances, they purchased land from their siblings in order to still retain as much of the original family lands as they could, and also purchased other nearby lands.  I have seen this scenario play out in many families.

This is the one extant text which provides, unless one does not accept this as evidence, the full name of our Isham Richard Browder, even though, as previously discussed, the name “Isham” is marked through.  I do not believe that the clerk made a mistake here, but did mark through the name Isham at Richard’s request.  In fact, having seen and read and even retyped many, many land deeds, I can attest to the fact that, if this was a mistake by the transcriber, it is the one and ONLY instance of exactly this type of mistake I have ever seen.

Then, on about October 8, 1858, Richard Browder gave his son, Andrew Jackson Browder, 160 acres of land.  This land was located in the northeast corner of entry #1071 (McCDB G, p. 151).

Finally, on April 7, 1869, Richard Browder deeded to Benjamin F. The property just north of the “Clem Sears tract” had already been deeded by Richard Browder to his first son, John.  Exactly when this had taken place is not known since records are unavailable (and that is probably also in the lost McNairy County deed book).  This tract was located in between the tracts of Benjamin F. Sanders and Andrew Jackson Browder, a part of entry #1071.  What is certain is that Richard Browder deeded sections of his #1071 tract to his sons (except for Franklin) sometime after they were married in each case.  His deed to Benjamin F. Sanders also, obviously, occurred after Benjamin and Mary were married.  It seems that he waited until each family was well-established, and had had at least one child, and then deeded land to each of them in turn. 

It also seems obvious from this evidence, and the fact that Richard and Mary Ann never moved, that Richard Browder likely was among those who voted for the railroad not to pass through Purdy.  Richard Browder was, at least at the beginning, moderately wealthy and, therefore, a relatively large tax payer.  Most large tax payers in the area opposed the railroad because of the proposed increased tax associated with it, and they certainly did not want it to go through their pretty little town of Purdy!  And, if said railroad had been built as planned, it would have gone through Richard Browder’s land too.  Obviously, he would not have wanted this to happen.

The original Richard Browder home place in McNairy County, as well as Browder’s Chapel Cemetery, are known to have been located just north of the Old Purdy-Stantonville Road.  This home place, and his other land holdings altogether, created a considerable piece of property.  Browder Schoolhouse, again, was located along what was an intersection of the old Purdy-Stantonville Road and the Old Stagecoach Road (which led on from Purdy to Corinth, Mississippi), close to the intersection of Cox Lane and McNairy Central Road today.  This Old Stagecoach Road also led on to Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery.  The impression of an old road in the area next to this cemetery is still evident today.  And, as can be seen from the map (shown next), the Richard Browder family literally resided “on Oxford Creek” as has always been said.  Unfortunately, the exact location of every Browder family home cannot be ascertained with certainty, but can only be approximated.

It has also been suggested that Richard Browder may have been a merchant as well as a farmer, since there used to be a store in the area of Oxford Creek where the family resided that was known as “the Old Browder store building” by later generations.  I have heard of this store building several times over the years, but no one knew of its exact location.  This is clearly not the one that Polk Browder later owned (which was in a different location).  It is possible that Richard Browder was indeed a merchant, perhaps along with Richard ”Dick” S. Harwell, who became one of the witnesses to Richard’s will.  Richard S. Harwell is, in fact, known to have been a merchant.  The “Browder Store” may well have been established by Richard Harwell and, perhaps, Richard Browder bought it.  Although no evidence has been found concerning Richard’s livelihood outside of farming, it seems that Richard very well could have owned and ran such a local store.  And that area where Spring Hill Cemetery is located used to be full of stores, etc., which no longer exist today, according to early interviewees. It was almost a little town.

But I believe that Richard Browder’s store was located closer to the intersection of the Purdy-Stantonville Road and the Old Stagecoach Road, somewhat further north of Spring Hill Cemetery than these others were.  It is known that Wiley Michael, father of Mansel Michael (August 4, 1922-March 18, 1998) and Roy Michael (March 26, 1917-May 23, 1993), owned part of the old Richard Browder lands.  Turner Michael, Wiley’s father, owned an old store place there which is known to have been owned by a Browder before him.  This could certainly have been the old Browder store that people remembered.  The fact that the old store was purchased by Turner Michael would place the store as having existed at least as early as the late 1800s.  So, it was surely first owned by either Richard Browder or one of his sons.  Much of this information was related to me by my uncle, Winfred Browder, and by Mansel Michael.

In McNairy County at that time the Browder family are known to have resided at an area which was close to that originally known as Snake Hill, near what is now known as Michael Hill.  Much of this land was later sold to Jack Hockaday.  This is where I believe the Browder store may have been located.

By 1850, according to the McNairy County, Tennessee census, the Richard Browder family was listed as owning four slaves, one male and three females.  This is obviously partly still the same family mentioned earlier.  Also, by 1850, the value of Richard’s real estate (the first year such values were included in census information) was listed as $3,000.  Listed members of the Richard Browder household (the first to list individual family members) in the 1850 census of McNairy County, Tennessee (District 13) were Richard (43), Polly (47), John (29), Martha (23), Augustus (22), Louisa (21), Jackson (19), Mary (15), Franklin (12) and Frances (8).  The Louisa Browder mentioned was the wife of Augustus, Louisa Sanders-Browder (August 7, 1826-August 21, 1862).  She was the daughter of Stanford L. Sanders (son of Thomas Saunders, Sr. and Elizabeth Rook) and Martha Ann “Patsey” Lebo.  Both of the children, Isham and Ellen Browder, had already married and moved out.

I believe that the fact that Mary Ann is called “Polly” in this census is a mistake, a fault of the census taker who took liberty with her name since it is known from Levin Harris’ will that he had another daughter he called “Polly”, who married Walter King.  I know of no other instance where Mary Ann was called “Polly” in any document.  All family members are listed correctly as having been born in North Carolina.

John Browder, the eldest son, was still residing within the household of his parents in 1850, as he was not yet married by the time the census was taken.  John and his brothers, Augustus, Jackson and Franklin, are also listed in this census as being farmers, and as having attended school within the year.  The girls had not attended school.  And another Franklin Browder is listed with a different age from Franklin Browder, son of Richard.  This is likely another error made by the census taker because no other known Franklin Browder resided in McNairy County.

Since the four brothers, John, Augustus, Jackson and Franklin, were all too old to have attended an ordinary school, and certainly had already obtained some previous education, it is my belief that they all attended Purdy Male and Female Academy, although records to confirm this have not been found since all of the records of attendance from this college seem, sadly, to have been lost or destroyed.  This college was located in the town of Purdy, the closest town of any size to the Browder property other than Lawton.  And this college was also, evidently, a Cumberland Presbyterian institution, again showing an early Browder connection with this church.

Interestingly, on July 4, 1859, according to the “McNairy County,
Tennessee County Court Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866”, Judges for the elections to be held in August of that year were appointed, which included John H. Black, Richard Browder, and H A Smith for district 15 as well as another ancestor of mine, Lawson Sanders, for district 2.  I include references from this record here and elsewhere because I did not have information from it when I published my book, “Faces of Our Ancestors”.  Lawson, and several other ancestors and relatives, are also mentioned several more times in the same record throughout the years. 

The 1860 census (District 13, taken October 6) of McNairy County, Tennessee lists Richard Browder (60), farmer, along with his wife, Mary (62), and children, Martha (30), Franklin (22), farm laborer, and Frances (18) within the same household.  By 1860 the value of Richard’s real estate was listed as $5,000. and the value of his personal estate was listed as $5,629.  Also, by that year several members of the Willis family were residing within this household.  These family members were Ellen Browder-Willis (28) and her children, Jackson (10), Narcissa (8), Elizabeth (6), Coe (4), and Mary (2).  Obviously, Ellen had moved back into the home of her parents following the death of her husband, Washington “Wash” Willis.

On October 6, 1862, according to the “McNairy County, Tennessee County Court
Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866”, S T Brooks, Richard Browder and Duncan Baker were appointed commissioners to lay off one year’s allowance to the widow and family of S L Browder, deceased. 

The 1862 McNairy County Tax Records is the first available that lists Richard Browder.  He is shown as residing in District 13, and as owning 741 acres of land that year.  He is again listed in 1867, the first available at the State Archives, as owning 435 acres of land in District 13 (value $2,600.) and 138 acres of land in District 6 (value $60.).  In 1868, he is again listed in District 13 as owning 430 acres of land (value $2,600.) and in District 6 as again owning 134 acres of land (value $600.).  In 1874 the value of Richard’s land in District 13 was $2,125.  He no longer owned land in District 6.  This valuation shows that Richard Browder was affected by some sort of catastrophe, which, as the numbers read, also affected others in the area.  It may have been a flood, a bad crop year or two, or some combination of this and/or other factors.  And, according to the numbers, this situation only affected the real estate values in the area, not the personal estate values, so it almost had to have been an act of nature.

Richard Browder is again listed from 1869 to 1885, the year of his death, only in District 13 consistently, as holding 300 acres of land.  This never changed again as long as he lived.  So, it is obvious that he and Mary Ann continued to reside on their original homestead property, which they later passed down to Franklin Browder, for the remainder of their lives and never moved.  Richard, by the year 1869, had deeded all of his other lands to his other children and/or their spouses.  He sold none of it to anyone outside of the family, as far as I can tell.  And, interestingly, he is referred to as “Dick Browder” in the 1872 tax list, the only place I have ever seen him referred to in that way.

In 1886 Franklin Browder was taxed on his land holdings for the first time (District 13).  This is logical since he had not actually received any lands of his own prior to Richard’s death.  However, what is intriguing about this is the fact that Franklin suddenly had 467 acres of land, rather than only the 300 acres of land that Richard gave him through his will.  And 467 acres is only one acre less than a combination of Entry #426 and Entry #111.  So, it would appear that Franklin actually acquired all of this land in 1886.  The problem with this scenario, although it is quite logical and seems self-explanatory, is that it leaves no room for Eleanor to have received 155 acres of land (as Richard stated he had given her in his will), and it does not account for the original 300 acre tract, part of which was actually located in District 6, and which did go to Franklin Browder.

By 1870, according to the McNairy County, Tennessee census (District 13), the value of Richard Browder’s real estate had risen to $4,000., and the value of his personal estate was $1,500.  Richard (70) and Mary Ann (71), are listed as residing alone together and as having both been born in North Carolina.  So, all of their children had moved out of the original home by this time.  It is fairly consistent throughout the census’ that Mary Ann is listed as being one year older than Richard.

In this census, the Franklin Browder (26) family is listed directly under the Richard Browder family, so they were next door.  Other close neighbors that year included the John Browning (27), Frances Forsythe (32), William Lockman (43), Josiah J. Horn (23) and David Horn (55) families.

In the 1880 census of McNairy County, Richard Browder is listed as residing within the Frank Browder household, and is referred to as “Richard, Sr.”  This is the first indication that Richard’s son, Franklin, was also named Richard.  I state this even though Franklin’s son, Richard, is also listed as residing within that same household.  But, no one is actually listed as “Richard, Jr.”, even him.

Mary Ann Harris-Browder had died by this time and was buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery close to the homeplace.  Richard Browder is listed as being 80 years of age (once again consistent with other censuses).  By this time. it is clear from this census, and Richard Browder’s will, combined, that Franklin Browder had taken over the original family homestead.  The Frances Forsythe family is also listed as residing within the same household that year.  So, within ten years three previously separately listed households became one household again.  This household is listed as being close to that of the Jack (Andrew Jackson) Browder family.

It is also interesting to note that in April of 1869 Richard Browder is listed as the sole witness in the case of the State vs. James Forsythe, et. al.  The source is McNairy County,
Tennessee Circuit Court Execution Docket [McCCCED], July 1855 to March
1860, page 53.  However, in this case, as in all of the others, no reference is made as to what exactly the   outcome of said case was or which side Richard Browder was a witness for, although it stands to reason that he took the side of the defense since James Forsythe was a relative.  And, as is often the case in said records, no reference is made as to exactly what James Forsythe was being prosecuted for.  But what makes this particularly interesting is that this is the SOLE reference to Richard Browder serving as a witness in any court case, as far as I have been able to find from extant records.  And he is never mentioned as a defendant or otherwise in any case whatsoever.  So, it is clear that, as a general rule, he stayed completely out of the court system, I believe on purpose.  I believe that his complete disdain for the court system is evident here and, again, stemmed from past family squabbles that he wanted to forever leave behind.

In an article in which Luco Crabtree was interviewed for Wagon Spokes, he made the following statement:

“When the older Browders like Jim, Dan, Dick, Benjamin, Wes, John and Big Pap, divided the land it was given to the boys.  The girls got the slaves to take care of them and when the Civil War was finished the slaves were freed and the girls had nothin’.  The original Browders were brothers of my grandmother.  So far as I know, not a foot of that land is owned by a Browder now.” 

Of course, Mr. Crabtree appears to have been obtaining his information solely from Richard Browder’s will.  He probably did not have access to all of the information presented here, and would not have known that Richard had, in fact, also given land to his daughters once they married.  And he seems to have missed the fact that Richard gave his daughter, Eleanor, 155 acres of land, as he stated in his will.

Luco Crabtree’s grandmother was Mary Ann Elizabeth Browder-Willis, daughter of John Browder (known by later generations as “Big Pap”) and Delia Martin.  However, since Luco Crabtree names John Browder, obviously referring to his great-grandfather, and also names “Big Pap” separately, the obvious conclusion is that he is there referring to the patriarch of the Browder family, Richard as “Big Pap.”  Therefore, it is an obvious conclusion that, with each successive generation, for at least three generations, the oldest male Browder was referred to as “Pap” and later “Big Pap”.  And the fact that he even remembered Wes (John Wesley) Browder, son of his great-grandfather, John Browder, is telling since Wes Browder died relatively young.

Evidence also strongly suggests that the Richard Browder family participated in horse breeding and racing (just as Richard’s parents and grandparents evidently did, continuing at least that part of their legacy) since it is known that horse racing took place in that area of McNairy County, Tennessee, along what is still known as “Race Path Road”, (located close to present-day Lawton and running partly through what was Black and Browder land), but what was then referred to as Oxford Race Paths.  Today’s Race Path Road received its name because of the fact that part of the old horseracing track is now a part of the existing road.  This is also something that came out of some of my earliest interviews with family members.

In fact, there can be no real doubt that the early Browder family in McNairy County did indeed race and breed horses.  The Browder family did own horses, some of which were probably descendants of the imported English horse of Capt. Thomas Isham Browder.  And it is not very far-fetched to conclude that the Richard Browder family may, in fact, have been instrumental in the establishment of the horseracing industry in McNairy County.

Sadly, the horseracing industry in McNairy County ceased to exist during the American Civil War because the war caused the destruction of most of the industry’s records there.  The industry was never fully revived in that area after the war because of the loss of these records.  This had to be a devastating economic blow for the Browder and other families who may have been doing the same thing.  With this loss, which would have been substantial, everything changed and the Browders had to rely even more on simply farming for a living.

A note in Narcissa Black’s Diary (p. 13), dated May 4, 1861, reads “I received letter from Hattisha Sears dated April the 12, 1861.  Alponso Cross and the volunteers started to day.”  Narcissa was the wife of John H. Black, who is first listed in the 1840 census of McNairy County, Tennessee.  Alphonso Cross was a brother of Maclin Cross, who is first listed in the 1830 census of McNairy County.  This is a reference to the wife of Clement “Clemon” Sears and refers to the specter of war which was about to engulf the entire area with devastating consequences.  This Sears family resided in the midst of the Browders at that time.

Indeed, it is obvious, even from the pictures, that the Browder family of McNairy County suffered economic decline following the American Civil War.  They were no longer the genteel Southern family of former generations.  Life became much more difficult for them, as well as for other families in the South, as is seen by history.

Certainly Richard Browder, the presumed soldier from the latter years of the War of 1812 and the Seminole Wars, moved from North Carolina in an attempt to begin anew.  Richard tried to ensure that the old legacy of the Browder family would continue in that the land he passed on would remain basically intact and within the family.  Richard passed all of his land and property to his descendants, selling none of it to anyone outside of the family.  He clearly intended for this property to remain basically intact, divided only between his descendants.  He surely expected that they would be able to keep it intact.

But it was simply not possible for future generations to carry on his vision.  Even though at least three generations had managed to build and preserve a legacy and pass that legacy down to each succeeding generation, first lawsuits among family, then the American Civil War and its aftermath and, finally, the Great Depression, all took a toll on this family’s legacy.  This family of former landed gentry was, because of these events, eventually reduced greatly in social and economic status, especially during and following the Reconstruction period in the South.  For them, recovery truly did not begin again until after WWII.

Perhaps the most important part of Richard Browder’s surviving legacy is the fact that the Browder family has now resided in McNairy County, Tennessee for over 173 years, as of the time of this writing (when I first penned it back in 1014).  The Browder family name is well-known in McNairy County, as in few other places in the entire United States of America.  Even though the Browder family has not been as prominent since the family moved to McNairy County as the family had been in Virginia and North Carolina, we have certainly had a great influence there in ways that are often intangible, but no less relevant.  And that even though the family members were, and often still are, very private people.

I am certain that Richard Browder had intended not only to leave family squabbles behind in North Carolina, but also the constant conflicts that existed and evolved regularly there.  But he never counted on a conflict that would pit brother against brother and father against son and engulf the entire nation.  He never imagined that the nation he loved and had fought for himself would be at war against itself.  The former soldier who had settled down, apparently becoming a merchant and a farmer, and who wanted only peace for the rest of his life, was now faced with the specter of terrible war once again right on his doorstep!  But that part of the story is detailed more in my book “Faces
of our Ancestors”.

The children of Isham C. Browder and Flora Ann Baker were John Wesley “Wes” (Oct. 3, 1847-Jan. 20, 1873), William Franklin Polk (Feb. 1850-Nov. 7, 1922), Martha Paralee (1853-1899, Ramer, Tennessee), Thomas J. (Feb. 15, 1856-Oct. 18, 1861), Richard Duncan “Dee/Dick” (1858-p. 1889 or Nov. 1900), Robert Coleman “Bob” (May 7, 1862-Jan. 24, 1936), and Benjamin Lee “Ben” (Jul. 25, 1865-Nov. 22, 1944). 

Martha J. Browder did not marry until she was about 33 years of age.  Prior to her first marriage, she remained within the household of her parents, according to the 1850 and 1860 census’.  However, some believe that William Riley Wardlow may have been her illegitimate son by John Wardlow (b. c1828, Rockingham County, North Carolina/listed in the 1840 census of McNairy County).  According to Wardlow family tradition, William was the son of John Wardlow and a “Forsythe woman.”  My research concurs with this. 

Martha married three times.  She first married (1) David Redding (Feb. 18, 1809, North Carolina-Aug. 31, 1874) on Nov. 24, 1861 in McNairy County, Tennessee.  The lodge return from Gravel Hill Lodge states that he died on Sept. 1, 1874.  Martha was David Redding’s second wife, as he had previously married Mary E. Horn (Feb. 20, 1818-Jan. 8, 1857) in 1850.  Both David and Mary E. Horn-Redding are buried in Prospect Cemetery, McNairy County, Tennessee next to one another.  So, David elected to be buried next to his first wife. 

Martha J. Browder-Redding later married (2) James Forsythe (Jun. 10, 1808-Mar. 7, 1884), son of Isham G. Forsythe (d. 1858 according to the “McNairy County,
Tennessee County Court Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866”), on Apr. 20, 1875 in McNairy County, after James’ first wife, Sarah, died. 

Following the death of James Forsythe, on Mar. 7, 1884, Martha J. Browder married (3) George Perry on Aug. 2, 1884 in McNairy County.  Others state that their marriage year was 1885, but the estate settlement for James Forsythe, deceased (Aug. 21, 1884) shows that Martha “Parry” was already married to George.  She was also Administratrix of James’ estate. 

No children were produced from either of these three marriages.  I believe that Martha is buried in Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery, but she has no marker. 

S. (probably “Sylvester”) Augustus Browder married (1) Eliza “Liza/Louisa” J. Saunders (Aug. 7, 1826-Aug. 21, 1862) about 1849.  Eliza was born in Tennessee, according to the censuses. 

Following Louisa’s death, Augustus Browder married (2) Charlotte Ann Barlow (Dec. 31, 1842, Orange County, North Carolina-Jun. 13, 1903) about 1867.  Charlotte was the daughter of Joseph Jerome Barlow (Oct. 20, 1802-Feb. 7, 1882 [a Presbyterian minister]) and Lucinda Crossett (Nov. 11, 1819-Sept. 22, 1877/m. Dec. 12, 1838 [d/o James Crossett and Charlotte Johnston]), and granddaughter of Thomas Barlow (d. Mar. 7, 1836, Orange County, NC) and Lucretia “Lucy” Hargus (1780-Aug. 20, 1862 [buried in Prospect Cemetery]/m. Dec. 26, 1796, Orange County, NC). 

On April 2, 1860, John Rains, Jr. was appointed overseer of the Purdy and Corinth Road, with hands, Dake Ray, James Ray, A Browder, and others, according to the “McNairy County, Tennessee County Court Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866.”  This was likely Augustus Browder since, within the same document, Andrew Browder seems to have always been referred to as “A. J. Browder”.

Augustus was buried in Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery next to his first wife, Eliza/Louisa.  So, he was obviously buried with her and his family members who had already died, although there is no headstone to mark his grave.  Charlotte Barlow-Browder died some years later than he, in 1903, and is buried at Crump, Hardin County, Tennessee in Crump Cemetery, plot #471.

The children of Gus and Eliza J. Browder were Sarah Elizabeth (Dec. 11, 1850-Jan. 11, 1925), Richard Sanders (c1853-Oct. 4, 1912), Hezekiah Penn (c1855-Dec. 20, 1861), Amy S. M. (Dec. 20, 1856-Dec. 16, 1861), Benjamin Franklin (May 1859-Dec. 20, 1861), and Eliza/Louisa Jane (Aug. 13, 1862-Jan. 25, 1942). 

The children of Gus and Charlotte Browder were Molly L. (c1868-c1885), John T. (b. June 1871), Robert Harrison (April 1871/73-Dec. 3, 1950), Cora “Corie” Alice (Aug. 21, 1874-Mar. 2, 1949), Laura “Lena” L. (c1878-c1935), Elmer L., and William Sylvester (Jul. 22, 1883-Oct. 15, 1968). 

Eleanor “Ella” Jane Browder married George Washington “Wash” Willis, son of Edward Willis, about 1847/48.  Wash Willis was born in 1821 in Tennessee and died about 1856 in McNairy County, Tennessee, at only about 37 years of age.  He was possibly the very first person buried at what would become Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery. 

Ella never remarried after Wash died and, therefore, remained single for a number of years until her own death, still residing within the household of her parents for much of that time.  Ella’s burial place is probably next to her deceased husband in Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery.  Although they do not have any markers, there is little doubt that they both are buried there.

The children of Wash and Ella Willis were Jackson “Jack” Monroe (Oct. 8, 1848-Jun. 11, 1930), Narcissa “Sis” Jane (b. c1849), Elizabeth (b. c1851), Levin Karl Coe (Nov. 26, 1952-Oct. 29, 1942) and Mary (b. c1855).  Please note the name “Levin” here, taken from a later census document.  This helps prove descent from Levin Harris.

Andrew Jackson “Jack” Browder, Sr. married Phoebe Caroline Helbert, daughter of John and Bonita Helbert, about 1854.  Caroline was born in February of 1836 in Rockingham County, North Carolina. 

A. J. Browder is named in the “McNairy County, Tennessee County Court Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866” as a member of a work crew, selected on October 4, 1858, to help repair the Simpson Ferry Road. 

Jack Browder, Sr. died about March of 1897 and is buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery, later known as the Frank Browder Cemetery.  Caroline died on Oct. 9, 1925 of kidney trouble and dysentery, and is buried in Carter Cemetery, McNairy County, Tennessee. 

The children of Jack and Caroline Browder were James “Jim” Franklin (Aug. 28, 1855-Nov. 11, 1931), John R. (c1856-before 1880), Milton “Milt/Mint” Lafayette (Sept. 21, 1859-Sept. 25, 1940), Robert “Bob” N. (c1860-c1893), Thomas “Tom” Calvin (Jan. 5, 1862-Jun. 21, 1946), Mary Nitha “Nith” (Jan. 11, 1865-Jul. 15, 1948), Frances “Fannie/Fan” (Jan. 1, 1864-Dec. 26, 1935), Andrew Jackson “Jack”, Jr. (June 15, 1870-Jan. 29, 1960), William “Bill” Forrest (Jul. 1872-Mar. 8, 1908), and George Edgar (Mar. 8, 1875-Aug. 27, 1956). 

On Apr. 5, 1858, by the way, according to “McNairy County, Tennessee County
Court Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866”, John Helbert was among those paid for having been selected for a grand jury, but the details are not available in this record. 

Mary Ann Browder married Benjamin Franklin “Frank” Sanders (Oct. 1830-c1910), son of Stanford L. Saunders and Martha Ann “Patsey” Lebo, and grandson of Thomas Sanders, Sr. and Elizabeth Rook, about 1854. 

According to the “McNairy County, Tennessee County Court
Minutes Abstracts, Jan 1858-Feb 1866”, B F Sanders was appointed administrator of the estate of W P Sanders, with bond set at $2,200 and with Lindsey Sanders and Augustus Browder as security, on September 5, 1859. 

I believe that Benjamin F. and Mary A. Sanders are buried at Spring Hill (Old Browder) Cemetery, as there are graves marked off by bricks other than those for John and Cordelia Browder, close to the Sanders children who are buried there.  Benjamin Sanders might possibly have purchased the land where the cemetery was located from Eleanor Browder-Willis, either in the late 1850s or early 1860s.  Sadly, the record for this is simply not extant. 

The children of Benjamin F. and Mary A. Sanders were Annie M. (Mar. 9, 1855-Mar. 1, 1891), Marcus “Marc” Lafayette (Nov. 20, 1856-Feb. 5, 1937), Thomas Richard “Tommy” (Sept. 1857-1915), Frances Anna “Hattie” (Feb. 1860-1901), Martha Paralee (Sept. 16, 1861-Sept. 7, 1863), Mary E. (Dec. 7, 1863-Sept. 26, 1865), Benjamin Lee (Mar. 15, 1866-Apr. 4, 1942), Benjamin Robert Lee (Mar. 15, 1866-Apr. 4, 1942), Caldonie “Dona/Dorcas” (c1868-c1899), Stanford D. F. (Aug. 27, 1870-Jul. 22, 1876), William A. (b. Mar. 9, 1875), Mary Ann “Missouri” (Mar. 9, 1875-Mar. 1, 1881), and Josie “Josia” Idella (Jun. 13, 1879-Sept. 21, 1965). 

Richard Franklin “Frank” Browder married Julia Ann “Beam/Bessie” Helbert, daughter of John H. and Bonita Helbert, on Jan. 4, 1866 in McNairy County, Tennessee.  Beam was born in May of 1838 in Rockingham County, North Carolina.

Franklin Browder died in McNairy County, Tennessee sometime after Nov. 7, 1896.  He would have died in late 1896 or early 1897 since his estate began to be taxed in 1897.  Beam Browder died on Apr. 19, 1910 at her home in McNairy County, Tennessee (McNairy County Death Index,
1908-1912/Death Certificate).  The certificate also states that Beam was “widowed”, proving that Frank had already passed away.  It also states that she was buried on the 20th.  Both Frank and Beam Browder are buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery in McNairy County, Tennessee, close to their home place. 

The known children of Frank and Beam Browder were Mary Bonita “Benitha” (Feb., 18, 1867-May 6, 1943), the twins Richard C. “Dick” (Mar. 24, 1868-Mar. 31, 1950) and John (Mar. 24, 1868-c1871), Laura “Ura” S. (1873-Dec. 20, 1962), unknown daughter (c1872-c1878), unknown daughter (c1873-c1878), James “Jim” Robert (Jul. 5, 1875-Oct. 8, 1949), and Daniel “Dan/Davey” M. (April 9, 1879-Nov. 16, 1951).  John is known to have died as an infant.  The two unknown daughters also died as young girls.  All children were born in McNairy County, Tennessee. 

All of the early Browder men died by or before 1900, as did all of the women, with two exceptions.  Old Richard Browder himself died in about October of 1885.  Gus died in Dec. of 1887.  Martha died in 1892. John died in July or August of 1893.  Isham died Jan. 25, 1896.  Frank died in 1896/97.  A. J. died in March of 1897.  And Frances died in 1897. So, within a span of about 15 years, all of the early Browder men and women, save two, had died.  The only members of the family to live past 1900 were Beam Browder, widow of Frank Browder, and her sister, Phoebe C. Browder, widow of A. J. Browder as well as Mary Ann Browder-Sanders, who died in 1901.  Beam was also ultimately the one best remembered by later generations, as I found so often in my early research.  People would still talk of Beam Browder even when they did not recall or even know anything about any of the other early Browders.  One could say that, if Richard had not passed his estate, for the most part and, therefore, his legacy, on to his youngest son, Franklin, then the Browder family might not have been remembered at all.  What little was remembered by later generations was mainly remembered because these later generations remembered Frank and Beam Browder.  The rest had to be revealed once again by research.

John Browder and Isham C. Browder, the eldest sons of Richard Browder, served as Executors of Richard’s estate.  Richard “Dick” S. Harwell and Asa Bell proved his will in open court, serving as witnesses to it.

The Last Will and Testament of RICHARD BROWDER, Deceased (McCWB 1, p. 51):

                In the name of God amen, I RICHARD BROWDER being of sound mind and heart but feeling the certainty that soon I must be overtaken by death, do make & publish this my last will & testament hereby revoking & making void any other will by me at any time heretofore made.

First        I desire that any just debt I may be owing at my death shall be paid by my executor out of the first monies that may come into his hands.

Second   I give and bequeath to my son JOHN BROWDER the tract of land on which he resides, it having been surveyed, & my intention is for him to have it with all the calls & corners …. & bounds as surveyed let it contain whatever number of acres it may.

3rd          I give to my son ISHAM BROWDER the tract of land on which he lives.  Supposed to contain one hundred & fifty acres more or less.  My object is for him to have it as it was surveyed with all the calls & corners as it was originally laid off.

4th          I have given to my daughter MARTHA REDING, formerly MARTHA BROWDER, a negro girl, Jenny which she recd. in possession.  I also gave her a cow & calf & bed & furniture all of which she received & I now desire that she has nothing more of my estate except what has been given to her.

5th          I give to my son AUGUSTUS BROWDER one hundred acres of land on which he resides, also one other tract called the Clem Sears tract.  I desire that he has them as surveyed and described in the deeds made to him.

6th          I have given to my daughter ELENOR WILLIS formerly ELENOR BROWDER, one hundred & fifty five acres of land which she sold to BENJ. SANDERS.  I also give her the usual amount required to fit her up for house keeping as I did my other children and I now desire that what I have given to her be her interest in my estate & that she receive nothing more out of my estate.

7th          I give my son A. J. BROWDER the home on which he lives containing one hundred & sixty acres as it is surveyed with all the calls & corners.  I also had given him a mare & outfit for house keeping which I desire to be his interest in my estate at my death.

8th          On account the faithfulness & kindness of my youngest son FRANK BROWDER his willingness to take care of & support myself & his mother in our present old age, I give to him the homestead on which I now live containing 300 acres more or less & this gift to him is conditioned as follows that during the lifetime of myself and his mother he is to support & take care of us & at our death he is to have the home tract of land on which we now live & half of the stock on the farm is now his & to continue so.

9th          I have given to my daughter MARY SANDERS one negro girl named Susan which she received into her possession also gave her the usual outfit for house keeping  & sold to her husband BENJ. SANDERS a tract of land containing 100 acres at a reduced price & in part price of this land I gave him three hundred dollars and afterward there was a dispute arose about the lines & title to make him whole I gave him in money a amount to make him whole and satisfied and further things done & received by him & her I desire that they have nothing more of my estate at my death.

10th        I have given my daughter FRANCIS FORSYTHE a negro girl named Margret which she received and controlled.  I also gave her the usual amount I gave my other children to fit them up for house keeping & in consideration for what I have done for her & what she has received I desire that she is to have nothing more out of my estate at my death except she remains a widow unto my death then I desire that her & her 2 sons has my interest in the stock on hand.  If she marries & goes away & her 2 sons remains until they are 21 years of age I require my son FRANK BROWDER to give to each of them a years schooling at a good neighborhood school.

11th        Having fully faith & confidence in the honesty of my 2 sons ISHAM BROWDER & JOHN BROWDER I hereby nominate & appoint them my executors & desire that they carry out my will as they know I desire it to be done, also I desire them to counsel my friends as to the proper & legal course for them to pursue in winding up & settling my business generally.  In testimony whereof I have hereunto set my hand and seal in my usual good health & in the presence of the subscribing witnesses who attest the same at my request.

Witness                                                                  April 5, 1871        Richard Browder (seal)

Asa Bell

R. S. Harwell

Calvin Shull

                This day the within last will and testament of RICHARD BROWDER was presented in open court and proven and set up by R. S. Harwell and Asa Bell, two witnesses in common form of law.

                This 2nd day of November 1885.                                               J. R. Adams Clk.

It is interesting to note that the Administration Bond lists several family members, not only Richard’s administrators, which was common practice (McNairy
County Clerk Bonds & Letters, Administrators [McCCB&LA] B, p. 266).  Those family members listed on said bond were John Browder, Isham Browder, A. J. Browder, and Frances Browder.  This bond was made on November 2, 1885.  It does confirm that John and Isham were Richard’s Executors.  Unfortunately, everything that County Court Clerk, J. R. Adams did, whether it be land deeds or any other legal document, he would sign a person’s name for them rather than having them sign said document themselves, having them only make an “x” mark instead.  So, we don’t have everyone’s signatures, as we would like to have here!  But, the really wonderful thing here is proof of Frances Browder’s first initial.  He wrote her name as “T. F.” Browder (not Forsythe, for whatever reason).  And, in fact, she didn’t even make a mark, for whatever reason.

Richard Browder revealed his true personality in the writing of this will better than, perhaps, he could have in any other way.  This will really has nothing to do with slavery, although it may seem to at first glance.  Richard made certain statements, and brought out certain details, in his will because he wanted to ensure that his will would be followed absolutely.  He stated that he had already given certain properties to certain people, and that this was all he wanted them to have, period.  What he was stating to everyone was that there would be absolutely no family squabbles.  No one would fight or sue over property, and his Executors, John and Isham, were to absolutely enforce this.  After what his father had been through, one can fully understand why he was so absolute in his will.  And, as far as I have been able to determine, his will was indeed followed absolutely!

However, sadly, it is also true that, although his will was followed to the letter, the family world that he had created would soon disappear.  Within two generations, all of the property that he had acquired and passed down to his children would no longer be in the Browder family at all. In fact, one set of siblings was known to have taken their inheritance, sold the land, and gone on a train trip across the country and back. When they returned, they had nothing.

Richard’s will also exemplifies his personality in another way.  It is obvious that, rather than allow himself to potentially be sued by B. F. Sanders over a disagreement, Richard chose to do whatever he could to make it right with Sanders.  Sadly, it does not appear that all of his descendants, including some of his own sons, had the same knack for staying out of lawsuits as Richard did, even after they observed his example.  In all of the records I have seen, I have noted various people, including Browders, suing and being sued.  But never once in all of these records have I seen a mention that Richard Browder was sued for anything at all.  Richard completely held his property, and life, together without resorting to lawsuits, and without borrowing any money from anyone, thus owing no one else.  And this is obviously the way he was determined to live his life after seeing the very sad example of his own relatives suing and fighting over property back in North Carolina.  I view this as exemplary.

Also, it seems interesting to note that, among these early Browders it seems that it was Isham who ultimately fared the best, with the possible exception of Franklin, as he had more property still available to pass on to later generations at his death.  And it seems that Isham’s children were doing quite well on their own.  Franklin deeded most of his property to the Hockaday family, who still own much of it today.  The remainder was divided among Frank’s children, and then his son, Richard, purchased each of his sibling’s shares so that he ultimately got it all, with the exception of that which had been already deeded to the Hockaday family.

It appears that John kept his land basically intact and, perhaps, also inherited what little property old Walter Martin (his father-in-law) had.  But, ultimately, all of this together was still not very much.  T. R. Sanders and John Wesley Browder divided John’s land after his death, they, having bought out my ancestor, Lee Roy Browder.  Gus lost the property Richard gave him quite early.  Jack also appears to have kept his inheritance basically intact, except that he also appears to have eventually sold most of it to the Hockaday family, as well as others.

Anyway, Levin Harris owned land which was located on Wolf Island Creek, so he was a neighbor to the Richard Browder family.  Levin, as earlier stated, in his will, also mentioned a son-in-law, Walter King, who was married to his daughter, “Polly.”  Levin also mentions that his property was bounded on the south by the land of John Harris, Sr. (apparently still intact at that time).  Executors were his sons John and Clement as well as his wife, Elizabeth.  Witnesses were R. T. Williamson, John Matlock and Calvin G. Smith.  It should be noted that Levin wrote his surname as “Harriss” throughout his will (Webster, p. 39).

Obviously, Elizabeth, Levin’s widow, continued to reside on the property purchased and later sold by Richard Browder until she died.  There was no reason for her to move away as long as she was allowed to continue residing on said property.  And there was no reason for her to stay behind either, while most of her children went on to Carroll County, Tennessee, unless, at least, some remained behind to ensure that she was cared for.  She certainly would not have remained behind totally alone!  And who is it who remained behind?  Mary Ann Harris-Browder. 

So, here I will add a little more information to the mix.  In addition to those mentioned in Levin’s will, other family members are mentioned in the 1843 land deed.  The family members listed in said deed are Ira Harris, Litha Harris, Ella Harris and Laura Harris.  Now, it seems obvious that Litha Harris is one and the same as Elley “Lethy” Harris, and that Ella Harris is one and the same as Elizabeth Harris.  And, certainly, both mentions of Ira Harris are of the same person.  But who is Laura Harris?  I simply do not know.  And the other brothers were not mentioned, perhaps, because they had already moved away. 

Regardless, it also seems obvious that neither Litha, Ella, nor Laura, were married at the time of the writing of said will, unless Laura was a widow of one of the Harris brothers mentioned in Levin’s will.  It is my belief, although I will not claim proof here, that the next year, in 1844, Elizabeth Harris, daughter of Levin and Elizabeth Harris, married Lewis Going.  There is a record of Lewis Going marrying Elizabeth Going on October 24, 1844.  The bondsman was Johnson Going, and the witness was E. W. Hancock.  I simply believe that the record contains an error, and that the writer meant to list Elizabeth as Elizabeth Harris.  Regardless, this couple did have at least one son, Lewis Going, Jr, who was born in 1844.
