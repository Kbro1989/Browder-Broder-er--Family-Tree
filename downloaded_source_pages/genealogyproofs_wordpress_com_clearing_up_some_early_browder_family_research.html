Clearing Up Some Early Browder Family Research:

Posted byjmarkbrowderDecember 20, 2023January 2, 2025Posted inUncategorized

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (Dec. 19, 2023, updated Jan. 1, 2025)

First of all, let’s be clear about one thing. The earliest researchers into the Browder family line were direct descendants of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Richard was born about 1719 and died about 1807. His birth is not actually recorded anywhere, but we can ascertain it based on his connections to his siblings as well as one record which is suggestive, but not to be considered as proof. That record is the following:

Aug. 11, 1719 – JOHN BROWDER along with several others, was summoned to answer a petition and complaint of William Davis, Overseer of Highways, for their contempt in refusing to assist in clearing the highway, and these persons (except JOHN BROWDER), failing to show just cause, it was ordered that each of them, except BROWDER, pay Davis 5 shillings. Present: Maj. Robert Munford (Va. Gen. Soc. Qtly., Nov. 1986, p. 58).

The timing of this alleged incident is interesting to me as it seems to have been around the time of Elizabeth’s pregnancy with their son, Richard Browder, and this birth may have had something to do with John’s refusal to appear in this situation, as he probably needed to be home. Obviously, John Browder had already made his reasoning clear, and it was accepted, whatever it was.

The earliest researchers into the family didn’t know the name of Richard’s father anymore. It had already been lost to time by the time research was being conducted. This fact is first demonstrated by a passage in the addendum to the booklet, Genealogy of David Browder, of Virginia and His Descendants, in which David P. Browder refers to research he had recently obtained through his nephew, the Rev. Robert W. Browder, which Robert Browder, in turn, had obtained from his father, the Rev. George R. Browder, who had, in turn, obtained the information from his father, Robert Browder (b. 1804), son of David Browder and Mary Cousins. In the addendum to this book by Thomas E. Browder, David states this fact in the following manner:

“One —– Browder, an Englishman, married a Welsh woman, and emigrated to America early in the 18th century, and settled in Virginia, where he lived and died. His name has not been retained in the family. The children of these progenitors of the Browder family were Richard, Joseph, Arthur, George and Nancy as far as known. . . .

Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Thompson Browder, married a cousin Isham Browder. Their children were Mary and Richard so far as is known (Browder, Thomas, pp. 37-38).”

David P. Browder also reiterated this fact in a letter written on Nov. 27, 1901. Even so, no one had any direct proof of the union of Isham Browder and Martha Browder until I uncovered it from court records.

Now, there are some errors here, but the information did lead later researchers in the right direction. The “Englishman” in question, we now know, was John Browder who married Elizabeth. I’m not going to get into what her maiden name was here because it is not necessary to the narrative. Suffice it to be said that she is here referred to as a “Welsh woman”. The text also states that the “Englishman” emigrated to Virginia. However, we now know that he was actually born here.

The rest of the book deals with the earliest known descendants of some of the children of Richard and Mary Browder. None of them were named Isham, By the way.

We actually know at least the names of three sons of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, including John, because of one particular land deed. On July 20, 1736, John Browder was granted 300 acres of land, located on Gravelly Run, by King George II. According to the document, this land joined that which he had previously been granted. It also bordered on the land of his brothers (as stated in the document) George Andrew Browder and Edmund W. Browder (Prince George County Land Grant Records Book [PGCLGRB] 17, p. 125).

So, we know that Edmund and Elizabeth Browder had at least three sons, John, George Andrew, and Edmund W. If they had any daughters who lived to maturity, it is not known.

Four of the children of John and Elizabeth Browder are known because their births are listed in the Bristol Parish Register. These are William, George, Anne, and Joseph. They are the youngest four, the others having been born before the extant Registry was started. The ones born prior to the start of said Registry were Arthur, Edmund, John, and Richard. So, we have exact birth dates for the last four, but have to estimate the others. Notably, none of them are named Isham either. But the two to be focused on here were the brothers, Arthur (b. 1715) and Richard (b. c1719).

Again, those who did the earliest research into the Browder family were descended from Richard Browder. The open question was who the parents of Isham Browder were. They did not try to answer that in the booklet. They only stated that he was a cousin of Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder. And, in fact, he was her cousin as court records demonstrate (we will get to them), but early researchers were looking at the wrong Isham Browder as the cousin of Martha Browder. They THOUGHT that he was the Rev. War soldier who had died in Kentucky in 1830. But they were wrong. Since that time, others have attached the Rev. War soldier to Arthur Browder as Arthur’s son. They are also mistaken.

Now, Arthur Browder did indeed have a son. But that son is not named in any extant record. There is only one record that demonstrates that Arthur had a son. From the Bristol Parish Vestry Book, the notation in question is presented here, verbatim:

“Concerning the Returns of Processioning for the Year 1739 (pp. 98-99) the notation reads as follows:

“Miles Thweatt and William Batt[e] the Same (Processioned all the Lands in their Precinct) excepting two lines of Batte & Jordan they disagreeing & another piece belonging to the HEIR of ARTHER BROWDER the HEIR being an Infant.”

This short notation demonstrates that (1) Arthur Browder was already deceased and that (2) he had only one heir, who was an “infant” in 1739. Unlike today, a person was considered an “infant” until the age of 21, at which time one could inherit land and perform other legal actions. So, the term was used differently back then than it is today. It did not mean he had to still be a baby. Sadly, the notation neither names the child’s mother, nor the child. But there can be no doubt that the members of the Vestry knew exactly who the child was and who his mother was.

Regardless, the same sources from whom these earliest researchers obtained their information also stated that Richard Browder had taken in the son of his deceased brother, Arthur, and had brought him up within his own household. And those earliest sources named him Isham. So, it is actually clear that Isham Browder was THE son of Arthur Browder and that he was brought up by Arthur’s brother, Richard after Arthur died. What happened to the child’s mother is never told.

Now, anyone can see that Isham Browder had to have been born prior to 1739. We know that this Isham Browder first married Agnes Tatum in Surry County, Virginia. We also know that they were married about 1758 because they had a son, Isham Browder, Jr., who was born on March 17, 1759. He was christened on June 3, 1759, in Albemarle Parish, Sussex County, Virginia (Albemarle Parish Register [Colonial Dames] 44). Isham, Jr.’s godparents were Nathaniel Beddingfield, William Weathers and Phoebe Pettway, all named in said document.

PLEASE NOTE that this Isham Browder (1) did not marry anyone named Martha and (2) that he had a son named Isham by his wife, Agnes Tatum. That son was born in 1759. There is no way that Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Rev. War could be descended from this Isham Browder, period. So, the Rev. War Isham Browder is not descended from Arthur Browder and is not descended from his son, Isham, either.

If that isn’t proof enough, then here’s more.

There is no further record of either Agnes Tatum-Browder or her son, Isham Browder, Jr. Agnes isn’t even mentioned in the will of her father, Christopher Tatum, who died in 1769. Isham Browder, Jr. isn’t either. Whatever tragedy happened, these two were clearly deceased by 1769. In fact, they were deceased earlier than that because Isham Browder, Sr. married again on Feb. 3, 1767 to Talitha Cox in Lunenburg County, Virginia. Documentation states that Talitha Cox was of Mecklenburg County, Virginia (Mecklenburg County Will Book and Marriage Records 3, p. 125), but she was actually born in Brunswick County – the part later made into Mecklenburg County. Isham and Talitha were married within Cumberland Parish, Lunenburg County, Virginia. The documentation also states that Isham Browder was of Halifax County, Virginia. So, that is where he resided at that time. 

PLEASE NOTE that Halifax County, Virginia absorbed parts of Surry and Isle of Wight Counties in 1732. It has become clear that Isham Browder, Sr. resided in Halifax County, but, for whatever reason, attended church within Albemarle Parish which was partly in Surry County. Perhaps that part of Surry County was absorbed by Halifax County in 1732. The lines were rather fluid during the earliest years in any case.

There is more than ample evidence and proof that Isham Browder, Sr. and Talitha Cox had another son named Isham. I am certainly not going to present all of it here. But here is the basic framework.

Isham and Talitha Browder are found in Rowan County, North Carolina within a year of their marriage. So, they had moved from Virginia to North Carolina, settling on Wolf Island Creek. That part of Rowan County was formed into Guilford County within a few years. Isham Browder, Sr. died in Guilford County in 1784, according to ample documentation. After his death, that part of Guilford County was made into Rockingham County. Talitha Browder died in Rockingham County in 1828.

Now, their son, Isham, was born about 1774. This is proven by census records. Said records also show that his wife’s name was Martha. They also show that she was also born about 1774.

By 1810, according to the Rockingham County, NC census of that year, Isham Browder and Martha Browder had two sons, as well as one daughter. Their sons are listed as being between the ages of 10-15 and under 10, respectively. The daughter is listed in the next to youngest age range (10-15). We know that one son, the older one, was Richard and the daughter was Mary, but the name of the other son is a mystery. He is never again directly referenced in any known document except the 1820 census. He obviously reached adulthood, but appears to have soon died.

This census shows that the Isham Browder family resided close to his mother, Talitha Browder, and close to the Augustine Claiborne Browder family (his deceased brother’s family).

However, backing up just a  bit, there is a document that establishes their relationship.

Isham Browder and Martha Browder, his wife, gave Power of Attorney to Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia to sell Martha’s interest in about 200 acres of land, located on the Gravelly Run (RCDB N, p. 339). While all of this is not directly specified, Richard Browder, Martha’s father, was not a large land owner and probably only owned about 200 acres at the time of his death. So, it is certain that he did not bequeath it all to Martha, but bequeathed each of his living children a certain percentage of divided interest in said land. But sadly, we don’t have a Will to consult.

NOTE: It is likely that the Richard Browder mentioned in the Power of Attorney was the son of Martha’s brother, David Browder and his wife, Mary Cousins, since Martha’s father had already died about 1807, and that the land in question must have been inherited from her father, Richard Browder.

It should be understood that there is no reason for Martha to have been named in such a document other than the fact that she had inherited a percentage in said land, and that she was actually the one giving Power of Attorney to sell land that she had inherited. Otherwise, it would have only been necessary for Isham to have been named.

So, this document shows a direct relationship between Richard Browder and his daughter, Martha, and shows that she married her cousin, Isham Browder. It clearly shows a connection between Martha Browder and Dinwiddie County, Virginia. The fact that Power of Attorney was given to a member of the Browder family also shows conclusively that Martha was a member of the Browder family. Otherwise, why would she not have elected a member of some other family? This is the only known document that conclusively names Martha Browder as the wife of Isham Browder, although the division of Jones Browder’s property (we will get to that) also indicates it directly.

From RCDB N, p. 339 (from a copy by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

“Browder, Richard from BROWDER, ISHAM & WIFE, Power of Attorney, 200 A of Land: Know all men by these presents that we ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER his WIFE, both of the county of Rockingham and state of N. C., have for good cause to us moving, have nominated, authorized, ordained and appointed, and do by these presents authorize, ordain and appoint our friend Richard Browder of the county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth of Va., to act, make over convey and confirm a certain piece or parcel of land situate, lying and being in the said county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth aforesaid and the waters of the Gravelly Run, and contains about 200 acres of land: And we, the said ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves and heirs, creditors and administrators do make or convey and confirm unto the said Richard Browder full power absolute authority to sell and dispose, make over, convey and confirm at the will and pleasure, a sure and good right and title to the aforesaid land and premises as above described, to whomsoever he may think proper, as full and ample in every respect, as we could do were we personally present, to all which matter and things – we the said ISHAM BROWDER and the said MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves our heirs creditors and Administrators firmly by these presents, sealed with our seals and dated this day of ___ in the year 1808. Signed, sealed and acknowledged in the presence of ISHAM BROWDER (seal) Augustine C. Browder MARTHA BROWDER (seal) Thos. Winston Joseph Mclain St. of N. C. Rockingham Co. May Session 1810: The written power of attorney was duly proved in open court by the oath of Thomas Winston & on motion ordered to be recorded. R. Galloway, C.”

OK, so in late 1799 or very early 1800, Isham Browder, son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox, and Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder, were married, probably in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Court records also prove this union. There is no known marriage certificate, so no other details are known. 

And, so, on to the court documents. It is only necessary to present one document since they are all very repetitive. These court documents have to do with the estate of Jones Browder.

Now, for clarification, Jones Browder (c1764-1826) is known by documentation to have been the son of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786)who had other sons also – Samuel, Frederick, and Harrison. These last three sons were killed during the Rev. War and, so, Jones, the youngest (too young to serve during the war) inherited their legacies. After Jones Browder died, family members sued for portions of his property since he had no legal descendants for it to go to. The court determined that shares of this property would be divided between the FIRST COUSINS OF JONES BROWDER ON THE PATERNAL SIDE OF HIS FAMILY. Only a few others were allowed to take part, the other parties agreeing to this.

PLEASE NOTE that the Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) who was the father of Jones Browder was NOT the son of John Browder and Elizabeth. He was a different Arthur Browder. In fact, because of the stipulation that the estate be divided among the first cousins of Jones Browder, it becomes clear that this Arthur Browder was a son of Richard Browder and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. The reason we can conclude that this is true is because every legatee was (1) a descendant of John and Elizabeth Browder, but (2) only the ones that were allowed to join by permission were NOT descendants of Richard Browder and Mary. In other words, the actual legatees were ALL descendants of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia except those allowed by the court to also participate. Since Jones Browder was a grandson, therefore, of Richard and Mary Browder, all of the legatees were also grandchildren of Richard and Mary Browder except those allowed by permission to take part.

PLEASE ALSO NOTE that Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) did NOT have a son named Isham. All of his sons are known. They were Frederick, Harrison, Samuel, and Jones. There are two reason se KNOW that he had no other sons, least of all one named Isham. (1) Jones Browder inherited everything, period. He obtained the estate of his father and the Rev. War pensions of his deceased brothers as the only heir.

Sept. 6, 1777 – The three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel Browder, sons of Arthur Browder (b. c1742), enlisted together into the Continental Army in Capt. Peter Jones’ Company of Continental Regulars, 14th Virginia Regiment, commanded by Col. Charles Lewis. They are known to have been at Valley Forge during the bitter winter of 1777. They are also said (perhaps incorrectly) to have been at White Plains, and West Point, but obviously not at any battles at these places because they took place prior to their enlistments.

Winter 1777-1778 – During the bitter winter Continental soldiers massed at Valley Forge. Pvt Isham Browder was among them. This is when the Continental Army was finally made into an effective fighting force by the efforts of Gen. George Washington and Gen. Baron von Steuben. Again, the three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel, were also there. Interestingly, so was John Marshall (grandson of Rev. James Keith and Mary Isham Randolph), who would later become Chief Justice of the US Supreme Court. Whether they all were acquainted with each other or not is unknown, but it seems possible.

Feb. 28, 1778 – Frederick Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at Lancaster, Pennsylvania.

1778 – Samuel Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at Lancaster Pennsylvania.

June 28, 1778 – The Battle of Monmouth Courthouse takes place. Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830) is wounded in the arm during this battle. This battle was a stalemate between the forces. Several mutinies took place afterward, including that of Maj. Gen. Benedict Arnold.

Nov. 26, 1778 – Harrison Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at York, Pennsylvania.

June 15, 1786 – Jones Browder, son of Arthur Browder and Rebecca Jones (the other three brothers may have had a different mother), who was too young to enlist in the Revolutionary War along with his brothers, inherits his deceased brother’s Revolutionary War service monies as well as 300 acres of land from their war bounty. John Lewis and Littleberry Browder appeared earlier on April 19th before James Greenway and Edward Pegram, Jr, Justices in Dinwiddie County, Virginia, stating that Jones Browder was the only surviving heir. The document not only shows that the three brothers were deceased, but that their father, Arthur, was also.

NOTE: Both Arthur Browder and Littleberry Browder were brothers.

Oct. 2, 1826 – Jones Browder, son of Arthur Browder and Rebecca Jones, and grandson of Richard Browder and Mary, dies at Blanford in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. At his death he was one of the few Browders to still own land at Gravelly Run in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. It totaled just over 345 acres. He also owned two slaves and six horses. But he had no descendants to leave his property to.

1832 – Some family members initiated a lawsuit over the property of Jones Browder since he left no will and had no descendants.

Apr. 2, 1834 – From the document dated April 2, 1834 (Dinwiddie County, Virginia Chancery Order Book 1, 1832-1852, pp. 46-47), concerning the division of Jones Browder’s estate, the following is copied verbatim (but structurally modified for readability, also adding information in brackets as a help to identify persons named):

“Mary Pentecost, formerly [Mary Thompson] Browder [daughter of Richard and Mary Browder], John W. P. Pool [Pettypool] and Mary [actually, Nancy A. Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder] his wife, formerly Williams, Nancy Fraser [daughter of Susanna Browder and John Thacker], formerly Thacker, Joseph Lewis and Elizabeth [daughter of Susanna Browder and George Timmons] his wife, formerly Timmons, George Timmons admr. of Susanna Timmons [daughter of Richard and Mary Browder] decd. and Martha Timmons, heirs of Susanna Timmons decd., formerly Browder, Elizabeth Pulliam, Tabitha Browder, Jane Browder, Benjamin Browder, Venus [actually Vines] Browder, Jesse Browder, and Jonathan Browder, heirs of Thompson Browder [son of Richard and Mary Browder] decd. John P. Pool, Stephen P. Pool, David P. Pool, Pettipool Massie and Elizabeth his wife, formerly Pool, Peterson P. Pool and Elizabeth P. Pool, heirs of Henry P. Pool decd [all descendants of Nancy A. Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder]. Obedience Wilkinson, formerly Browder, John Browder, Lucy Browder, Nancy Browder, Susan Payne, Joseph Browder, and Elizabeth Browder

Plts against John T. Sydnor exor of David Thacker [son of Susanna Browder and John Thacker] decd who was admr of Jones Browder decd ISHAM BROWDER [son of Capt. Isham Browder, Sr. and Talitha Cox], Augustine Browder, Nancy Fear[s], formerly Browder, Talitha Clarke, formerly Browder and Martha Whitmore, formerly Browder, heirs of ISHAM BROWDER decd [who married Talitha Cox]

Mary A. Low, formerly Browder, Littleberry Browder [son of Richard and Mary Browder], Anthony Sydnor and Mary C. [daughter of David Browder and Mary Pegram] his wife, formerly Browder, Richard Browder [son of David Browder and Mary Cousins], Robert Browder [son of David Browder and Mary Cousins], William Browder [son of David Browder and Mary Cousins], John Moore and Nancy [daughter of David Browder and Mary Cousins] his wife, formerly Browder, Caleb Bell and Jane [daughter of David Browder and Mary Cousins] his wife, formerly Browder, Robert Hutchings and Almedia [daughter of David Browder and Mary Cousins] his wife, formerly Browder, heirs of David Browder decd, John P. Pool, Coleman Gordon and Nancy his wife, formerly Pool heirs of Perkinson P. Pool [descendants of Nancy A. Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder], David Hampton and Elizabeth his wife, formerly Browder, Sarah Barnes formerly Browder, Anica Kennedy formerly Browder & Rhoda Browder Defts.

This day this cause came on to be . . . heard on the bill answer of John T. Sydnor exor of David Thacker decd and exhibits, the other defendants who are out of the county and against whom the plaintiffs appear to have proceeded in the mode proscribed by law against absent defendants still failing to appear and answer, on the motion of the plaintiffs by counsel the Court doth take their bill for confession as to those defendants and was agreed by council on consideration whereof the court doth order that the defendant John T. Sydnor exor of David Thacker decd do render an amount of his Testators administrations of the estate of Jones Browder decd before a commissioner . . . is directed to . . . and settle the same and make report thereof to the Court with any matters specially stated deemed pertinent by himself, or which may be required by the parties to be so stated, and that the commissioner advertize once a week for two months in some newspaper printed in the city of Richmond and in the Cty of Washington for all persons who may claim to be distributes of Jones Browder decd in the maternal line to appear before him and prove their relationship to the decedent on or before the first day of September next or they will be excluded from all share of the estate.”

NOTE: No members of the maternal line ever responded, so none ever became part of the suit.

Now, to be clear, Martha Browder (c1774-c1811) is mentioned multiple times in these records from the Dinwiddie County, Virginia Order Book of the Circuit Superior Court, which details the settlement of Jones Browder’s estate, although she was already deceased by the time the case went to court and, thus, her children, Richard and Mary, are also named in her stead. Martha is mentioned because she was a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, and Jones Browder (c1764-c1826) was her nephew, making her children cousins of Jones Browder. This becomes absolutely clear from the text. So, if her children were first cousins to Jones Browder (and they were), then the father of Jones Browder had to have been her brother. There is no way around this. And it has been established by documentation that Jones Browder had three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel, and that all of them were sons of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786). That makes Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) Martha’s brother.

THIS Arthur Browder, clearly a son of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, is NOT the one who may have married Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772). He is, instead, believed to have first married a daughter of Gabriel and Grace Harrison, although this has not been proven. He is known to have married Rebecca Jones, perhaps his second wife.

The case was finally settled on April 4, 1837 by John T. Sydnor, Executor of David Thacker, who had been Administrator of Jones Browder’s estate. On that date, Jones’ estate was ordered divided among his FIRST COUSINS on the PATERNAL side of his family. It was divided among some of them on September 21, 1836, and also on April 4, 1837. Martha Browder and her heirs (children), Richard and Mary, are named in these documents. Martha Browder, who married Isham Browder, Jr. (son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox), therefore, again, WAS a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.

For the children of Martha Browder (daughter of Richard and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia) to have been first cousins on the paternal side of Jones Browder’s family, Martha had to be a sister to Arthur Browder, father of Jones Browder, period. But they didn’t have to be in order to be named because Martha was a first cousin on the paternal side of the family in question herself. So, SHE is the one actually named as an inheritor. But, since she was already deceased when everything was decided, her children inherited in her stead.

NOTE: Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Rev. War, who later moved to Kentucky, was deceased by the time this lawsuit was initiated. However, that would not have prevented his still-living wife and children from participating IF they had had a claim to the estate. But none of them ever participated. This leads to the FACT that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was NOT a first cousin on the paternal side of this family, nor were his children. He was simply NOT descended from any of these people, except for John and Elizabeth Browder. Remember, only those who were not descended from Richard and Mary Browder, but WERE descended from John and Elizabeth Browder, were allowed to participate if they petitioned the court and it was accepted by the other parties. But none of Isham’s descendants did that either. In fact, several lines descended from John and Elizabeth did not take part. Perhaps they did not feel that they deserved a part of Jones Browder’s estate. Others, on the other hand, did.

I submit that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was indeed a descendant of John and Elizabeth Browder. But his parents were NOT any combination of Arthur Browder and wife, or of any Isham and Martha Browder union. Proof has been provided that the Isham Browder who married his cousin, Martha (purported to have been the parents of Isham Browder (1762-1830) could not have been his parents, not least of which because they were born AFTER he was. Also, he was NOT a son of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) because ALL of his sons are known from court documentation and other records and none of them were named Isham. Finally, Isham Browder (1762-1830) could not have been a son of Isham Browder, Sr. (who married Agnes Tatum and later Talitha Cox), because Isham Browder, Sr. had sons name Isham by both marriages (yes, that sometimes happened), and neither one was born in 1762. And we know that Pvt. Isham Browder was born in 1762 because of his military pension records and his tombstone.

ADDENDUM:

Genealogy research being relatively new even during my early years (I started in 1976), and somewhat prior to that, some people began asking questions about their heritage quite early, and for that, I am grateful. The first to be mentioned in this regard is David Parrish Browder, Sr. of Logan County, Kentucky (b. 1882), great-grandson of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. He is known to have conducted extensive interviews with living family members. Part of his questioning focused on “Isham Browder”. David thought that the Isham Browder he was receiving information about was the Isham Browder (1762-1830) who had moved to Fulton County, Kentucky. He basically received accurate information, but had it confused with the wrong Isham Browder.

This is shown in a letter he wrote on March 6, 1937 to Gussie Browder, who resided in Fulton, Kentucky. In this letter David stated that he felt “certain” that Richard Browder’s brother, Arthur, had had a son named Isham. He was the first known to have connected Isham Browder to Arthur Browder as his infant heir mentioned in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book. Obviously, David had also read the excerpt in the Vestry Book which, again, did not actually name Arthur’s heir. The letter also establishes David’s understanding that Richard Browder had taken Arthur’s infant heir into his own household to be brought up following Arthur’s death. And this information, taken by itself, is correct, as records show.

However – and this is where the inaccuracy comes in – David also believed that the Isham Browder brought up by Richard Browder was a father of Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Revolutionary War. He also believed that the elder Isham Browder had married his cousin, Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.

Thus, this latter part is completely incorrect, as records show. The Isham Browder who was the son of Arthur Browder was brought up by Richard and Mary Browder and did have a son named Isham. But it was Isham, Jr. who married his cousin, Martha. In so many words, David had obtained basically the correct information, but none of it had to do with Isham Browder (1762-1830) of Kentucky. We now know these things because of extant court documentation which David apparently did not uncover, but which is brought out here.

Now, a cousin of David P. Browder, Thomas E. Browder of Olmstead, Kentucky (great-grandson of Richard and Mary Browder), had also done a great deal of research. Sadly, most of his research was destroyed after his death. However, he wrote a book entitled, “The Genealogy of David Browder of Virginia and His descendants”. The book is dated Nov. 27, 1901.

It is interesting to me that the two earliest known researchers into the Browder family were descendants of Richard (c1719-c1807) and Mary Browder. It seems that their interest was initially sparked by their hope to be connected to Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Revolutionary War. They were in error. Regardless, they did come up with some valuable initial research even if their efforts to connect to that particular Isham Browder were a bit misguided since he was not a descendant of Richard and Mary Browder. It was actually a good guess on their part, though, since it becomes obvious by way of their research that at least one Isham Browder was brought up by Richard and Mary Browder – just not that one. If they had not asked the questions concerning Isham Browder we might not know the relationships today except for what can be gleaned from court records.

Anyone should be proud to be descended from Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830). But they owe it not only to their descendants, but also to him, to get his genealogy right. His life has been misinterpreted for far too long. It takes strength to let go of cherished notions even when faced with evidence that they are incorrect. But I submit, his descendants, and others, have that strength within them and will now strive to do right by him and his descendants.
