Providing accurate information on the Browder and related families

Isham C. Browder and Martha Browder of Rockingham County, North Carolina

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 3, 2019October 4, 2025Posted inUncategorized


By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (corrected and updated Dec. 25-27, 2024)

Isham “Coleman” Browder was born in1773 in Guilford County, North Carolina, and died sometime early in 1832 in Rockingham County, North Carolina, aged about 57 years, almost fifty years after his own father’s death.  He never moved, but Rockingham County was made from the northern part of Guilford County where Isham resided for most of his life.  

I refer to him with the second name of “Coleman” because, as will be shown, his middle initial was “C.”, and it is the likeliest name to begin with that letter that his middle initial could signify, since it is a family name, and “Claiborne” was his brother’s middle name and “Cox” was the middle name of one of his sisters.  Also, he had several relatives from the Cox family with the second name of Coleman. In addition, he had a grandson, Isham C. Browder, who was obviously named after him and who had a son, Robert Coleman Browder.  I cannot prove that “Coleman” is correct, but it is most likely since Talitha’s mother had been Mary Coleman (c1702-c1794). And there is no other connection to the Coleman family. Thus, I will refer to him in said manner. 

Isham Coleman Browder (1773-1832) is known to have married his cousin, Martha Browder (c1768-c1811), daughter of Richard Browder and Mary Eppes Randolph of Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  They were married in late 1799,  probably in Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  We know this because Martha Browder first married (1) Simon Swail on May 7, 1792, at Petersburg, Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  This ceremony was performed by the Rev. John Cameron, an Episcopal minister (Bell, Cumberland Parish Lunenburg County, Virginia 1746-1816, Vestry Book 1746-1816). However, Simon Swail soon died. We know that he was deceased at least by 1799 because the Alexandria County, District of Columbia (Arlington County, Virginia) Marriage Bonds Book, 1801-1822, and Minister’s Returns, pp. 128-29 contains a will abstract of Simon Swail which reads:

Will of Simon Swail

Recorded: June 3, 1799 Will Bk. 1, p. 281

Heirs: wife Martha; my bro. Vallentin’s son Vall; my sister Betsey and her Simon named for me.

Presented by – Jas. Campbell, Eliz. McDonald.

Thus, this union is obvious from the records, and is also directly stated in the letter written by David P. Browder on November 27, 1901.  David, it should be remembered, was a great-grandson of Richard Browder and Mary. 

Mary “Thompson” (Mary Eppes Randolph) was also a descendant of the Isham family line, in addition to a Randolph family line.  And one should also remember that Isham Coleman Browder had a sister named after Martha’s mother, Mary Thompson Browder (d. 1793), indicating a very close family connection. 

This record demonstrates that Simon Swail soon died, and the couple had been childless.  Therefore, Martha would have inherited the property of Simon Swail, if any, upon his death.  And, in fact, we know that he owned some property because on Jan. 5, 1796, Isaac Lowe deeded 157 acres of land on Little Troublesome and Hogan’s Creeks in Rockingham County, North Carolina to Simon Swail of Petersburg, Virginia. So, it seems obvious that Simon and Martha Swail had intended to move to that area. But Simon soon died and after his death Martha remarried to Isham Coleman Browder. 

Isaac Lowe, by the way, did not marry Mary A. Browder (taken from the court records of the estate of Jones Browder) as some suggest. He married Catherine, whose maiden name is not known, but who outlived him. 

Martha Browder was born in Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  She lived up to about 1811, for certain, probably dying due to complications from child-birth.  Isham is found in the 1820 census of Rockingham County, but, mysteriously, Isham does not appear in the 1830 census, although he was certainly living as late as 1832, according to court records.  One has to think that he was residing with someone else, but I have still not been able to locate him in the 1830 census record. 

It is known that Isham C. Browder was in Prince George County, Virginia, between 1789-90.  He is listed as residing with William Cole in the 1790 Virginia Tax List, which listed all males aged 16 and older within a household.  His mother and siblings may well have also been residing there at that time, but they were not listed.  They would not have been listed because females were not listed at all in those tax records unless they were heads of households, and anyone under the age of 16 would not have been listed at all.  

Again, Isham C. Browder probably remained in Virginia for a few years to be educated, and while there married his cousin, Martha, following the death of her first husband, Simon Swail.  It seems obvious that Talitha Browder and her family, including Isham C. Browder, resided very near the Richard Browder family of Dinwiddie County, Virginia during this time, within the household of William Cole.  So, Isham certainly would have known his cousin, Richard, son of David Browder.  Regardless, by 1800 Isham and Martha Browder had returned to Rockingham County, North Carolina, as shown in the 1800 census and the 1808 Power of Attorney (discussed later).  Talitha, as well her other children, would have returned by about that same time, all having returned no later than 1800. 

I believe that Augustine Claiborne Browder also spent time in Virginia for educational purposes and may have continued to reside with William Cole after other family members returned to North Carolina, but would have returned soon after they did.  I have found several Booth families in Dinwiddie County, Virginia listed in censuses, etc. which could have been families from which Betsy Booth, Augustine’s wife, came.  If so, then the two brothers both married women from their “ancestral homeland” of Virginia. 

During the August 1800 session of the Rockingham County, North Carolina County Court, Robert Chapman was given leave (allowed a license for the year) to keep the tavern at “Browder’s Crossroads”.  This is the first mention in the records of the tavern located at Browder’s Crossroads.  But it would by no means be the last. 

Interestingly, the very first mention of Isham C. Browder in any record comes from Rockingham County, North Carolina Court Minutes.  During the February 1803 session, Isham Browder was given leave to keep the tavern located at Browder’s Crossroads.  Obviously, this tavern had already existed prior to this point (as shown in the records), but who started it is unclear from this record. 

Still, that is not the first mention in said records of Browder’s Crossroads, for during the February 1800 session of said Court, Edward Newham was appointed as overseer of the road from John Linder’s to “Browder’s Crossroads”.  Later, during the August 1800 session, Robert Chapman was again given leave to keep the tavern at Browder’s Crossroads.

Because of these records, we see that Browder’s Crossroads had been established which accommodated people traveling to and through that area by means of a tavern or, as it was called in the day, an “ordinary”. How much of this had been established by Capt. Isham and Talitha Browder is uncertain. But it seems obvious that they had at least started everything, with Isham Coleman Browder and Robert Chapman, at least, continuing the trend. The records seem to suggest that Augustine had no real interest in the tavern while Isham did. So, the two brothers concentrated on different efforts, Augustine placing more emphasis upon being a Jurist like his father and Isham placing more emphasis upon tavern keeping as well as law enforcement, as we will see. 

To continue with the records, during the May 1802 session, John Smith was appointed as overseer of the road from H. Mill’s to the crossroads, where Chapman’s “Ordinary” (tavern) was located (so, this time it was called “Chapman’s” in the record).  But, during the February 1803 session, Isham Browder was given leave to keep the tavern at the crossroads, with security of 250 pounds from John Wardlow.  Thus, it seems as if the tavern actually sort-of switched hands between the two men, even though it was likely started by a member of the Browder family and was located at Browder’s Crossroads. 

This Robert Chapman (b. C1772, Essex County, Virginia) was a son of Reuben Chapman and Ann Reynolds. He was a relative of Talitha Cox-Browder, Isham’s mother, and also possibly a relative of Elizabeth “Betsy” Booth-Browder, wife of Augustine Claiborne Browder. Robert Chapman married Elizabeth “Bettie” Sutton. 

Now, Isham C. Browder acquired much of the land his father had been granted (at least some 908 acres of it, in all).  According to the land deeds, Isham managed to acquire about 1,308 acres of land in total over the course of his lifetime because he deeded that amount to others.  But, obviously, all of this land did not come from his father.  And, from 1804 to 1807, Isham C. Browder acquired even more land than this in his own right, which was also located on Wolf Island Creek. 

On February 28, 1804, Isham C. Browder sold 70 acres of his land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Clement Whittemore, Jr. (RCDB I, p. 91).  This deed was witnessed by A[braham]. Philips (the man educated by Thomas Isham Browder), along with J. Ladd. 

Interestingly, during the May 1804 session of the Court, Isham Browder and Thomas Winston were selected to function as slave patrollers in William Bethell’s district.  This is the first indication that Isham served in a law enforcement capacity.  Then, during the August 1804 session, Aaron Cantrell was appointed as overseer of the road from Cantrell’s Meeting House to “Browder’s place”.  Later, during the November 1804 session, William Wheeler was appointed as overseer from Morehead’s Tanyard to “Browder’s Road”.  Also, in November of 1804, Alex Alexerdice was appointed as overseer of the road from “Browder’s Fork” (the same as Browder’s Crossroads) to the Danville Road (now US 29 Business).  And, in November of 1804, Charles Wright was appointed as overseer of the road from Browder’s Fork to Hugh Williams’ old place. 

As will be shown, we know from the records that Isham C. Browder later served as a constable. He also worked in some capacity in the legal field because he was referred to as “Esquire” in later records. When, exactly, he began either is a question, but it would seem that the previous records show that he started at least as early as 1804. 

On September 14, 1805, Isham C. Browder deeded one acre of that land, located on the south side of Wolf Island Creek, to the church trustee, Isaiah Hancock, and the members of the Baptist Society at Wolf Island, for ten shillings.  Witnesses to this deed were Aaron Cantrell and John H. Taylor (RCDB M, p. 33). 

Cantrell’s Meeting House, formed in 1777, had been situated there for some time prior to this transaction, having been started by Isaac Cantrell, who had moved on to South Carolina.  The deed was made so that the new church could officially be established, having its own land, which would include the cemetery already started there, the oldest grave there being known to have been for Talitha Cloud-Cantrell (1729-1768). 

It can be surmised from this that Isham and Martha Browder were possibly members of the Baptist Church at Wolf Island, where his own mother, Talitha, was certainly a member by 1827, according to a list of members for that year.  The present-day church and cemetery are located at Reidsville, North Carolina, at the intersection of Ida Wilson Rd. and Wolf Island Rd. (formerly, Browder’s Road).  It is today called Wolf Island Primitive Baptist Church.  From RCDB M, p. 33 (from a copy made by genealogist Maude Reynolds):

BROWDER, ISHAM To Hancock, Isaiah 1805: This indenture made this 14th day of Sept. in the year of our Lord, one thousand eight hundred and five, between ISHAM BROWDER in the Co. of Rockingham and State of N. Carolina, of the one part, and Isaiah Hancock and members of the Baptist Society at Wolf Island, in conjunction in the same County and state aforesaid, of the other part; – witnesseth that the said ISHAM BROWDER for and in consideration to him of the sum of ten shillings, to him in hand paid at and before the signing of these presents the receipt thereof, he doth hereby acknowledge and hereof exonerate, acquit and discharge, hath given, granted, bargained sold aliened, enfeoffed and confirmed, and do by these presents to give, grant, bargain, sell & confirm unto the sd’ Isaiah Hancock and said members, his and their heirs and assigns, one acre of land, situate, lying and being on the south side of Wolf Island Creek in the county aforesaid: Beginning at a post ISHAM BROWDER’S line, running east 8 poles to a post oak saplin, thence north with ISHAM BROWDER’S line 20 poles to a hickory saplin, thence west 8 poles to a white oak, thence south 20 poles to the first station, and contains 1 acre of land, the same more or less. Together, with all and singular the rights and profits, woods, ways, waters hereditaments and appurtenances to the said land belonging or appertaining to hold to the sd’ Isaiah Hancock, and members, his and their heirs and assigns forever, and the s’d. ISHAM BROWDER and his heirs the said tenement and premises and every part thereof against him and his heirs and against all and every other person or  persons, whatsoever, laying lawful right or claim thereunto, to have and to hold to the said Isaiah Hancock, his heirs and assigns forever do warrant and defend the same. In witness whereof ISHAM BROWDER set my hand and seal, this day and year first above written.

Test: – Aaron Cantrill   ISHAM BROWDER (seal)  John H. Taylor.

So, Isham C. Browder deeded one acre of land to this church body which would have been where the building and the cemetery were located already.  The earliest extant gravestone in this cemetery is dated 1817.  There are, in addition, known to probably be at least 100 unmarked graves in this cemetery.  Many of these date to earlier than 1817, and could include Browders.  In fact, once again, it is probable that Thomas Isham, Talitha, Martha, Isham C., and Augustine Claiborne Browder (along with any earlier children Isham and Talitha may have had) are all buried there, along with related members of the Harris family, in unmarked graves.  It seems more than obvious that the church was located on Cantrell land, which was borderingIsham’s property, and that Isham sold the one acre to this church because he wanted the part of the cemetery where his father had been buried to be included in the church property. Also, from RCDB K, p. 21:

Whittmore one hundred and Sixty nine acres of Land situate lying and being on the Waters of the Lickfork of Hogan’s Creek in the County aforesaid. Beginning at a corner of William Bethells land following his line to the other corner of Bethell’s land Thence South to John Johnstons line . . . it contains one hundred Sixty nine Acres of Land. To Witness whereof To the Said Lewis W. ISHAM BROWDER Attorney in Fact

This document shows that Isham C. Browder witnessed said deed, standing in for William Bethell, Jr., who was Aquilla Wilson’s attorney; Isham Browder also being named “Attorney in Fact” because he was Wilson’s Power of Attorney. 

During the November session of 1805, mention is made of the road from John Linder’s to “Browder’s old tavern”.  So, it seems that, if Isham C. Browder did not start said tavern, perhaps Talitha, or even Thomas Isham Browder, did.  Or this document could be referring to an older tavern no longer in use by 1805 because it seems unlikely that the one still in operation would have been referred to as “old”. 

During the August 1806 session, Robert “Chapen” was given leave to keep this tavern at “Browder’s Crossroads” once again. 

In 1807 Isham C. Browder was deeded 100 acres of land.  On March 6, 1807, Isham Browder sold 41 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to George Courts.  George paid Isham $82.00 for said land.  This land was bounded by land already owned by George Courts, as well as the land of Samuel Smith and Clement Whittemore.  Witnesses to the deed were A[braham]. Philips and Aaron Cantrill.  This deed was proven in open court and recorded during the May Session of 1807 (RCDB M, p. 244). 

Also on the same date, Isham Browder sold 127 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Samuel Smith (RCDB M, p. 247).  A[braham]. Philips and Aaron Cantrell also witnessed this second deed, which was proven during the May Session of 1807.

During the May 1808 session of the County Court, William Lawson was appointed as overseer of the road leading from William Bethell’s house to Isham Browder’s.  During the November 1808 session of the County Court, Alex Alexedire was appointed as overseer of the road leading from the forks of “Browder’s Road” to Wolf Island Meeting House (church).  Also, Vincent Wheeler was appointed as overseer of the road from Morehead’s Shop to the forks of Browder’s Road. 

And, as indicated previously, in 1808 Isham C. Browder and Martha Browder, his wife, gave Power of Attorney to Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia to sell Martha’s interest in about 200 acres of land, located on the Gravelly Run (RCDB N, p. 339).  While all of this is not directly specified, Richard Browder, Martha’s father, was not a large landowner and probably only owned about 200 acres at the time of his death.  So, it is certain that he did not bequeath it all to Martha, but bequeathed each of his living children a certain percentage of divided interest in said land.  But, sadly, we don’t have a Will to consult.

It is likely that the Richard Browder mentioned in the Power of Attorney was the son of her brother, David Browder and his wife, Mary Cousins and that the land in question must have been inherited from her father, Richard Browder, who resided in Dinwiddie County all of his life.  It should be understood that there is no reason for Martha to have been named in such a document other than the fact that she had inherited a percentage in said land, and that she was actually the one giving Power of Attorney to sell land that she had inherited.  Otherwise, it would have only been necessary for Isham to have been named. 

So, this document shows a direct relationship between Richard Browder and his daughter, Martha, and shows that she married her cousin, Isham C. Browder.  It clearly shows a connection between Martha Browder and Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  The fact that Power of Attorney was given to a member of the Browder family also shows conclusively that Martha was a member of the Browder family.  Otherwise, why would she not have elected a member of some other family?  This is the only known document that conclusively names Martha Browder as the wife of Isham C. Browder, although the division of Jones Browder’s property also indicates it directly.  From RCDB N, p. 339 (from a copy by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

Browder, Richard from BROWDER, ISHAM & WIFE, Power of Attorney, 200 A of Land: Know all men by these presents that we ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER his WIFE, both of the county of Rockingham and state of N. C., have for good cause to us moving, have nominated, authorized, ordained and appointed, and do by these presents authorize, ordain and appoint our friend Richard Browder of the county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth of Va., to act, make over convey and confirm a certain piece or parcel of land situate, lying and being in the said county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth aforesaid and the waters of the Gravelly Run, and contains about 200 acres of land:  And we, the said ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves and heirs, creditors and administrators do make or convey and confirm unto the said Richard Browder full power absolute authority to sell and dispose, make over, convey and confirm at the will and pleasure, a sure and good right and title to the aforesaid land and premises as above described, to whomsoever he may think proper, as full and ample in every respect, as we could do were we personally present, to all which  matter and things – we the said ISHAM BROWDER and the said MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves our heirs creditors and Administrators firmly by these presents, sealed with our seals and dated this day of ___ in the year 1808. Signed, sealed and acknowledged in the presence of ISHAM BROWDER (seal) Augustine C. Browder MARTHA BROWDER (seal) Thos. Winston Joseph Mclain St. of N. C. Rockingham Co. May Session 1810: The written power of attorney was duly proved in open court by the oath of Thomas Winston & on motion ordered to be recorded.      R. Galloway, C.

Notice that, although he was a witness, Augustine C. Browder did not directly inherit any of said land, nor similar property, and did not make a Power of Attorney to convey anything himself.  This might have been the case if Isham C. Browder were the inheritor of said property, because his brother, as well as other siblings, would have also inherited similar property from Richard Browder in that case.  This shows that Isham C. Browder was not descended from Richard Browder, but that Martha Browder was.  She is obviously the one who inherited this property, located in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. 

It is interesting to note that, in this document it is stated that the couple believed that they had good reason to move.  Obviously, the inference is that they moved from Dinwiddie County, Virginia, or close to that location, and relocated back to Rockingham County, North Carolina at some point, obviously prior to 1800.  What prompted this move?  The answer to this question is unknown, but they probably always intended to.  Whatever their reasoning was, they considered it good enough not only to move, but also to elect another trusted family member to deal with her inheritance so that they did not have to return to do so.   What eventually happened to said property is still an open question since I have located no further documentation concerning it.  But, presumably, it was sold, so that Isham and Martha Browder received their part of the proceeds from said sale.

Later, during the February 1809 session of the Court, George Courts was appointed as overseer of the road from William Bethell’s to Isham Browder’s.  And John Ellington was appointed as overseer of the road from “Browder’s Fork” to Richard C. Hall’s.  Also, during the May 1809 session of the Court, William Lawson was appointed as overseer of the road leading from William Bethell’s to Isham Browder’s.  Finally, in August of 1809, the Court utilized Isham Browder as one of its jurors during that current court session. 

During the May 1810 session of the Court, Abel Hudson was appointed overseer of the road from Morehead’s Shop to “the forks of Browder’s Road”.  Also, William Lawson was appointed overseer of Iron Works Road from Caswell’s line (the Caswell County line) to Isham Browder’s.  

During the February 1811 session of the Court, Charles Wright was appointed overseer of the road from the forks of Browder’s Road to Wolf Island Meeting House. 

Then, on June 4, 1812, Isham Browder sold 51 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Clement Whittemore, Jr.  Isham signed this document, as he often (but not always) did, rather than only providing a mark.  This deed was witnessed by A[braham]. Philips, Sterling Whittemore, and Pleasant Ellington.  It was proven during the May Session of 1816 (RCDB Q, p. 124). 

Also, during the February 1812 session of the Court, “Pideus” Wright was appointed as overseer of the road from Browder’s forks to Richard C. Hall’s. 

During the August 1813 session of the County Court, John Miller was appointed overseer of the road from Browder’s to Richard C. Hall’s.  During the February 1814 session of the Court, the Sheriff was instructed to pay John Harris (Jr.), Isham Browder, and John Hancock ten shillings for slave patrols in Capt. ? district.  And during the August 1814 session, the Court used Isham Browder as one of its jurors during that current session. 

During the February 1815 session of the Court, Philip Briserdine was appointed overseer of the road from Col. Bethell’s to Browder’s Crossroads, with help from George Courts, William Lawson, R. H. Courts, and Samuel Smith, Sr.  And, during the August 1815 session of the Court, Isham Browder was again used as a juror, along with others. 

The 1815 Rockingham County tax list shows that Isham C. Browder had (or claimed) 743 acres of land, valued at $1,486.00, at that time.  Isham Browder also received another 51 acres back from Clement Whittemore, Jr. in 1816 (RCDB Q, p. 124).  And, in 1817, Isham Browder sold two slaves to Francis Bell (RCDB Q, p. 431). 

During the February 1816 session of the Court, Abraham Whittemore was appointed overseer of the road leading from William Lawson’s lower plantation on Wolf Island Creek to Isham Browder’s, with the help of Elizabeth Browder, George Courts, William Lawson, and others. 

On February 24, 1818, Isham C. Browder purchased 22 more acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, from Richard Micheaux.  This deed was witnessed by Abraham Philips, and proven during the February Session of 1818 (RCDB R, p. 165).  And, on December 6, 1824, Isham Browder sold 350 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to William J. Meador (RCDB Y, p. 175), which was Isham’s way of beginning to divide his land between his two heirs.   Obviously, for such a transaction to have taken place, Talitha Browder would have had to have given her blessing and Isham’s siblings would have understood that it was his to do with as he chose.  From RCDB Y, p. 175 (from a copy made by genealogist, Maude Reynolds – name Meador corrected from “Meadows”):

BROWDER, ISHAM To Meador, William Deed 350 a Wolf Is. Ck. 1824: This indenture made this 6th day of Dec. in the year of our Lord, 1824, between ISHAM BROWDER of the county of Rockingham & St. of N. Browder’s son.   From RCDB Y, p. 175-76 (from a copy made by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

On January 26, 1826 Isham Browder deeded 168 more acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Richard Browder, his son.  From RCDB Z, p. 304 (from a copy made by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

The above deed obviously references the 168 acres received by Capt. Isham Browder from the State of North Carolina on November 8, 1784.  Finally, on January 26, 1826, Isham Browder sold 168 more acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to his son, Richard Browder (RCDB Z, p. 310).   This last tract of land is the one that Richard Browder then immediately sold to Clement Whittemore, Jr. (ibid). 

The initial 168 acre tract is the one that Isham C. Browder actually resided on at Browder’s Crossroads, Isham likely finally selling it and the latter 168 acre tract both on the same date to Richard Browder due to failing health. Richard was then expected to take over, in effect.

Now, moving back in time just a little, this family is not shown in the 1790 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina.  However, before 1800, according to the census of Salisbury, Rockingham County, for that year, “Isam” and Martha Browder had already had their first child, a daughter.  The child is listed as being under ten years of age.  We know that her name was Mary Thompson “Polly” Browder, named after Isham’s sister, who had been murdered by Native Americans in 1793.  Mary Thompson Browder, daughter of Isham C. and Martha Browder, would have been born in 1800.  This family also owned one slave in 1800.  They resided close to Talitha Browder, and also close to Clem. Whittemore, William Bethell, and John Dillon. 

By 1810, according to the Rockingham County census of that year, Isham C. Browder and Martha Browder had two sons, as well as one daughter.  Their sons are listed as being between the ages of 10-15 and under 10, respectively.  The daughter is listed in the next to youngest age range (10-15). 

We do know from extant records that one of their sons, my ancestor, was named Richard.  Richard Browder was born about 1801 (late 1800 or early 1801), so he would have been the first son.  

In 1810, according to the already mentioned census, the Isham C. Browder family still resided close to Talitha Browder, who resided with one daughter (Martha) and 12 slaves at the old home place.  They also resided close to the family of Isham’s brother, Augustine, and close to the family of John Sanders (who was in the oldest category of males for that census), and the family of Zephaniah Sanders (same age category), as well as the families of Abraham Whittemore, William Bethell, William Lawson, William Sanders, and Walter Martin.

The 1820 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina lists Isham Browder (45 and over), along with his daughter (Mary Thompson, 26-45) as residing close to the households of his mother, Talitha Browder, and his son, Richard Browder.  Also listed within the same household is one male, aged between 16 and 26 (presumably Richard Browder’s brother).  Isham is also listed as owning two male slaves that year.  Martha, wife of Isham C. Browder, is not listed.  So, sadly, it becomes obvious that she was deceased by this date in time. 

Richard Browder, their son, is listed separately in the 1820 census as head of household.  We also know that Richard’s presumed brother reached maturity since he is listed as still residing with Isham Browder in 1820.  According to this census, Isham Browder could also read and write, as we already know (but, he still sometimes signed only with a mark). 

Elizabeth Browder, widow of Augustine Claiborne Browder, and her family, at that time resided close to the old home place where Talitha Browder and other family members resided, in the home she had already been residing in for several years. It is obvious that they resided on the 640 acre tract that Talitha Browder had purchased from John Browder. We know this partly because this tract was not among those that Isham C. Browder sold or divided between his two heirs. So, it was obviously understood, regardless of what Capt. Isham Browder’s 1779 will stated, that this land was to go to Augustine and his heirs. 

Interestingly, during the November 1825 session of the County Court, the case of the State vs. Isham Browder and Polly Newman came up.  It is not at all clear what this case was about, but either he or they was/were found guilty as charged.  Isham was fined $50.00, and Polly was fined $10.00, with John Forrest to pay her fine for her. 

Now, again, it is known that Isham C. Browder served as a Rockingham County Constable during the years 1829, 1830 and 1831 (Perdue), as shown by the records.  Whether he served in this capacity prior to these dates cannot be ascertained from extant records, but it seems obvious that he did.  It is interesting to note here that future U.S. President, Andrew Jackson studied law in Guilford County, North Carolina, and was also appointed as a Constable there in 1789 (Stoesen, p. 9).  Could the Browder family have known him personally and professionally during this time?  It is entirely possible.  Richard Browder himself certainly knew him at a later date, I think.  From p. 125, Rockingham County Minute Docket, Spring Term 1807 to Spring Term 1826:

It has previously been thought by some researchers that Isham C. Browder’s Will was dated 1833 (RCWB B, p. 84).  However, having requested, and received, the above dated Will myself from the North Carolina State archives, it has become obvious to me that the Will in question is, instead, that of Capt. It is obvious from the list of property here that, by this generation, our Browder ancestors were no longer in the tobacco business.  This is in contrast to the Isham Browder (1762-1830), the American Revolutionary War soldier.  He was very much in the tobacco business.  It seems, then, that the Isham C. Browder and Martha Browder family made their living strictly by farming, and through the profits of various land transactions (and, probably also through horse breeding and racing).  This in addition to whatever legal fees Isham may have charged for his services and what little a constable of that day might have been paid. 

Only Richard and William J. Meador received any perishable property following the death of his father, Isham.  This brings about a question.  Why indeed had Richard’s brother received no perishable property upon the death of his father, Isham?  It seems obvious that he was deceased by this time.

Meador married Richard’s older sister, (1) Mary Thompson Browder (1800-c1844).  In Rockingham County Marriage Bonds Book [RCMBB]   ?, p. 172, can be found a record of the marriage between “Polly” Browder and William J. Meador on December 24, 1823 (Ingmire, p. 46).  Clement Whittemore, Jr. stood as bondsman, and the ceremony was witnessed by Robert Galloway, Jr.  This is the main reason we can conclude that she is the one listed in the 1820 census, rather than her mother.  She was still residing within the household of her father, Isham C. Browder, in 1820, since she had not yet married. 

By 1837 William J. Meador himself had sold 229 acres of land to Luke Palmer.  Thereafter, he and his wife, Mary, moved to Itawamba County, Mississippi.

If all of this seems a bit mixed up or confusing, so does the court case.  The case concerning the Will of Thomas Isham Browder, which plagued Isham C. Browder’s final years, was one of the major probate court cases in early laws of civil proceedings in the United States.  Documentation from these proceedings follows verbatim:

All that seems to be certain about the purported Will of 1778 is that it has apparently not survived to this day, and that no one, other than Isham, seemed to have been particularly interested in it during the lawsuit period.  It seems reasonable to believe, however, that the “lost Will” would have changed nothing between family members in any case.  The court determined that all real estate was to be more or less equally divided among all of the children of Isham and Talitha Browder. 

But, obviously, Isham C. Browder had indeed seen this Will and it is the one that had earlier been annexed and followed.  Capt. Isham Browder surely had meant for this Will to have been replaced by the 1779 Will in any case.  The fact that Isham Browder never wrote another Will to include his remaining children more directly, by name, is sad.  But it changed nothing.  The change was brought about due to the fact that family members initiated the lawsuit.  If they had not done that, it seems to me that the 1778 Will of Isham Browder would have been followed, because it never would have wound up in court.  This is what Talitha Browder had obviously intended – to keep everything intact and bequeathed according to the Will of her late husband. 

Sometimes, one can actually solve a mystery by noting what is not said, just as much as what is said.  It is my belief, even after all of the previous speculation as to what may have caused the death of Capt. Isham Browder, that the greatest likelihood is that he simply died of some unfortunate accident.  This must have been something which caused him to become missing and, perhaps, eventually found, deceased.  This has become obvious to me because, otherwise, his eldest son would surely have known the exact date of his death.  Since he did not, it is painfully obvious that Isham Browder died, perhaps, while out hunting, or during bad weather, or that he encountered someone who did harm to him, and his body was found at some later date.  Whatever it was, it was sudden.  So, no one really knew exactly when he died, or what happened to him.  This would explain a great deal concerning conflicting information in extant records.  In any event, it is still certain that he died in late 1784 or early 1785 (most likely, late 1784).  To continue with the court case:

One can only see the foregoing documentation from the North Carolina State Archives as a surreal situation of at least as great a mystery as anything else involving Isham Browder.  The reason is that this Abraham Philips, having known Isham Browder well, and having outlived both Isham Browder and his wife, Talitha, by this time, stated that he not only has had in his possession several of Isham’s papers (presumably school papers), but still possessed said papers some 46 years after Isham’s death!  Beyond that, what became of all of these papers – not only the ones in the possession of Abraham Philips, but also those which Talitha Browder had in her possession at the time of her death?  Obviously, Capt. Isham Browder left much in the form of important documents, which we do not have access to today. 

It should also be noted that, not only do these foregoing documents essentially prove that Capt. Isham Browder (1) died in mid to late 1784 (Isham C. Browder’s testimony), but also show that he (2) taught school or was a tutor, and that he (3) died at or close to his home in, then, Guilford County, probably on the 569 acre plot since the eastern edge of that was close to DeLap’s shop.  Still, apparently, no one knew (a) when Isham died, or (b) about the contents of the two wills, other than, perhaps, Isham C. Browder, since Talitha was deceased.  It becomes obvious that, in order to prevent hard feelings among family members while she lived, Talitha simply allowed everyone to believe whatever they wished as long as she lived. 

Finally, two proof texts, also from the North Carolina State Archives, that show Isham Browder, Jr.’s middle initial as “C.” are provided.  And on the following pages, the details of the court decision, in which Isham’s name is repeatedly spelled incorrectly (which he did not actually do when writing his own Will) as “Isom”, are presented.

What exactly the meaning of this final court decision is, does not seem to be quite obvious, at least to an intelligent mind.  Like any other Will, the Will of Capt. Isham Browder appears to pass everything to future generations effectively.  It is, in fact, quite specific even if he did acquire more land after it was written. 

First, he bequeathed his entire estate, including the home and plantation, furniture, etc, plus five horses, all stock and all slaves as well as their children, to Talitha, as long as she lived, or as long as she remained a widow. 

Then, after her death, or the end of her widowhood, he bequeathed that his personal estate “not otherwise devised and its increase” to be equally divided among his living descendants.  So, everything not specifically mentioned in his Will concerning his personal estate, thereafter, was to be equally divided among all of his living children. 

Second, his eldest son, Isham, was to receive the home and plantation, containing at that time about 1,280 acres at that time, plus his English horse and the profits from said horse, except for maintenance costs.  Isham was to receive said profits at the age of 21 and, obviously, was to receive the rest of the property at the death of his mother, or at the end of her widowhood.  At the very least, if read correctly, it stipulated that Isham would receive the two plots, then thought to total 1,280 acres.  That Talitha herself obviously changed things with her purchase, along with Augustine as an infant, of the 640 acre plot from John Browder, demonstrates that she believed that she had the right to adjust things as she saw fit.  It also demonstrates that Isham was definitely deceased by the time she made this purchase. 

Third, all of Isham’s children were to be educated utilizing the profits from his estate.  Fourth, Isham’s wagon and team were to be sold, along with a tract of land of about 150 acres, in order to pay all of his just debts.  Once these debts were paid, Talitha was to receive the balance of the profits of this sale herself. 

Finally, he bequeathed his daughter, Talitha, one gray horse and the profits from said horse entirely clear of any debt or penalties. 

So, the bulk of the estate was to remain with Talitha until it was specified to go to Isham C. Browder, after the just debts of Capt. Isham Browder were paid.  He bequeathed his first daughter a horse.  Everything else concerning his personal estate not specifically mentioned, was to be divided among all living children, including Isham and Talitha, equally.  What might have actually been left, if anything, of his personal estate to be divided after Talitha died and Isham received his share, is uncertain.  But evidently, it wasn’t much, if anything, or relatives surely would not have sued for some of his real estate.  One can plausibly argue  that Isham had intended for all of his real estate to go to his son, Isham, even though he only specified the 1,280 acres he was thought to have at the time of the writing of the will.  Otherwise, surely he might have stipulated that at least some real estate be divided between all of his heirs.  But he made no such stipulation.  So, the fact that he (and Talitha) acquired more land after this will was written is irrelevant.  But that is not how the court saw it. 

Regardless, the following are copies of the page covers that show Isham’s middle initial as “C.”. 

Still, once again, everything was apparently done according to Isham’s Will as long as Talitha lived.  Certainly, some of the children felt essentially left out, without adequate inheritance.  But there was no conspiracy against them involving Talitha or Isham C. Browder.  She preserved at least one of Isham’s original Wills, after all (and, probably, both).  There was, in fact, no reason for anyone to know what Isham’s will was until she passed away, since she had control of the property as long as she lived and remained a widow anyway.  And for all she knew, the correct Will had been duly recorded as it should have been (and maybe it was, but had become missing).  She had no way of knowing that a backlog had existed concerning the recording of Wills and, perhaps, Isham’s Will had not been recorded, as she rightfully believed it had been. 

From what we now know, it is practically a miracle that Isham’s will was ever known in the first place.  The fact that she preserved it ensured that it would be known and followed.  She was not concealing his Will; she was preserving it.  And she acted accordingly to the best of her ability. 

Still, things did not turn out quite as she had hoped.  Although Isham C. Browder had asked for an appeal, he was never to get that far.  The appeal would have been held during the fall of 1832.  But, instead, Isham died early in 1832.  I do believe that Isham died, probably, due to the stress of the court battles and family squabbles.  That seems obvious. But he had really slighted no one if one looks at all of the land transactions, especially Elizabeth Browder, who apparently is the one who found the 1779 will and initiated the court case.  Again, Isham C. Browder never so much as touched the 640 acre plot that Elizabeth and her children resided upon, so to speak.  It was presumed to be theirs.  He further made other land transactions with the Whittemores with no apparent issues between them.  That he made none with any of his other sisters is simply because they had all moved away to Georgia, and one was already deceased. 

In any case, although it is said that his son, Richard, carried on said appeal, it seems that this appeal changed nothing with reference to the court’s decision.  The court had made its decision.  Capt. Isham Browder had obviously intended for his property to be passed on to his descendants.  Talitha Browder obviously took steps to ensure that it would be, mainly by remaining there when she might instead have left the area to go with her daughters to Georgia.  But following her death, family members sued, and that ensured that the decision would be placed into the hands of others, who decided that Isham’s Will did not effectively pass on said real estate property.  Frankly, it was a travesty for a man who had been an important figure in the county’s history. 

Perhaps the Will of 1778, if it truly existed (and I am certain that it did), was a better Will, and Isham C. Browder was prepared to present it to the court.  Obviously, Isham C. Browder felt that it was a better Will for some reason.  But that never happened, and it was never ordered recorded.  So, again, there is no real mystery concerning the death of Isham C. Browder, in my opinion.  It was caused by the stress of other family members suing him over his father’s estate, a suit which was wholly unnecessary.

State of North Carolina Rockingham County William Meadour & RICHARD BROWDER Administrators of ISHAM BROWDER Decd.  Report to Fall term 1835-  In obedience to the Order of the Honl. Court of Equity for the Court of Rockingham made at Spring term 1835 in the Case above in the Case above stated, I worshipfully submit the following report; I find that ISHAM BROWDER (the Elder) died in the year 1784 or 1785 having personally made & published his will bearing date the 27th September 1779, that at the date of said Will he had living, the following children to wit, Polly, ISHAM, Nancy Washington & Talitha, and that after the date of said Will & before his death he had two other Children born to wit Augustine & Patsey – having the said six children him surviving – and or I answered agreeable to the intentions of the Testatum the said ISHAM BROWDER (the Elder) as … in his said Will, each one of his or her … entitled to one sixth part of the money arising from the Estate in the Hand of the Defendant Clement Whittmore arising from the  … & Sale of the Slaves of the said Testator;  I find that Polly intermarried with Richard Thrasher & that they were both killed by the Indians about the year 1792, leaving an only child Nancy, who grew mature & died childless and Intestate about the year 1807 and that William Meadour one of the Complaintants administer on his Estate*  I find that ISHAM BROWDER (the younger) died in the year 1832 and that William Meadour & RICHARD BROWDER administered on his Estate—I find that Nancy Washington, intermarried with Cloud Thrasher who having died by whom she had two children, and after the death of said Cloud Thrasher both of said children died   Infants, and the said Nancy intermarried with William Fears by whom she had one of the Complaintants Augustine Fears, and she then died leaving her said Husband William Fears him surviving, who hath also since died having other children him surviving.  That Augustine Fears the Complaintant administered on the Estate of the said Cloud Thrasher … on the estate of the said William Fears and Nancy Fears – it is respectfully submitted to the Honl. Court … to say in right of which of his Intestates the said Augustine Fears shall …of said Nancy-I find that Talitha Whittmore with Isaac Thrasher & whom she had three children … having died the said Talitha intermarried with the Complaintant Isaac Clark and that Augustine Fears has administered on the Estate ….

Now, aside from the obvious mistakes in the above document, some facts need to be belatedly stated here.  According to the extant records, Thomas Henderson served as the first Clerk of Court for Guilford County, North Carolina.  He later became the first Clerk of Court for Rockingham County at the court’s first session, held in February of 1786, after the county of Guilford was divided.  After this division, Thomas Searcy was elected the first Clerk of Court for Guilford County on February 21, 1786.  John Hamilton became the third Clerk of Court for Guilford County, serving from 1790 through 1816.  Afterward, John Hanner became the Clerk, during which time A. E. Hanner served as Deputy Clerk.  In 1833 W. W.  Woodburn was elected, but only served for a short time, after which A. E. Hanner became Clerk, having been elected during the May term of 1836.  He served until the November term of 1837.

It appears that no wills were recorded until John Hanner became Clerk of Court in 1816 and that he divided out the accumulation of wills alphabetically and then arranged the wills under each letter chronologically and then started two books – one for the old wills and one for the current wills. . . .  There are 418 wills in Book A, so it must have taken quite a while to record this backlog.  How many had been lost??? (Stossen, p. 1).

Knowing this, it would indeed seem that the first three Clerks were certainly negligent in failing to record Wills properly, as Isham C. Browder had suggested (something, again, which would likely have been unknown to Talitha Browder).  And even when an effort was finally made, some Wills were never recorded in the will books at all, but, instead, remained in boxes for a long time or became lost.  We are indeed fortunate that the 1779 Will of Thomas Isham Browder survived at all.  And it survived only because Talitha Browder kept it.  She didn’t hide important papers; she preserved them.  

So, the 1778 Will might have been recorded, sometime by or after 1816, and that is obviously the Will that had been annexed and utilized by Richard Browder and William J. Meador.  And that would have been the Will that Isham C. Browder would have presented to the Court, had he lived long enough to do so.  Augustine would have known about this will too, and Isham stated that Augustine had written on it, or added to it.  But, it seems we will never really know, since it appears to no longer be extant.  That the 1779 will would obviously have superceded the 1778 will makes perfect sense.  But if the 1778 will had stipulated that Isham C. Browder was to receive all of Capt. Isham Browder’s real estate, regardless of acreage (because he only had about 150 acres at that time), then that might have allowed for a different decision to have been made by the court. 

One other thing is certain; not very long following the death of Talitha Browder, the descendants of Isham and Talitha finally began to go their separate ways.  The main groupings, the descendants of Isham C. Browder, and the descendants of Augustine Claiborne Browder, went to Tennessee and Georgia respectively (some having already done so).  The Whittemores remained in Rockingham County.  All others had already migrated to Georgia.  It is likely that they never again made any effort to contact one another, as there was much ill feeling now between the family groupings over the lawsuit concerning the Will of Thomas Isham Browder.  This would, perhaps, be the saddest part of our family’s legacy. 

The known children of Isham C. and Martha Browder were Mary Thompson “Polly” (1800-c1844), Isham Richard (c1801-1885) and an unknown male who did not live past young adulthood (b. c1804). 

Mary Thompson “Polly” Browder (1800-c1844) married William J. Meador on December 24, 1823 (Rockingham County Marriage Bonds Book [RCMBB] ?, p. 172/Ingmire, p. 46).  Clement Whittemore, Jr. stood as bondsman, and the ceremony was witnessed by Robert Galloway, Jr. 

Again, Isham C. Browder sold several tracts of land to both William J. Meador, and his son, Richard Browder, thus dividing his land between them.  However, Richard received the bulk of Isham’s land in this way, as he was deeded more of it by his father.  

By 1837 William J. Meador himself had sold 229 acres of land to Luke Palmer.  Thereafter he and his wife, Mary, moved to Itawamba County, Mississippi.  It is obvious that they had moved prior to 1840, since they are not found in that census of Rockingham County.  Where they were in 1830 is not known as they are found in no census.  But the census taker of Rockingham County obviously missed several families that year for some reason, so they were presumably still there and probably taking care of her aging father, Isham C. Browder, who is also not listed in any census for that year. 

By 1860 the family is known to have moved on to Cherokee County, Texas, but without Mary, because she died in Itawamba County, Mississippi about 1844.  William J. Meador married (2) Narcissa Allen on January 16, 1845 in Itawamba County, Mississippi.

The 1850 census of Itawamba County, Mississippi (District 7) lists William J. Meador (50), farmer, along with his wife, Narcissa (37) and children, Thomas (23), Martha A. (19), Mary F. (17), William (15), Elizabeth (11), Zachariah (4), Jesse (2, m), and Lila (1, f).  The family’s real estate value in 1850 was $1,300.  William is listed as having been born in Virginia.  Narcissa is listed as having been born in Tennessee.  Thomas, Martha, Mary, and William are listed as having been born in North Carolina.  These would have been children of William Meador and Mary Thompson Browder.  Thus, the known children of William and Mary Meador were Thomas (b. c1827), Martha A. (b. c1831), Mary F. (b. c1833), and William (b. c1835).  The rest of the children are listed as having been born in Mississippi.  Elizabeth (b. c1839) would also have had to have been a daughter of William and Mary Meador.  So, Mary would have died sometime between 1840 and 1844.  The rest of these listed children would have been those of William and Narcissa.  No other members of the Browder family, or any other known family members, are listed as residing close to this family in 1850.

The 1860 census of Cherokee County, Texas (Beat 8) also lists Wm. J. Medor (60), along with his wife, Narcissa (48), and son, Monroe (8), as well as Hannah O’Neil (21), and Wm. J. Webb (8).  This census confirms that William Meador was born in Virginia and that Narcissa was born in Tennessee.  All other household members were born in Texas.

So, the known children of William and Narcissa Meador would have been Zachariah (b. c1846), Lila (b. c1848), Jesse (b. 1850), and Monroe (b. c1852).
