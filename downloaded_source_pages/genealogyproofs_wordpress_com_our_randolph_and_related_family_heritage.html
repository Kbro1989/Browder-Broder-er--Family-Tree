Our Randolph and Related Family Heritage

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Dec. 28, 2025)

John Randoll I was born between 1470 and 1475 at Lewis, County Sussex, England and died in 1552, probably in the same area since he also resided at Lewis, County Sussex, England.  It is unknown who he married.  He left a Will dated January 24, 1552, which was proven on November 8, 1552.  So, we know that John died between January and November of 1552.  His known children were Anthony (d. c1555), William, Isabel, Robert, Thomas, and John II.

John Randoll II, son of John Randoll I, was born in 1505 at Lewis, County Sussex, England.  He married Johanna “Johan” Webbe, daughter of William Webbe (b. 1482, Hamsey, County Sussex, England).  Johan Webbe was born in 1508 at Hamsey, County Sussex, England.  John and Johan Randoll resided at Lewis, County Sussex, England where they both died.  John Randoll II died about 1560 and left a Will, dated November 27, 1560.  He died in1577.

The known children of John and Johan Randall were Robert, John, Edward, Johan, Mary, Agnes, Sara, Susan, Rachel, Elizabeth, and Alice. 

Robert Randoll, son of John and Johan Randoll, was born in 1550 at Hamsey, County Sussex, England.  He married Rosa “Rose” Roberts, daughter of Thomas Roberts (b. c1525, Hawkhurst, County Kent, England) at Northamptonshire, England.  Rosa Roberts was born in 1554 in Hawkhurst, Biddenden, County Kent, England. 

Robert and Rosa Randoll resided at Hams (Hamsey) near Lewis, County Sussex, England.  Robert died in June of 1602 and was buried on June 8, 1602 at Hamsey, County Sussex, England.  The known children of Robert and Rosa Randall were William I (1572-1670), George, John, Anny S., and Margaret.

William Randolph I, son of Robert and Rosa Randoll, married (1) Elizabeth Smyth, daughter of Thomas Smyth, Gent. of Newnham by Daventre County, Northampton, England, and later (2) Dorothy Lane, daughter of Richard Lane and Elizabeth Vincent, and widow of Thomas West “Third Baron De La Warr ” (Jul. 9, 1577-June 7, 1618), who died at sea aboard the Neptune returning from England, on March 30, 1619, at Little Haughton, Northamptonshire, England.  Obviously, Dorothy had remained behind.

William was born in 1572 at Biddenden, County Kent, England, and Dorothy was born in August of 1589 at Courtney, Bichester, Oxfordshire, England.  They resided at Little Houghton Parva, County Northumberland, England.  Dorothy was christened on September 4, 1589 at Courteen Hall, Bichester, Oxfordshire, England.  She had previously married (1) Thomas West on January 16, 1609/10. 

William Randolph I died in 1670, and Dorothy Lane-Randolph died on July 16, 1656.  They both died and are buried at Moreton Morrell, Warwickshire, England. 

The known children of William Randolph I and Elizabeth Smyth were Thomas (June 15, 1605-March 17, 1634/35), Elizabeth (b. 1613), William II (b. 1607), and Robert (d. 1671). 

The known children of William Randolph I and Dorothy Lane were John (d. 1680), Richard I (Feb. 21, 1621-May 2, 1678), Capt. Henry I (Nov. 27, 1623-1673), Anne, George (1627-June 6, 1645), Margaret, and Judith (b. 1630, Hams, Sussex, England). 

Richard Lane (b. 1544, Courtenhall, Northamptonshire, England) married Elizabeth Vincent (b. c1547, Harpole, Northamptonshire, England) about 1570 at Harpole, Northamptonshire, England.  Richard Lane was the son of Thomas Lane (c1520-1589) and Katherine Trentham (b. 1520, Northamptonshire, England), and the grandson of John Lane (c1500-1577) and Katherine Patrick (b. c1503, Raunton, England).  John Lane and Katherine Patrick were married about 1520 at King’s Bromley, Northamptonshire, England.

Elizabeth Vincent was born about 1547.  She was the daughter of Clement Vincent (b. c1514, Peckleton, Leicestershire, England) and Anne Tanfield (b. c1516, Grayton, Northamptonshire, England).  Clement Vincent and Anne Tanfield were married about 1539 at Grayton, Northamptonshire, England.

Anne Tanfield was the daughter of Francis Tanfield (c1489, Grayton, Northamptonshire, England-1588) and Bridget Cave (b. c1491, Grayton, Northamptonshire, England).  Francis Tanfield and Bridget Cave were married about 1514 at Grayton, Northamptonshire, England.

Clement Vincent was the son of George Vincent (b. 1485, Peckleton, Leicestershire, England) and Anne Slorey (b. c1489, Sliford, England).  George Vincent and Anne Slorey were married about 1508 at Sliford, Leicestershire, England.

Anne Slorey was the daughter of William Slorey (b. c1462, Sliford, Leicestershire, England).

George Vincent was the son of Richard Vincent (c1425, Bernacle, Northumberland, England-after 1486) and Anne Grimesby (b. c1461, Drakelow, England).  Richard Vincent and Anne Grimesby were married about 1485 at Drakelow, England.

Cpt. Henry Randolph I was a merchant who emigrated from England to James Fort “Jamestown”, Virginia in 1635.  He was the first of the Randolph family to emigrate to Virginia.  He married (1) Elizabeth. Due to the preponderance of the evidence, I am now convinced that Henry’s first wife was Elizabeth Worsham, sister of William, George, and Martha Worsham. William Worsham married Elizabeth Littlebury, who married Francis Epes II following William’s death. George Worsham married Mary Jordan, daughter of Capt. Samuel Jordan I and Cicely Reynolds. Martha Worsham married Thomas Shippey, Jr.

After the death of Elizabeth, Henry married (2) Judith Soane (who married Peter Field in 1678 following the death of Henry), daughter of Col. Henry Soane.  Henry Soane was the Speaker of the House of Burgesses.  Henry Randolph I prospered in Virginia and in 1659 he was named clerk of the House of Burgesses, thus becoming a member of the aristocracy of Tidewater, Virginia.

In 1669 Capt. Henry Randolph I returned to England for a visit.  While there he so impressed his nephew, William Randolph I, with his tales of the Americas, that William emigrated to Virginia a few years later, in 1673, and succeeded Henry Randolph I as Clerk of the House of Burgesses, and, trained as an attorney, eventually became Attorney General of the Virginia colony. 

Capt. Henry Randolph I and Judith Soane had several children, including a son, Capt. Henry Randolph II (Jan. 16, 1665-Feb. 26, 1693) who married Sarah Swann (1672-1714) on December 17, 1687.  When he was only 18 he succeeded his cousin, William Randolph I, as Clerk of the House of Burgesses from Henrico County; again, the same office his father had held for many years.  Henry Randolph II, however, also died early, at the age of 28, and is buried beside his father in the Bristol Parish churchyard. 

The orphans of Cpt. Henry Randolph II are mentioned on the Quit Rent Rolls of Prince George County, Virginia in 1704, but not named.  The known children of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann were Henry III (Jan. 1, 1688-Aug. 1, 1726), William (c1690-c1726), and Thomas (Jul. 1, 1692-1693). 

On August 20, 1707, Capt. John Worsham and Capt. Joseph Royall entered themselves as securities for the estate belonging to the orphans of Capt. Henry Randolph, decd. in the stead of Maj. Peter Field, dec., and accordingly gave bond (Orphans Court Order, p. 48). 

Henry Randolph III married Elizabeth Epes (1690-1777), daughter of Francis Epes III and Anne Elizabeth Isham, on March 29, 1714 in Henrico County, Virginia.  On November 5, 1723, Henry Randolph III purchased 1,200 acres of land on Deep Creek, south of the Appomattox River.  And, on April 1, 1725 Henry Randolph III “of Henrico County” purchased an additional 1,053 acres of land on the lower side of the Beaver Dam Branch of Deep Creek. 

The known children of Henry Randolph III and Elizabeth Epes were Sarah (October 10, 1715), Anne (December 31, 1717), Henry IV (February 11, 1721), Francis (June 19, 1724), and the twins, Grief I (March 1727-March 1778), and Mourning (March 1727).  All children were born in Henrico (now Chesterfield) County, Virginia. 

See, as the story goes, Henry Randolph III also died early, during Elizabeth’s fifth pregnancy, on August 1, 1726, at age 37, just weeks prior to the birth of twins.  The twins were a boy and a girl.  Elizabeth expressed her sorrow by naming the boy “Grief” and the girl “Mourning”. 

In any case, at Henry’s death, Elizabeth received his considerable property and, against societal precedent, refused to remarry.  She died in 1777.  Only two of the sons reached maturity, Henry IV and Grief I. 

Grief Randolph I (1726-March 1778), son of Henry Randolph III and Elizabeth Eppes, died the year after his own mother died.  He had married Mary Epes.  Their children were Henry V (January 15/18, 1757-October 1, 1832), John (c1759-c1800), William B. (b. 1754), Isham, Elizabeth Wormley, Anne, Marie, Edmund, and Francis. 

John Randolph I of Warren County, North Carolina, married Mary and had a son named Grief (b. c1789).  He and family journeyed on to Wilson County, Tennessee by 1810, where they settled.  He is believed to have served in the Virginia Continental Line during the American Revolutionary War.  

John Randolph II (c1770-April 13, 1814/15), son of John Randolph I and Mary (d. August 20, 1820), married (1) Sarah Whittington (b. c1760) in Greensville County, Virginia, in December of 1785, and later (2) Mary G. Whittington (1766-October 6, 1810), on March 6, 1794, and perhaps much later also, (3) Rebecca Martin, a widow.  This Randolph family resided in North Carolina and were shoemakers by trade. 

The John Randolph II family moved to Lebanon, Wilson County, Tennessee by 1810.  John purchased a lot in Lebanon, on September 26, 1810, from Joseph Martin (WCDB D, p. 489).  Soon after he made this purchase, his wife, Mary, died.  Thereafter the children are believed to have been cared for by their grandmother, Mary.  John sold his lot to his brother, Grief Randolph (b. c1789), on December 13, 1810 (WCDB D, p. 489), and sold still more land to him in 1812.  Grief Randolph in turn sold this original lot in Lebanon to Harry Douglas on November 16, 1811 (WCDB D, p. 490). 

The child of John Randolph II and Sarah Whittington was Elisha (b. c1785).  The children of John Randolph II and Mary G. Whittington were Grief (b. January 11, 1795), Lavinia/Levinia W. (m. John B. Holman, September 19, 1828), Peyton W. (b. July 6, 1797), Rebecca, John Green (November 11, 1799-February 24, 1880), Beverly, Harrison, and George. 

Stricken by sadness concerning the death of his second wife, Mary, John Randolph II had enlisted in the Army soon afterward, serving under Gen. Andrew Jackson during the War of 1812 as a mounted spy, and later during the Indian Wars.  His sons, Grief (named after John’s brother), and Peyton, enlisted along with him.  Peyton Randolph was captured during the War of 1812 by the British and taken to Quebec as a prisoner, never to be heard from again.  Once John Green Randolph heard about his brother’s capture, he also enlisted in October of 1813, at the age of 13.  It is said that John G. Randolph was too young (and probably too small) to carry his musket while drilling and marching, but he was allowed to serve anyway. 

John Randolph II died toward the end of the war, seven days after the Battle of New Orleans, in 1815.  He was at Andrew Jackson’s camp near Citronelle, Mobile County, Alabama, Mississippi Territory, where he died of dysentery.

John Green Randolph served in the U.S. Army from 1813 to about 1820, during the War of 1812, and for five additional years as a regular.  After the war, John returned home to Wilson County.  There he remained for a few years.  He relocated to Madison County, Tennessee by 1827.  

John Green Randolph later married Nancy Gayle Wynne near Jackson, Tennessee.  He is listed in both the 1830 and 1840 censuses of Madison County, and in the 1838 school census of Madison County.  He later relocated to Montezuma in McNairy County, Tennessee between 1840 and 1845 (Wright). 

On July 9, 1827, John G. Randolph sold 323 acres of land, located on Hurricane Creek in Wilson County, Tennessee, to John Bond [Jr.].  This tract was bounded by the property of Benjamin Castleman (Madison County Deed Book [MCDB] M, p. 93).  On July 19, 1827 John Bond [Jr.] of Wilson County sold a tract of 468½ acres of land on the Forked Deer River, Madison County, to John G. Randolph of Wilson County for $1,200. (MCDB 2, p. 295).  Thus, they traded properties with one another. 

This Randolph family made their living by farming and were vast land holders, and among the most prominent families in the area at that time.  John G. Randolph owned sixteen slaves by 1850.  That year his real estate value was listed as $20,000., and the census confirms that he was from North Carolina.  

John G. Randolph and family always resided in the 3rd or 4th Civil District once the family moved to McNairy County.  This district included the town of Montezuma.  However, he owned land in several other areas along the border between Madison and McNairy counties and elsewhere, as detailed in my book “Faces of Our Ancestors”.  But, when and how John G. Randolph acquired the 640 acre tract of land that he later sold to Richard Browder is a mystery, since it is clearly not mentioned in any of the deeds or grants that I have found. 

According to the McNairy County tax list of 1867, John G. Randolph held properties in districts 3, 4, 5, 7 and 14.  In that same year Richard Browder is shown on the same tax lists as holding land in districts 6 and 13.  Some of the land in District 6 went to his son, John, although, again, the deed records are missing.  The reason we know this is because John is always listed as residing in District 6 after he moved out of his parent’s home and Richard states in his Will, that John resided on lands that Richard had given him.

Once in McNairy County, Richard Browder performed several real estate transactions, including the one on January 24, 1848 (proven on the 28th) in which he bought 640 acres of land, located on Oxford Creek, for $1,500. from John G. Randolph, which was located in Range 4, Section 2 of the county.  This tract of land was the entry #1071, which had originally been made “in the name of the trustees of the University of North Carolina.” 

Back to our own Randolph family line – Richard Randolph I, son of William Randolph I and Dorothy Lane, was born on February 21, 1621, and was christened on February 24, 1621 at Little Houghton (or Houghton Parva), Northumberland, England.  He married Elizabeth Riland/Ryland, daughter of Thoms Riland/Ryland at Yorkshire, England, prior to 1650.  Elizabeth was born in 1625, also at Yorkshire, England.  Richard and Elizabeth Randolph resided at Moreton Morrell, Warwickshire, England.  Richard died on May 2, 1678 at Dublin, Ireland.  It is unknown why he was there, but he had likely been on a business trip. 

The known children of Richard Randolph I and Elizabeth Riland/Ryland were Richard II, Dorothy (b. March 1647), Mary (b. 1648), Col. William I (November 7, 1650-April 11, 1711)), Thomas (b. February 1651/52), John (b. July 1653), Elizabeth (b. December 8, 1655), and Margaret (b. February 25, 1656/57). 

Col. William Randolph I, son of Richard Randolph I and Elizabeth Riland/Ryland (and the afore mentioned nephew of Henry Randolph I), married Mary Isham, “the much sought after” daughter of Capt. Henry Isham I and Katherine Banks of Bermuda Hundred, on November 13, 1680, in Henrico County, Virginia.  Also, it has been stated that William and Mary Randolph have been referred to as the “Adam and Eve of Virginia”, because they had so many important descendants. 

Col. William Randolph I was born on November 7, 1650 at Moreton Morrell, Warwickshire, England, and was christened on November 7, 1651 at same.  William worked as a carpenter’s apprentice while in Yorkshire, England, where he resided.  He later sailed from England to Virginia in 1660.  Mary Isham was born in 1659, and died on December 29, 1735, at about 76 years of age. 

Through the Will of her uncle, Sir Edward Brett, Mary and her sister, Ann, inherited a valuable estate in England.  Their brother, Henry Isham II, died in 1678, willing them an inheritance of the balance of this estate, as well as his own.  Included in this estate, for Mary, was her mother’s wedding ring and her best feather bed, along with the furniture.  Further, Mary was exempted from any debts or expenses.  To his half-brother, Joseph Royal, Henry willed £40 in goods.  To his “honorable mother, Mrs. Katherine Isham”, Henry willed 1/3 of his personal effects, both in Virginia and England.  To his “loving sister Anne Isham”, he also willed 1/3 part of his estate.  He willed his plantation, “commonly known by name of Doggams”, to be equally divided by his two sisters “Mrs. Mary Randolph and Mrs. Anne Isham”.  And, to “Mr. William Randolph”, the rest of his estate in Virginia and England, also naming him as Executor.  Witnesses were Ja. Tubb, John Wynn, Wilbert Daniel, and Hugh Davis.  It was proven on February 1, 1678 (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Wills and Administrations, Part I, 1677-1692, Will and Deed Book p. 71). 

On April 2, 1678, William Randolph patented to William Robins, planter, 591 acres on north side of the Appomattox River, and the north side of Swift Creek (written on the back of a patent for same).  This patent was recorded on October 1, 1674.  Witnesses were Richard Cock, Sr. and Thos. Charles.  Mary, his wife, relinquished her dower rights to said property wit: Anthony Tall and Thos. Charles; Mary, wife of Randolph, relinquishes her dower rights (Wills, Deeds, etc. 1677-1692, p. 37).

Col. William Randolph I, no doubt, met Mary Isham through the agency of his uncle, Henry Randolph I, who had extensive contacts, and who was a friend of Capt. Henry Isham I.  Upon arriving in Virginia, the family of Henry Randolph I became like William’s own.  When Henry Randolph I died, his widow remarried, and from that point on William looked after his younger cousin, Henry Randolph II.  No doubt William, in this way, repaid the debt to Henry Randolph I, and was instrumental in the rise of Henry Randolph II. 

The Isham family was also like a surrogate family for William, both before, and especially after, he married.  He held money in trust for his widowed mother-in-law.  At the death of Henry Isham II in 1678, William inherited property in England and Virginia, and was the sole Executor of the estate. 

In the 1680s, his brother-in-law, Francis Epes IV, became a partner with him in property and mercantile transactions.  When Joseph Royall IV, half-brother of his wife, became Henrico County Justice in 1692, William Randolph I, as a member of the County Court, doubtless approved his appointment (Cowden’s Massive Randolph Thesis, 1977). 

Mary, a woman of means, was capable of handling financial affairs on her own as when, at one point, she played the part of intercessor and asked William Bird II to intervene between her husband and an English creditor, Michajah Perry.  It is also of interest to note that Mary played the cittern, an instrument common among Irish musicians (Randolph, p. 23).

William and Mary Randolph settled on Turkey Island, Virginia, about thirty miles north of Jamestown, in 1674, establishing a plantation there.  William became the clerk of the House of Burgesses for Henrico County.  He also served as Speaker of the House in 1698 and as a member of the Council of Virginia for Henrico County.  He practiced law and served as Virginia Attorney General in 1696 and 1697.  He also served as a Justice of the Peace as well as the Henrico County Coroner and Escheator General for all lands on the south side of the James River.  And finally, William also served as Trustee for Henrico County.

Both William and Mary Randolph were members of the established Church of England within Henrico Parish, Virginia.  William served in the Colonial Militia as Captain, eventually rising to the rank of Colonel.  Finally, William also helped found the College of William and Mary in Virginia in 1693 and became a Trustee of said college.

At Turkey Island, William built “an imposing mansion of brick imported from England.”  However, only ruins now remain of this mansion, as it was burned during the American Civil War.  Eventually, William Randolph I owned as much as 10,000 acres of land there.

By 1691, Col. William Randolph I owned all of the 1,012 acres of Turkey Island, Virginia.  He also established a line of ships which regularly sailed between Bristol, England and Virginia.  It is known that, in 1687, he brought 36 persons from England to Virginia; in 1691, eight persons; and in 1697, eleven persons.  It is said that “. . . his worldly fortunes kept full pace with his progeny, all of whom he settled around him on fine estates, and left rich.” (Brock, p. 58).  It is entirely possible that early members of the Browder family arrived aboard one of the ships owned by William Randolph. 

Col. William Randolph I died on April 11, 1711, and his wife, Mary, died on December 29, 1735, both at Turkey Island, Virginia.  Her headstone reads “Here Lies Mary Isham Randolph. Wife of Col. William Randolf. 1659-1735.” 

Col. William Randolph I writes his will on March 6, 1711. The will stipulated several things, including that his widow, Mary, would retain the 400 acre plantation, part of the 1,000 acre tract called “Turkey Island”, and after her death it would go to their son, William. She would also retain certain other lands not otherwise disposed of. Sons Thomas and Isham were to equally divide a certain 750 acres, and Thomas was to have his choice of this tract. They, along with their other brothers were also to equally divide another tract of 3,256 acres.

From The Valentine Papers, Vol 1-4, 1864-1908  Randolph Family Henrico County Records (Packages of Original Papers) Will of William Randolph, of “Turkey Island”.

“In the name of God Amen: I William Randolph of Turkey Island in the County and parish of Henrico on James River in Virga. Gent. being now in the sixty’th year of my age and at this time (I thank the Almighty God) in health and of a sound pfect and disposing memory and considering the frailty of mankinde and that nothing is more certaine than death nor nothing more uncertain then the time of it, I have thought fit in prudence to make this my last will and Testament and doe revoke annull and make void all wills by me heretofore made, and hereby make ordaine publish and declare these prsents to be my last will and Testament in Manner and forme following.

 First: I Comit my Soule into the proteton [protection] of my Almighty Omnipotent and great Creator wth a steadfast faith and an assured hope through his mercies and the Merritts of his Son, my blessed (and all sufficient) Lord, Savior and redemer Jesus Christ to have pardon and remission of all my (Manifold) Sins and transgressions and to receive a joyfull resurrection and inherit eternall Salvation and plenty in his heavenly glorious and everlasting Kingdom and my body to be decently buried at the discretion of my Exors. hereafter named.

Next as to what temporall effects it hath or shall please God to bestow upon me in this life I dispose of as followeth. I give and divise to my dear and loving wife Mary Randolph during her Naturall life the plantation I now live on being four hundred and odd acres part of the thousand acres called Turkey Island wth all the houses Orchards and appurtenances whatsoever to the same belonging and doe not oblige her to keep anything in repair but leave it wholly to her discretion. After her death I give and devise the sd. plantation and all the houses and appurtenances belonging to the same to my son William Randolph and his heirs for ever. I give and divise to my said dear and loving wife Mary Randolph the third part of all my land houseing &c. that I have with in this County of Henrico or else where in Virga. during her naturall life Except the third of my land on pigeon Swamp in Surry County wch. I have disposed off other ways in this my will. I give and divise to my said dear and loving wife Mary Randolph and to her heirs forever my share or part of Martins Swamp and the acre of ground in ye towne at Burmoder [Bermuda] hundered wch. I have entered for hoping that she will give her land in the Neck wth the Sd. part of the Swamp & the acre of ground in the towne to one of my sons if he should live upon the sd. acre of ground in the towne aforesaid. I give and devise all my land wth all priviledges included in my pattent of Curles or the conveyance of Curles land and in the same called the Slashes lying on both sides the popler brook, to my Sons Isham and Thomas Randolph and each of their heirs for ever to be equally divided between them, the said tract containing by estimation about seven hundred and fifty acres and in regard my son Isham in probability will not settle in Virginia I give to my son Thomas his choice of the sd. land after the division and my desire to both my said sons is, if either of them should sell their part of the said land that they would sell it to the other. I give and divise to my sons Richard and Hon. John Randolph all my three tracts of land wth all houses and appurtenances to the same belonging wch I purchased of John Woodson, Samuel Knibb and John Morton wth sd. three tracts Joyne together upon Chickaming Swamp and containe by estimation aboute nine hundred acres to be equally divided between them and each of their heirs for ever, and to my Son Richard I divise the upper part of the said three tracts of land, and to my son John the lower part of the same after the Division, and my desire to both my sd. sons is, if either of them should sell their part of the sd. land that they would sell it to the other. I give and divise to  my son Edward Randolph and his heirs for ever, all that tract of land lying upon Chickahoming Swamp between the land now belonging to Mr. James Cocke and the upper side thereof and the land of Joseph Watson on the lower side thereof being six hundred twenty-five acres wch I bought of Mr. Thomas Cocke, decd. wth all priviledges and appurtenances whatsoever to the same belonging. I give and divise all my land wch lyes between the land given to my son William about Westham Upper Creek and Tuckahoe Creek being part of three thousand two hundred fifty-six acres I purchased of Edmond Jennings Esqr. to be equally divided between my five sons, Isham, Thomas, Richard, John and Edward, my son Isham to have the lower part adjoyneing to my son William, my son Thomas to joyne upon Isham, my son Richard to joyne upon Thomas, my son John to joyne upon Richard and my son Edward to joyne upon John being the lower part of the sd. land each of wch parcels of land to be divided as aforesd. I give and divise to each of my said sons and each of their heirs for ever wth all appurtenances to the same belonging or appertaining. I give and divise to my son Isham Randolph and his heirs forever the Upper halfe of my lower Island lying above Westham Upon James River on the North side thereof (the other halfe I have already given to my son William) with all appurtenances to the same belonging. I give and divise my upper Island lying on James River as aforesd to be equally divided between my three sons, Thomas, Richard and John, Thomas to have the lower part, Richard the middle part, and John the upper part each of my said sons to have each of their parts of the sd. Island wth all appurtenances to the same belonging to each of them and each of their heirs forever. I give and divise my land upon the North side of James River wch lyes above Tuckahoe Creek, and is part of my pattent wch I have for the Island with all appurtenances to the same belonging unto my son Edward Randolph and his heirs forever. I doe hereby allot and appoint my land and plantaiton at pigeon Swamp in Surry County being by estimation between a thousand and eleven hundred acres with stock of cattle and hogs upon and belong to the same and these ten Negroes, vizt; Black Jack, Eustham, Serro, David, Mingo, Yamon (Janson? ) Moll, Besse, Condyand Hannah to be set apart for the use of Mr. Micajah Perry and Company, Merchants in London, and their Crops of Tobaccoe and other profit that may arise by the said stock to be set to them from time to time to Satisfie what I may Justly owe them, untill the same be fully paid and I desire my sons Henry and Thomas Randolph to take care and provide the same be Shipt and assigned to them th Said Mr. Perry and Company or their assigns provided they allow as much for the tobaccoe as others do for such tobaccoe, and to encourage my sd sons or the Survivor of them to see the tobaccoe well handled and other things managed to the best advantage, that the said debt may be paid as soon as possible. I give and order them a share of all the tobaccoe and corne that they shall make wth out an Overseer, and give them Liberty if they think fit to work all or any of the sd. Negroes upon their owne plantation, and above for their trouble in disposing of the profitts of the stock and receiving the produce and sending it to Mr. Perry and Company as aforesd I give and appoint them to take to their owne use or uses ten pct. out of the sd. produce of the stock and other profitts Arising in the Country and to allow and pay themselves for the Negroes Clothes, leveys, Cask or such Usuell charges as for the Negroes provisions they are to have what is fiting for them out of the sd. Stocke and when the sd. debt is paid I give, bequeath and divise the sd. land with houses and all appurtenances, and alsoe the Negroes and all the Stocke aforesd to be equally divided between my two sons their heirs for ever, or the survivor of them, if either of them should dye before the sd. debt is paid, and if my said  sons should refuse to take this trouble upon them, then I appoint one of them and if he refuses I appoint either of my other sons that will take the care and prforme, what I have here desired and in consideration thereof I give to him that shall – undertake the trouble as aforesd the same allowances and also give and divise to him and his heirs forever all the land houses and appurtenances and the sd. ten Negroes and all the stock aforesd; and if non of my sons will undertake the management of the premises, then I order the same to be put to an Overseer, upon as cheap terms as can be agreed on; and when the sd. debt is paid I give and devise the sd. land and appurtenances, negroes and stock as aforesd to be equally divided amongst all my children and every of their heirs forever. I give and bequeath to my dear and loving wife Mary Randolph one bed and furniture wch. she shall make choice off, wth. four pieces of Holland Sheets two pillows one Rugg and one paire of Blankets all to be at her choice and alsoe all her wearing apparell Rings and Ornaments of her body and what ready money may be of mine in the house at the time of my death. I alsoe give and bequeath to my aforesd loving wife my negroe man named Ned, my Negroe woman named – and her children, vizt. Bowser, Nanny, Judy, Neddy and Ruth and the Indian girle Patty & to her heirs. I give and bequeath the use of all the rest of my negroes, goods and chattels of what kinds soever to my said dear and loving wife for the support of her selfe and family and to be disposed of according to her discretion, the advancement of such of my children as at time of my death she shall Judge not well Settled to live by themselves any part of the sd. negroes, goods and chattells to be given them as they come of age or at the time of my wife’s death as she shall think most convenient not doubting but she will be just to them all and therefore I appoint she shall not give herself the trouble nor the other of my Exors. to Inventory or appraise the est

On November 10, 1716, Mary Randolph, widow, along with Francis Epes III and his wife, Anne, deeded to Joseph Royall, Jr., for £30, 74 acres of land located on the south side of the James River, at the bottom of Bermuda Hundred, next to the swamp which was bounded by a patent to Henry Isham on September 24, 1661, next to Royall and Gurgaine’s landing.  Witnesses were Richard Randolph, John Ewert, John Wilkinson, Rene LaForce, Thomas Eldridge, and Will Kennon.  Signed by Mary Randolph, Francis Epes, Anne Epes.  Recored on June 3, 1717 (ibid; Deeds 1714-1718, p. 170). 

The children of Col William Randolph I and Mary Isham were Elizabeth (1680-Jan. 22, 1720), Col. William II (Nov. 6, 1681-1742), Lt. Col. Thomas “of Tuckahoe” (Jul. 10, 1683-1730), Col. Isham (Dec. 1684-Nov. 2, 1742), Henry (b. c1687), Mary (b. 1692, Yorkshire, England), Sir John “Johnny” (b. April 1693), Col. Richard (1690/95-1748), and Edward (c1695-p. 1756).  And one unnamed daughter is known to have died during birth. 

Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph, Gent., son of Col. William Randolph I and Mary Isham, was born on July 10, 1683 on Turkey Island, Henrico County, Virginia.  Thomas married the very wealthy Judith Fleming (Nov. 1689-1743), daughter of Charles Fleming (c1650-c1720; son of Sir John Fleming (c1633-c1686) of Scotland and Mary) and Susanna Tarleton (Oct. 11, 1661-Oct. 7, 1717, daughter of Stephen Tarleton (d. c1687) and Susannah Bates), on October 16, 1712 in St. Peter’s Parish, New Kent County, Virginia.  Judith was born in St. Peter’s Parish, New Kent County, Virginia.

Some posit Judith’s middle name as having been Churchill or that, perhaps, the Fleming estate may have been called “Church Hill.”  Neither, however, is correct. 

The Fleming family were devout Quakers.  It is theorized by some that Thomas Randolph may have married Judith Fleming in a Quaker ceremony prior to the date of their “official” marriage, which would have been performed because Quaker marriages were not seen as legally binding in those days, and any children born from such a union were considered illegitimate.  This speculation is because both were officially married relatively late in life.  It is also possible that Thomas had been married to someone else prior to his marriage to Judith.  However, no evidence for either has been found, to date. 

It is possible that they could have had a child, or children, prior to their “official” marriage, but this is also speculation.  If they did, the names of said children are unknown.  Not only that; Thomas’ father, William Randolph I, had been involved in the persecution of Quakers, thus providing another reason to further legitimize their union. 

Thomas and Judith Randolph resided at Tuckahoe, Henrico County, Virginia, where they established “one of the James River’s most famous plantations.”  It was far removed from most of the other Randolph families (about 15 miles above the falls of the James River) but, since it was on the James River, it could be easily accessed, since river travel was the most common mode of transportation at that time.  The name “Tuckahoe” came from a Native American name for the creek flowing through the property.  It is also known that Thomas Randolph owned “several notable horses” which were used for breeding and racing. 

At court, in April of 1724, Thomas Randolph deeded land to Robert Carter, which Thomas’ wife, Judith, relinquished her dower rights to (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Court Order Book 1719-1224, p. 332). 

From the following passage we can see that Judith was a medicine woman.  Concerning Judith, William Byrd in his book, A Progress to the Mines, (p. 126), during a visit to their plantation on September 22, 1732, wrote thus:

“At length I discovered SHE was a notable quack, and therefore paid that regard to HER knowledge, as to put some questions to HER about the bad distemper that raged then in the country. . . SHE told me SHE made use of very simple remedies in that case, with very good success.  SHE did the business either with hartshorn drink that had plantain leaves boiled in it, or else with a strong decoction of St. Andrew’s cross, in new milk instead of water. . . .  But for fear this conversation might be too grave for a widow, I turned the discourse, and began to talk of plays, and finding HER taste lay most towards comedy, I offered my service to read one to HER, which SHE kindly accepted.”

Thomas Randolph served as a member of the Quorum Court of Henrico County, Virginia in 1702 as Presiding Justice.  In March of 1712, Thomas Randolph became overseer of all of William Byrd’s plantations.  He was also appointed Deputy Sheriff, and served as a Justice of the Peace, and as a Coroner.  Thomas also served as Captain of a Company of the Virginia Militia, rising to the rank of Lieutenant Colonel.  And Thomas also owned a mill, which was located on his property.  Finally, he also served as Vestryman of St. James Parish. 

On June 3, 1722 (p. 89), Thomas Randolph purchased 670 acres of land in Henrico County, Virginia, located on the south side of the James River.  This was land which had first been granted to Charles Fleming on June 16, 1714, upon condition that it be cultivated. 

In 1728, Thomas Randolph was deeded 322 acres of land in St. James Parish by Nowel Burton (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Wills and Deeds 1728-1734, p. 45).  On August 19, 1729, Thomas was deeded an additional 150 acres of land by Bouth Napier (ibid, p. 115).  Later, referring to the deed executed in 1728, on August 14, 1730, the Executors of the Will of Thomas Randolph, deceased, acknowledged that, for £123.10.10 for the security of that mortgage, had been paid the deceased within his lifetime, and to his Executrix since his decease; signed by William Randolph, John Fleming, Richard Randolph; acknowledged by John Fleming and on motion of Noel Burton was recorded (ibid, p. 219). 

Thomas Randolph died in 1730, obviously prior to August 14th, at Tuckahoe in Goochland County, Virginia.  At the time of his death, Thomas Randolph was the presiding Justice.  Judith died about 1743, and is supposedly buried in the Randolph family cemetery at Tuckahoe. 

The known children of Thomas and Judith Randolph were Col. William (1713-1745), Mary Isham (c1716 – Dec. 1772), and Judith (b. c1718). 

No Will has been found for Thomas Randolph.  Most likely, since Thomas was relatively young when he died, he simply did not see the need to write a Will. 

Judith remarried after the death of her husband, Thomas Randolph, to (2) Nicholas Davies (or Davis) of Goochland County, Virginia, on December 19, 1733 (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Goochland County Marriage Book, p. 1).  Judith left the Thomas Randolph plantation to reside with Nicholas at a new Goochland County settlement, and also later died in Goochland County, Virginia, but is said to be buried at Tuckahoe. 

William Randolph of Tuckahoe, in his Will, dated July 20, 1745 and recorded May 20, 1746, directed that monuments be put up to the memory of “Thomas Randolph, my ever-honored father” (not even referring to his own mother), as well as for the mother of his wife, Mary (ibid; Deed Book 5, 1745-1749, pp. 73).  His Will also mentions his beloved sister, Judith, but does not refer or allude to his sister, Mary, in any way.  His most prominent son was Thomas Mann Randolph. 

Mary Isham Randolph, daughter of Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph and Judith Fleming, was born at Mansfield, Louisa County, Virginia, and died at Bristerberg, Fauquier County, Virginia. 

Interestingly, for our purposes, Judith Randolph, daughter of Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph and Judith Fleming, married Rev. William Stith (c1707-1755), son of John Stith II and Mary Randolph, and President of the College of William and Mary, Virginia on May 17, 1738 (Goochland County Marriage Book, p. 263). 

In any case, the Fleming family is of Flemish origin, having settled in Scotland during the reign of King David I of Scotland.  The original settler is said to have been a man of “high rank.”  The family can be traced to a Sir Malcolm Fleming, Sheriff of Dumbarton during the reign of Alexander III. 

Sir. John Fleming, the progenitor of the Virginia Flemings, was, then, a descendant of Sir Malcolm Fleming.  John was the second son of Alexander Fleming (c1612-c1668), son of Lord John Fleming VI, 2nd Earl of Wigton, 7th Lord of Fleming, Scotland (Dec. 1589-1650) and Lady Margaret Livingston.  Alexander Fleming’s wife was named Elizabeth. Sir John Fleming’s wife was named Mary.

While in Scotland the family became very prominent, producing many distinguished persons.  “They were friends of the Bruce, and favorites of successive kings, as well as it seems of the people of Scotland also, forming the most illustrious connections, and attaining finally to the rank and title of Earls of Wigton.” 

Charles Fleming, son of Sir John Fleming and Mary, was born in New Kent County, Virginia.  He married Susanna Tarleton.  The family resided at Mansfield in St. Peter’s Parish, New Kent County, Virginia.  This area was later made into Hanover County and still later into Louisa County.  The area is situated next to Goochland County.  Charles Fleming died about 1720 in Henrico County, Virginia. 

Susanna Tarleton was born on October 11, 1661 (or c1670) in New Kent County, Virginia.  She was the daughter of Stephen Tarleton (1637-1687, St. Peter’s Parish, New Kent County, Virginia) and Susanna Bates, daughter of John Bates (b. 1598, County Kent, England) and Elizabeth.  Stephen and Susanna Tarleton were married on June 15, 1680 in Henrico County, Virginia. 

Elk Island, on the James River, was once inhabited by Native Americans who used part of it as a burial ground.  It first became owned by Europeans when it was purchased by John Woodson and wife, Judith Tarleton.  It was apparently passed on to their son, Tarleton Woodson.  Tarleton Woodson married Ursula Fleming, sister of Judith Fleming.  It was later owned by Charles Fleming.  These individuals were all devout Quakers. 

On June 16, 1714 (p. 151) Charles Fleming purchased 732 acres of land in Henrico County, Virginia on the north side of the James River.  On June 16, 1714 (p. 164) Charles Fleming was granted another 670 acres of land in Henrico County, Virginia, located on the south side of the James River.  And, on June 16, 1714 (p. 166), Charles Fleming and John Woodson purchased 1,278 acres of land in Henrico County on the north side of the James River.  This land was adjacent to Elk Island. 

The known children of Charles and Susanna Fleming were Elizabeth (b. October 28, 1682), Urselah (b. 1684), Robert (b. c1688), Sara (b. c1689), Judith (b. November 1689), Grace (b. c1691), Susannah (b. 1693), John (May or November 23, 1697-November 6/7, 1766), Tarleton (b. c1699), and Anne (b. January 21, 1712/13). 

Also of interest for our purposes, to show more family ties, Col. John Fleming, son of Charles and Susanna Fleming, resided at Mt. Pleasant, Goochland County, Virginia where he died.  He was a member of the House of Burgesses and Virginia Treasurer.  He married Mary Bolling (b. 1711), daughter of John Bolling (descendant of Pocahontas) and Mary V. Kennon on January 20, 1730/31.  

The children of Col. John and Mary Fleming were Col. Thomas (m. a Randolph/killed January 12, 1777 in Battle of Princeton), Maj. John (killed in the battle of White Plains, Oct. 28, 1776), William (July 6, 1736-February 15, 1824), Charles, Mary, and Caroline.
