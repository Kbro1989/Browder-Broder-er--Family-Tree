Proofs That Martha Browder (c1768-c1811), Who Married Isham C. Browder (1773-1832), was a Daughter of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) of Dinwiddie County, Virginia and That Isham Browder (c1774-1832) Was Her Cousin:

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 3, 2019October 4, 2025Posted inUncategorized


By: John Mark Browder, BA, MS, MTS, MDiv (updated Jan. 1, 2025)

Martha Browder (c1768-c1811), daughter of Richard and Mary Browder, married her cousin, and my ancestor, Isham C. Browder (1773-1832). This fact is first stated in the addendum to the book, “Genealogy of David Browder, of Virginia and His Descendants”, in which David P. Browder refers to research he had recently obtained through his nephew, the Rev. Robert W. Browder, which Robert Browder, in turn, had obtained from his father, the Rev. George R. Browder, who had, in turn, obtained the information from his father, Robert Browder (b. 1804), son of David Browder and Mary Cousins.  In the addendum to this book by Thomas E. Browder, David states this fact in the following manner:

“One BROWDER [John], an Englishman, married a WELSH WOMAN [Elizabeth], and emigrated to America [as we know, he was actually born here] early in the 18th century, and settled in Virginia, where he lived and died. His name has not been retained in the family. The children of these progenitors of the Browder family were Richard, Joseph, ARTHUR, George and Nancy as far as known. . . . MARTHA BROWDER, daughter of RICHARD and MARY THOMPSON BROWDER, married a cousin ISHAM BROWDER. Their children were Mary and RICHARD so far as is known” (Browder, Thomas, pp. 13-14).

This, as far as I know, is the very first written mention in a book of the marriage of the cousins Isham C. Browder and Martha Browder.  But I still wound up having to prove it, as many wanted to dismiss it.

First, in 1808 Isham Browder and Martha Browder, his wife, gave Power of Attorney to Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia to sell Martha’s interest in about 200 acres of land, located on the Gravelly Run (Rockingham County, North Carolina Deed Book N, p. 339).  While all of this is not directly specified, Richard Browder, Martha’s father, was not a large land owner and probably only owned about 200 acres at the time of his death. So, it is certain that he did not bequeath it all to Martha, but bequeathed each of his living children a certain percentage of divided interest in said land. But, sadly, we don’t have a Will to consult.

It is likely that the Richard Browder mentioned in the Power of Attorney was the son of her brother, David Browder and his wife, Mary Cousins, since Martha’s father had already died about 1807, and that the land in question must have been inherited from her father, Richard Browder, who resided in Dinwiddie County all of his life. It should be understood that there is no reason for Martha to have been named in such a document other than the fact that she had inherited a percentage in said land, and that she was actually the one giving Power of Attorney to sell land that she had inherited. Otherwise, it would have only been necessary for Isham to have been named and she would not need to sign off on it. 

So, this document shows a direct relationship between Richard Browder and his daughter, Martha, and shows that she married her cousin, Isham C. Browder. It clearly shows a connection between Martha Browder and Dinwiddie County, Virginia. The fact that Power of Attorney was given to a member of the Browder family also shows conclusively that Martha was a member of the Browder family. Otherwise, why would she not have elected a member of some other family? This is the only known document that conclusively names Martha Browder directly as the wife of Isham C. Browder, although the division of Jones Browder’s property also indicates it directly. The POA follows from Rockingham County, North Carolina Deed Book N, p. 339 (from a copy by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

“Browder, Richard from BROWDER, ISHAM & WIFE, Power of Attorney, 200 A of Land: Know all men by these presents that we ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER his WIFE, both of the county of Rockingham and state of N. C., have for good cause to us moving, have nominated, authorized, ordained and appointed, and do by these presents authorize, ordain and appoint our friend Richard Browder of the county of Dinwiddia (sic) and commonwealth of Va., to act, make over convey and confirm a certain piece or parcel of land situate, lying and being in the said county of Dinwiddia (sic) and commonwealth aforesaid and the waters of the Gravelly Run, and contains about 200 acres of land:  And we, the said ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves and heirs, creditors and administrators do make or convey and confirm unto the said Richard Browder full power absolute authority to sell and dispose, make over, convey and confirm at the will and pleasure, a sure and good right and title to the aforesaid land and premises as above described, to whomsoever he may think proper, as full and ample in every respect, as we could do were we personally present, to all which matter and things – we the said ISHAM BROWDER and the said MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves our heirs creditors and Administrators firmly by these presents, sealed with our seals and dated this day of ___ in the year 1808.

Signed, sealed and acknowledged in the presence of ISHAM BROWDER (seal) Augustine C. Browder MARTHA BROWDER (seal) Thos. Winston Joseph Mclain St. of N. C. Rockingham Co. May Session 1810: The written power of attorney was duly proved in open court by the oath of Thomas Winston & on motion ordered to be recorded.          R. Galloway, C.”

Notice that, although he was a witness, Augustine C. Browder did not directly inherit any of said land, nor similar property, and did not make a Power of Attorney to convey anything himself. This might have been the case if Isham C. Browder were the inheritor of said property, because his brother, as well as other siblings, would have also inherited similar property from Richard Browder in that case. This shows that Isham C. Browder was not descended from Richard Browder, but that Martha Browder was. She is obviously the one who inherited this property, located in Dinwiddie County, Virginia.

It is interesting to note that, in this document it is stated that the couple believed that they had good reason to move. Obviously, the inference is that they moved from Dinwiddie County, Virginia, or close to that location, and relocated back to Rockingham County, North Carolina. What prompted this move? The answer to this question is unknown, but they probably always intended to. Whatever their reasoning was, they considered it good enough not only to move, but also to elect another trusted family member to deal with Martha’s inheritance so that they did not have to return to do so. What eventually happened to said property is still an open question, since I have located no further documentation concerning it. But, presumably, it was sold, so that Isham and Martha Browder received their part of the proceeds from said sale.

Martha Browder (c1768-c1811) is also mentioned multiple times in the Dinwiddie County, Virginia Order Book of the Circuit Superior Court, which details the settlement of Jones Browder’s estate, although she was already deceased by the time the case went to court and, thus, her children are also named in her stead. Martha is mentioned because she was a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, and Jones Browder (c1764-c1826) was her nephew. This becomes absolutely clear from the text. So, if her children were first cousins to Jones Browder (and they were), then the father of Jones Browder had to have been her brother. There is no way around this. And it has been established by documentation that Jones Browder had three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel, and that all of them were sons of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786). That makes Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) Martha’s brother.

THIS Arthur Browder, clearly a son of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, is NOT the one who may have married Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772). He is, instead, believed to have married a daughter of Gabriel and Grace Harrison, although this has not been proven. He did marry Rebecca Jones, whom I think was a second wife. 

The case was finally settled on April 4, 1837 by John T. Sydnor, Executor of David Thacker, who had been Administrator of Jones Browder’s estate. On that date, Jones’ estate was ordered divided among his FIRST COUSINS on the PATERNAL side of his family. It was divided among some of them on September 21, 1836, and also on April 4, 1837. Martha Browder and her heirs (children), Richard and Mary, are named in these documents. Martha Browder, who married Isham C. Browder (son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox), therefore, again, WAS a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder!

For the children of Martha Browder (daughter of Richard and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia) to have been first cousins on the paternal side of Jones Browder’s family, Martha had to be a sister to Arthur Browder, father of Jones Browder, period. But, they didn’t have to be in order to be named because Martha was a first cousin on the paternal side of the family in question herself. So, SHE is the one actually named as an inheritor. But, since she was already deceased when everything was decided, her children inherited in her stead. This is how it worked in all cases here. 

Again, it is also known, through family tradition, that Isham C. Browder married his cousin, Martha, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. For this to be possible, Isham C. Browder had to also have been descended from the same Browder line as Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. We know that his father was Isham Browder, who married Talitha Cox. There is so much documentation for this that there can be absolutely no question of it.

So, it becomes obvious that Isham C. Browder had to have been descended from one of Richard Browder’s brothers through his father, Isham Browder. This Isham Browder (c1732-1784) WAS the son, and unnamed heir, of Arthur Browder (1715-c1738), whether one accepts that this Arthur married Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772), or not. This becomes obvious because of the preponderance of the evidence.

ALL of the legatees who took part were descendants of John and Elizabeth Browder, and they were called “first cousins” in the court documents. There is no way of getting around this fact. So, obviously, Richard and Mary Browder named one son (most likely their first) Arthur, probably after Richard’s brother, who was already deceased by the time this son was born.

From the document dated April 2, 1834 (Dinwiddie County, Virginia Chancery Order Book 1, 1832-1852, pp. 46-47), the following is copied verbatim (but structurally modified for readability, also adding information in brackets as a help to identify persons named):

“Mary Pentecost, formerly [Mary Thompson] Browder [daughter of Richard and Mary Browder],

John W. P. Pool [Pettypool] and Mary [actually, Nancy A. Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder] his wife, formerly Williams,

This day this cause came on to be . . . heard on the bill answer of John T. Sydnor exor of David Thacker decd and exhibits, the other defendants who are out of the county and against whom the plaintiffs appear to have proceeded in the mode proscribed by law against absent defendants still failing to appear and answer, on the motion of the plaintiffs by counsel the Court doth take their bill for confession as to those defendants and was agreed by council on consideration whereof the court doth order that the defendant John T. Sydnor exor of David Thacker decd do render an amount of his Testators administrations of the estate of Jones Browder decd before a commissioner . . . is directed to . . . and settle the same and make report thereof to the Court with any matters specially stated deemed pertinent by himself, or which may be required by the parties to be so stated, and that the commissioner advertize once a week for two months in some newspaper printed in the city of Richmond and in the Cty of Washington for all persons who may claim to be distributes of Jones Browder decd in the maternal line to appear before him and prove their relationship to the decedent on or before the first day of September next or they will be excluded from all share of the estate.

Just this document alone proves (1) that Isham Browder, who married Talitha Cox, was considered a FIRST COUSIN ON THE PATERNAL SIDE and (2) that Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder was also considered a FIRST COUSIN ON THE PATERNAL SIDE. The document names Isham Browder (c1732-1784) directly, along with his descendants “ISHAM [C.] BROWDER, Augustine [C.] Browder, Nancy Fear[s], formerly Browder, Talitha Clarke, formerly Browder and Martha Whitmore, formerly Browder”. Then, separately, it also names Martha Browder directly, “Mary Meadows (sic) [actually, Meador], formerly Browder and RICHARD BROWDER, heirs of MARTHA BROWDER decd”.

To name both separately conclusively proves that they were both cousins on the paternal side of the family, which means that they were both descended from John and Elizabeth Browder, just like all the other plaintiffs were. This proves that Martha was indeed a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder and also conclusively proves that Isham Browder (c1732-1784) was the son of one of the brothers of Richard. The ONLY one of Richard’s brothers who died early enough for Richard and Mary to have taken in his son and brought him up (another family tradition), was ARTHUR. Thus, Isham Browder (c1732-1784) WAS the son of Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) mentioned in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book as his heir, period. Case closed.
