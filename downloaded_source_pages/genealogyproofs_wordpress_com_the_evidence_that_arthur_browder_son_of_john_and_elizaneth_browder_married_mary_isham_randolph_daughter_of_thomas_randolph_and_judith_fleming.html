The Evidence That Arthur Browder, Son of John and Elizaneth Browder, Married Mary Isham Randolph, Daughter of Thomas Randolph and Judith Fleming

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 3, 2019January 1, 2025Posted inUncategorized

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated Jan. 1, 2025)

To begin with, I want it well-known that I came up with the potential scenario that Arthur Browder (1715-c1738), brother of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807), and father of Isham Browder (c1732-1784), who married Talitha Cox (c1745-July 3, 1828), married Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772) by way of much research, using BOOKS way back in 1983-84.  I know; that’s ancient times for most of you, and WAY before the internet.  I didn’t have the internet to help me in the beginning and, for that, I am truly thankful, because it would have only led me astray, as it does so many today.  But, it has been a help in finding supporting documentation over the years. 

             I re-emphasize – I am the one who came up with this scenario all those years ago.  So, where the researcher may see someone linking Arthur Browder with Mary Isham Randolph on the internet today, know that I am the ultimate source for that.  If anyone wondered where it came from, there is no further need to wonder about it.  Other researchers had already relegated Arthur Browder (1715-c1738), a son of John (c1686-p. 1741) and Elizabeth (c1685-p. 1741) Browder, as someone who died early, apparently, without modern-day descendants (they believed that he had children, but also believed the line had died out).  I have shown that this was an error on their part, made simply because they did not have enough information.  

             In any case, I have not only proven that Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) was the father of my Isham Browder (c1732-1784), but I have all but proven, at least, that he married (after eloping with) none other than Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772), daughter of Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph and Judith Fleming.  An excerpt from William Randolph I of Turkey Island Henrico County, Virginia And His Immediate Descendants by Wassell Randolph that I found during my initial research, follows:

MARY ISHAM RANDOLPH (1718-20 –   ) was married twice.  Her first marriage was by elopement about 1732 with the overseer on the plantation of one of her uncles, a marriage which greatly scandalized her family.  Her first husband died, apparently, without issue.  She later married, about 1740, Rev. James Keith.  As one writer has remarked: ‘Family and friends little knew that this daughter, so scorned and derided for her misalliance was to be the chief glory of the Tuckahoe Randolphs.’  But this glory came through the emineace (sic) attained by so many of the descendants of this second marriage (Randolph, p. 51).

It should be noted that this text (full of inaccuracies, but still a good source, as will be seen) states that there were “apparently” no children from the first union.  And Mary’s first husband was not named.  The frank fact of the matter is that he never has actually been officially named in extant primary records, although a name was traditionally given to him somewhat later, which research (by myself, and supported by others) has shown to have been completely fictitious.  

             Now, David Parrish Browder, Sr., (grandson of David Browder (c1755-c1813), and great-grandson of Richard (c1719-c1807) and Mary (1717/20-p. 1778) Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, wrote a letter from Richmond, Virginia, dated March 6, 1937, concerning the ancestry of Isham Browder (he was thinking of the Revolutionary War soldier’s presumed father), to a relative, Gussie Browder, who resided in Fulton, Kentucky.  It is obvious by the content of this letter that David had done a great deal of research, mainly consisting of interviews with relatives.  In this letter David stated that he “felt certain” that Richard Browder’s brother, Arthur, had a son named Isham.  He believed that this son was the “infant heir” of Arthur Browder (1715-c1738), mentioned in the Bristol Parish Register in 1739, which we will get to.  Within the register record, a name is not provided for Arthur Browder’s heir; thus, the mystery.  Ordinarily a child would have to have been a son in order to have been an heir, so the obvious assumption was (and still is) that this heir was a son.  Also, it was unusual for someone to be mentioned, but not actually named, in such records. Even as an infant, he should have been named. 

             David P. Browder, Sr. also believed, according to this letter, that Arthur’s brother, Richard (again, David’s own great-grandfather), had taken Arthur’s “infant heir” into his own household to be brought up to adulthood.  This, normally, would only have taken place if both parents had already died, but in this case, I believe there were extenuating circumstances requiring that Isham be taken in even though his mother was still alive. 

As I will show, Mary Isham Randolph was, in fact, led to believe that her child, and her husband, had been killed by her own family!  So, she would have been in no position to bring up any children from this first marriage, since she believed that there were none who still lived, and since this incident made her a bit crazy, as we will see. 

             Again, David also believed that the particular Isham Browder he sought for was the father of the Isham Browder (June 12, 1762-Feb. 19, 1830) who fought during the American Revolutionary War, and that this Isham Browder married his cousin, Martha, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.  This part of his analysis is entirely incorrect.  We know this because of available records that he, obviously, did not have access to.  But, people still perpetuate this error to this day, based on faulty information.  David cannot be faulted for this, but those who perpetuate this information can, because we have so much more information today than he had then. 

             Now, to continue the story of Arthur Browder, son of John and Elizabeth Browder, one must look at the evidence in support of his marriage to Mary Isham Randolph, and of their naming their child “Isham”.  Here we meet William Byrd.  His book, A Progress to the Mines, is the ONLY known primary source for the poignant story of Mary Isham Randolph and her elopement.  This work is essentially a journal of Byrd’s travels while surveying the line between Virginia and North Carolina.  Therefore, like any journal, it comes complete with dates, and each entry must have been written on or about the date mentioned in the text only, perhaps, to be revised a bit later. 

Byrd visited the Thomas Randolph (July 10, 1683-1730) family plantation (Thomas Randolph had been Byrd’s chief plantation overseer) on September 20, 1732, at which time he learned of the story surrounding Mary’s elopement.  Again, Mary had been born about 1716, so she would have been about sixteen years of age at the time these events occurred, as they were of recent import at the time of Byrd’s original writing.  Obviously, Arthur, born in 1715, would have been about the same age.  Byrd wrote, (p. 124; entry dated September 20th):

I made a running visit to three of my quarters where, besides finding all the people well, I had the pleasure to see better crops than usual both of corn and tobacco.  I parted there with my intendant, and pursued my journey to MR. RANDOLPH’S, at Tuckahoe, without meeting with any adventure by the way.  Here I found MRS. FLEMING [that was her maiden name], who was packing up her baggage with design to follow her husband the next day, who was gone to a new settlement in Goochland.  Both he and she have been about seven years [so, they would have begun thinking about it, and perhaps preparing for it, in about 1725. That would, of course, be an exaggeration, since Thomas had not passed away yet, unless it was a thought of Thomas and Judith prior to his death which she wished to carry on] persuading themselves to remove to that retired part of the country, though they had the two strong arguments of health and interest for so doing. The widow smiled graciously upon me, and entertained me very handsomely. Here I learned all the tragical story of her daughter’s humble marriage with her uncle’s overseer. Besides the meanness of this mortal’s aspect, the man HAS not one visible qualification, except impudence, to recommend him to a female’s inclinations. But there is sometimes such a charm in that Hibernian endowment, that frail woman cannot withstand it, though it stand alone without any other recommendation. Had she run away with a gentleman or a pretty fellow, there might have been some excuse for her, though he were of inferior fortune: but to stoop to a dirty plebeian, without any kind of merit, is the lowest prostitution. I found the family justly enraged at it; and though I had more good nature than to join in her condemnation, yet I could devise no excuse for so senseless a prank as this young gentlewoman had played. Here good drink was more scarce than good victuals, the family being reduced to the last bottle of wine, which was therefore husbanded very carefully. But the water was excellent.

This passage is a bit puzzling upon first read, since Byrd states that “Ms. Fleming” was a widow, and at the same time states that she was intent upon following her “husband” to a new settlement in Goochland County.  Calling her by her maiden name in and of itself indicates that Thomas Randolph had already died, and it is known that he died in 1730.  She did remarry, in December of 1733, to Nicolas Davies (could be Davis), but at the time of this writing, he was not yet her husband, officially, at least.  Still, he is obviously the person whom Byrd refers to as her husband, they obviously already having the intent to marry (and it is possible that they had been married in a Quaker ceremony, which is something that was not legally recognized at that time).  Thomas Randolph is known to have died there at Tuckahoe, and Judith herself later died in Goochland County, Virginia. 

             Now, it seems, as the story goes according to Byrd’s account, that Mary Isham Randolph had eloped with a plantation bailiff, or overseer, whose name, it should be noted, was NOT given by Byrd, from the Dungeness plantation in Goochland County, Virginia of her uncle, Isham Randolph.  And from Byrd’s description of this bailiff, it would seem that he was alive and ACTUALLY PRESENT within the Randolph household at that very point in time, especially since Byrd used the word “HAS.”  In fact, the text reads as though Mary was also present at that very moment during the exchange, as Byrd seems quite unwilling to openly condemn her.  Even though Byrd calls her “prank” a form of “prostitution” in his diary, he, like Jesus himself, refuses to condemn her openly (Bible; John 8: 3-11).  If Mary had been elsewhere, I submit that Byrd would have been more inclined to condemn her openly, along with her family.  And though the family was said to be “enraged” by her elopement “Mrs. Fleming” seemed to have had other things on her mind at that time.  So, the situation was, perhaps, not so dire as the family’s complaints would have led Byrd to have believed, or, at least, written.  And Byrd is known to exaggerate a bit in his writings in any case.

             Byrd here describes the man in question, basically, as a brute of low breeding (“dirty Plebian”) – a “hillbilly”, if you will.  Of course, someone like Byrd would also have described even this nation’s Founding Fathers in a similar way or, at the very least, as ruffians.  And Byrd also actually describes him as an IRISHMAN since he is referred to using the word “Hibernian.”  Hibernia is the Latin and poetic name for Ireland.  This word is always associated with the Irish.  And finally, Byrd describes the Judith “Fleming” family as at that time practically destitute, since they had all but run out of wine! 

That is the sum total of everything that Byrd tells us in the ONLY extant primary source known concerning these events.  If it were not for Byrd, we may not know about this story at all.  No other primary record is available, and only one other detailed reference is ever made by the Randolph family or any other relative, which would have been rendered unremarkable if it were not for this journal entry.  The reader should already be seeing exactly why the “infant heir” of Arthur Browder might deliberately have not been named in the vestry record.  No one wanted to acknowledge this event, or him. 

             But, one other reference, as indicated, is to be found in later tradition, which has embellished somewhat upon this tale, rendering the episode a tragedy.  That tradition states that Mary’s “brothers” were particularly incensed about the affair and were bent upon murder!  As this story goes, the couple were hunted down and eventually located by Mary’s brothers, hiding out on what later became Thomas Jefferson’s plantation (one should note that he was a Randolph relative himself, a grandson of Col. Isham Randolph and Jane Rogers) at a remote place called Elk Island on the James River.  

This story culminates tragically with the plantation overseer and child being murdered by Mary’s brothers, and Mary being forcibly returned to her family household.  Because of these tragic events, Randolph family tradition also holds that Mary became a “lunatic”.  And witnesses bear out that, whatever really happened, she was somewhat unhinged in her later years. 

But, in the meantime, Mary did later remarry, on March 2, 1735 at Germantown, Virginia, to the Rev. James Keith, and thereafter had several children by him.  So, if this tradition is in any way true in that these events affected Mary’s mind, she did recover to live a relatively normal life for several years afterward.  

             This story is detailed by William M. Paxton in his book The Marshall Family, or a Genealogical Chart of the Descendants of John Marshall and Elizabeth Markham.  That section is here reproduced verbatim here, as follows:

The story is told that when MARY ISHAM RANDOLPH was blooming into womanhood, she was induced by the bailiff upon the estate of Tuckahoe to elope with him. There was great excitement among the family and neighbors, and threats were freely made by the brothers . . . The search for the fugitive for a time was fruitless.  At length their retreat was discovered on Elk Island, in James River. The angry brothers came upon them at night, murdered the bailiff and the child, and brought their sister home. The deed of blood and cruelty so affected the wife and mother that she became deranged. But care was taken that no allusion should be made to the harrowing scenes she had witnessed, and her reason was at length restored.

             Years passed. MARY RANDOLPH married Parson James Keith. A family of children had grown up around them. The tragedy at Elk Island had been forgotten. The bailiff was supposed to be dead. But, one day MRS. KEITH received a letter, and, upon opening it, found that it purported to be from the supposedly deceased bailiff, stating that he had fled to foreign countries; after years of wandering had returned to look upon his lawful wife; had found her married and happy; that he would not afflict her by claiming her as his own, but advised her to be happy and forget him, who had more than died for her love, for she should hear no more from him. This letter was perhaps written by some evil-disposed person, or may have been only a practical joke. However that may be, it unhinged the mind of MRS. KEITH. She vainly sought for him, and throughout the remnant of her days the insanity manifested itself by a quiet melancholy, varied by some sudden freak of folly. Mrs. Colston lived with her for many years, and she, and all who met her in her widowhood, testify that she was a lunatic.

             Much mystery attends this tale. Mrs. Colston cited as a source was evidently Mrs. Rawleigh Colston, who was Elizabeth Marshall, Mary’s granddaughter, and sister of Chief Justice John Marshall. She would certainly have been a qualified witness to her grandmother’s insanity, and perhaps as to its cause. But Mary Randolph had no “brothers” who could have murdered the despised husband of their sister. The only brother she had was the Tuckahoe heir, William Randolph (1713-1745), described by Byrd as a “pretty” boy of nineteen. Still, gossipy as the famous diarist was, it seems impossible to believe that he completely fabricated the tale. [Because T]he grisly story of Elk Island (later owned by Jefferson) is said to have been full grown before Byrd’s diary was published (Randolph, pp. 50-51). 

It seems more likely to me that Arthur Browder (if he was the bailiff) apparently was not killed, and did not die upon an island (like the mythical King Arthur) after all.  He may well have been harmed during some kind of altercation, but we know that he died about 1738 (because that would be the reason that a survey had to be taken of his property and noted in the Vestry Book), too late to have been killed during such an episode with the Randolph family under these circumstances.  One must, first, remember that the Randolph family had gone to Goochland County, Virginia.  Apparently, Mary and her husband went along with them, for why would they have remained behind?  And Elk Island was in Goochland County. 

             On the surface this story by itself would still seem to hold no real clues for us if we are attempting to find the parentage of Isham Browder except for similarities of dates, since the husband of Mary Isham Randolph is not named.  However, upon closer inspection of the life of Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) as combined with this story, we can come to the following conclusions. 

Since this Arthur Browder was born in 1715 (or very early 1716), he could in fact have been married by 1732 at the age of about seventeen.  And, Mary could indeed have been married by the age of sixteen.  Youthful marriages were not uncommon during this time-period.  Neither was significant responsibility at an early age, such as working as a plantation bailiff.  Certainly, if one wished to get ahead in life at an early age, one might take up such a profession, even if it lacked a certain glamour.  Plantation bailiffs took charge of the plantations and the slaves for the plantation owners (and this may be key).  And, it would indeed have been beneficial when considering personal contacts which might occur during the performance of one’s duties.

             So, one can posit that Arthur Browder, if he was this plantation bailiff, was obviously interested in success.  And what better family to be working for than the already very prominent Randolph family of Virginia?  The Browders had been acquaintances with the Randolphs already for at least two generations even though this was mainly with the Henry Randolph line, and they all also attended church within the same parish.  And, again, Thomas Randolph, Mary’s father, had been a chief plantation overseer himself, already holding a certain status in society by virtue of the fact that he was part of the Tidewater Virginia aristocracy because of his family ties.  No doubt, he would have at least known of Arthur Browder, at least as a neighbor.  In addition, one can already see, given the example of Arthur’s grandparents, Lt. Arthur Jones and Susannah King, that this couple would see no real reason why they should not be married, as they were only, each, about one year younger than Arthur Jones and Susanna King had been when they married.  Frankly, the fit couldn’t be more perfect as far as a timeframe and proximity are concerned.

             Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) was born in Prince George (formerly Charles City) County, Virginia.  He is understood to have resided in the part of Prince George County that became a part of Halifax County, Virginia (next to Dinwiddie County) in 1752.  This made him, and other Browder family members, neighbors to the Randolph family, who resided along the James River.  Not only that, one must understand that Isham Browder (c1732-1784) resided in Halifax County, Virginia prior to moving to North Carolina. 

In any case, contact would have been natural since they were all neighbors and members of the same parish, even if such contact was, perhaps, a little strained due to differences in social status between the families.  Contact with Mary Isham Randolph would have been inevitable for Arthur Browder, it most likely having been initiated during church functions.  Love may indeed have blossomed between the two, but there is no doubt in my mind that Arthur Browder wanted to become a member of the Randolph family circle.  Marriage to Mary Isham Randolph could have made that happen.  And if Arthur Browder was influenced to any degree by the tale of King Arthur and his round table, chivalry and the like (something I get more into in my previous book, Faces of Our Ancestors), it seems likely that he could well have imagined the acceptability of having an affair with, and later marrying, a woman who at first might seem unreachable socially, just as King Arthur had mythically done.  Mary Isham Randolph would indeed have been such a young lady.  

             Still, even knowing all of this, even if all of this were to be accepted, it CANNOT be seen as absolute proof.  But, again, there is a clue to the identity of the person Mary eloped with in later Randolph family tradition.  That clue is contained in the statement that the plantation overseer in question was “an Irish nobody”.  That statement falls in line exactly with the statement William Byrd made.  Whoever this plantation overseer was, neither Byrd, nor the Randolph family, had anything good to say about him, according to Byrd’s narrative and later tradition.  But, more than that, he was again identified as being an IRISHMAN. 

So, if the correlating family traditions of the Browders having sailed from Ireland are true, then it becomes obvious that even during this time-period, no less than three generations after immigration, the Browder family was still seen as being Irish (even though they were not indigenously Irish).  And there were not very many families in Virginia during this time period which were Irish, as most Irish emigrated at some later date.  Beyond that, could Byrd’s slight have also been a backhanded effort to debase someone who was likely proud to hold the name of the great King Arthur – proud of his Welsh ancestry?  Byrd was reluctant to condemn Mary, but not so reluctant to condemn her husband.  

             Thus, according to Byrd’s primary account, along with other facts and Randolph family tradition combined, what we have here is a young local “Irishman” who worked for Isham Randolph as a plantation bailiff, eloping with Mary Isham Randolph, daughter of Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph and Judith Fleming, having a child, and, apparently, meeting his death during the 1730s (but, not because any Randolph “brothers” killed him).  This much can be considered clear.

             But, while it is possible to believe that Mary’s relatives may have wanted to murder this unnamed bailiff, there is no evidence in any extant official record that anyone in fact did so.  No one, as far as is known, was arrested or brought to trial for such an incident.  And it is even less likely that anyone really would have murdered “the child”.  In fact, again, Mary had only one brother, so if there is any accuracy within this part of the story at all, it would likely be that the “brothers”, in fact, would have had to have been Mary’s brother along with some of her cousins, as has been suggested by others. 

One can readily understand that even though youthful marriages were not uncommon in those days, it would have been quite likely that Mary’s mother (for, again, her father was already deceased) would not have given her approval to such a relationship on several levels, not least of which would have been the fact that both Mary and Arthur were, perhaps, seen as just a bit too young, or a bit immature, for marriage.  This is probably the very reason she finally did not give her approval, not so much because of differing social statuses between the respective families.  And this is likely the very reason why an elopement had taken place, because approval had not been given, and young people really don’t tend to listen to their elders.  That said, Mary’s mother was at that time in a relationship with someone that she was not “legally” married to, so her example was not entirely pristine either. There can be little doubt that this would have been noticed by Mary and the unnamed bailiff. 

             It seems most likely to me that the young couple probably promised that they would wait to consummate their marriage, and that this had been accepted as a compromise, since they had already eloped anyway.  However, it was not long before Mary was found to be pregnant.  Surely, she was not yet showing by the time of Byrd’s visit in September of 1732, or he would have noted it.  The family probably got all the way to Goochland County  before this was discovered.  Mary’s family would have been, more or less, justifiably angry over this perceived betrayal, so Mary and her husband fled to a place that Mary would have known about; land that was already owned by members of her mother’s family (that is a fact that is in the records).  They probably felt relatively safe on an island, isolated from most other people.  This is the place where their child would have been born – in Goochland County on Elk Island.  But, eventually, they were found by someone, of this there can be no doubt.  An altercation ensued, and Mary was forced to return home, without her husband and without her child!

             But, even with all of their presumed anger, it is difficult to see anyone in the Randolph family murdering a child, and legally getting away with it.  Killing the plantation bailiff might have been seen as socially acceptable during that time period in order to preserve family dignity, but murdering a child would likely not have been seen in the same way.  No, the Randolphs allowed them to live, but kept it a secret from Mary, who never saw either of them again.

             Poor Mary was led to believe that her family had indeed done both of these things, and she was never told otherwise by them as long as she lived.   She never knew for sure that she still had a living child by her first love!   The Randolph family kept their secret from her and from the rest of society, perhaps with the help of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, who, according to tradition, took the child of his brother, known to have been named “Isham”, in after his death.  Thus, Richard Browder, brought Isham up as if he were his own.  Again, Browder family tradition recalls that Richard took in the child of his brother, Arthur.  That child, I submit, could have been none other than our Isham Browder!  This, because it becomes obvious, through the process of elimination, that it is Isham Browder who is mentioned in the Vestry Book as the heir of Arthur Browder.  

             Chief Justice John Marshall (1755-1835) wrote of his maternal grandmother in this fashion: “My mother was named Mary Keith.  She was the daughter of a clergyman of the name of [James] Keith, who migrated from Scotland and intermarried with a Miss Randolph of James River.”   

In this description Marshall carefully avoids mention of the circumstances of his grandmother’s life and even avoids mentioning her directly by name.  No doubt he did this primarily due to his embarrassment concerning her conduct (not to mention his grandfather’s conduct, which was also scandalous and who he also did not provide a given name for).  Whether Mary’s reputation was deserved or not, it still proved embarrassing to Marshall and he would not talk about it.

             But what is truly interesting here is that he would obviously have known his grandmother’s given name, yet he refused to actually name her.  Everyone seemed to be so careful about not naming her child and not naming the bailiff, and Marshall would not even name HER.  The reason for this has to be more than simple embarrassment due to family scandal. 

             Later, other non-primary accounts also mention that Mary was actually about 16 or 17 years of age when she eloped with the overseer in question, which fits the story perfectly.  They also restate that the overseer was an “Irishman”, and even give the name of “Enoch Arden” as that of the plantation overseer.  However, these later sources can easily be called into question as to their accuracy, or lack thereof.  This mainly because there is no actual record of an Enoch Arden during that time period.  Trust me, I have looked extensively for him.  This was simply a fictitious name.  But I am not the only one to come to that conclusion either. 

             To reiterate just a bit, this sad story continues with Mary Isham Randolph falling in love with the Anglican clergyman, the Rev. James Keith, who was the minister of Henrico Parish.  Henrico Parish was among the largest and most important parishes in Virginia at that time.  This parish included Tuckahoe and other Randolph plantations on the James River, as well as the fledgling town of Richmond.

             Things went well for this family until some years later when Mary received a letter purporting to be from the man she had first eloped with, triggering insanity.  Mary despaired of the possibility that her first love might still be alive after all and, more than that, that if he were still alive, then her marriage to James Keith might be invalid, according to church teaching, and her children illegitimate!  Is it any wonder that John Marshall avoided mention of this event and wouldn’t even name her? 

I submit that, perhaps, the Browders proceeded in the same manner, burying, for all practical purposes, their relational ties with the Randolph family here.  The whole matter became a secret kept between the two families.  No Browder family descendant, save one several generations later, was ever given the name “Randolph” and I submit that there is no connection as far as memory of these events is concerned.  In addition, even though it is now clear that Richard Browder married Mary Eppes Randolph, she was always known by her adopted name of Thompson and girls named after her were never named “Mary Randolph” Browder, but were instead named “Mary Thompson” Browder.  But that still did not cause the two families to proceed as mortal enemies, as documentation shows.  It was just a secret between families that, I submit, Isham Browder himself probably never knew. 

             One other important fact should be kept in mind.  Mary Isham Randolph did not marry the Rev. James Keith until 1735 (not 1740 as some sources state), at about the age of nineteen.  Scandal or no (and it was a scandal for reasons I do not detail here), her marriage at this age would have been seen as somewhat more acceptable to her family.  And one must remember that if Isham Browder was born in 1732 or 1733, he would have only been about seven or eight years of age in 1739 and could technically have been called an “infant” according to the prevailing usage of the time (persons could be classified as infants up to the age of twenty-one, at which age they could inherit property).  

Let it be said that, if one is determined that Isham Browder was actually born closer to the date of 1745, as was believed for many years by other researchers, then it should be pointed out that he first appears in extant records in 1756.  We know this simply by fiat, since he was the very first person named Isham Browder.  Due to the nature of the record, one would have to assume that Isham was already considered to be an “adult” by that time and would, therefore, have been at least twenty-one years of age.  For that to be the case, then, Isham would have had to have been born no later than 1735.  So, the date of 1745 is simply incorrect.  

             The process of elimination when determining who Isham’s father was is very telling.  First, we KNOW that he was descended from a brother of Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  We have enough evidence to support this conclusively.  And, obviously, we KNOW that he was not a son of Richard Browder himself.  Remember, all early Browders in America are believed to have been descended from Edmund Browder I, and evidence supports this.  Also, Richard Browder was also a son of John and Elizabeth Browder, and a grandson of Edmund Browder I.  John’s sons were Arthur, Edmund, John, Richard, William, George, and Joseph (at least).  No matter what date we give to the birth of Isham Browder, William, George and Joseph were all too young to have been his father.  And, we know who their descendants were anyway.  Actually, William could have been the father of Isham at about the age of 16, if Isham were born as late as 1736.  But, William died about 1763, well after Isham came to adulthood (whether he was born in 1732 or 1736, as the date of 1745 is, again, clearly too late for his birth) so there would have been no reason for Richard to take Isham into his household after the death of his brother, William, if William were his father.  So, William is also eliminated as a possibility.  That would leave either Edmund or John. The problem with Edmund is that we know that he had a son named Edmund. So, if he had also had a son named Isham, why would Richard have not also brought Edmund up along with Isham IF Edmund died early enough for this to be necessary?  Of course, since Edmund lived to about 1759, this would not have been necessary anyway.  That would make John the only other possibility.  But he is also eliminated for similar reasons.  So, the only son of John and Elizabeth Browder who could have been the father of Isham Browder was Arthur. 

             Therefore, since we have always known that Richard was not Isham’s father, that leaves only Arthur.  There literally are no other possibilities at all!  Isham’s father has to have been one of three brothers, Arthur, Edmund, or John, and Arthur is the ONLY logical candidate because, again, Arthur is the only one who died early enough for Isham to have been brought up by his uncle, Richard.  So, “proof” or no, the fact of the matter is that we are descended from Arthur Browder because he was the father of Isham Browder.  The case is made!

             It should be emphasized that, when Mary received the letter from her purported first love, it was evidently known by others from the very start that it had not actually come from him.  Why?  Because these other persons, I submit, knew that he was already deceased even though he had not been killed by the Randolph family.  It is true that the sources available do not state exactly when Mary received the letter.  However, it does seem perfectly obvious that it was received after the birth of her last child, who was born in 1748, for I doubt that she would have had any more children following such an incident.  Since Arthur Browder was deceased by 1738/39, at about the age of twenty-four, the writer certainly could not possibly have been him and, I again submit, certain family members and others knew this.  But Mary did not know this, and they could not have told her because, if they had, it would have exposed to Mary all that had happened and everything that her family had done to cover up the facts.

             Here is the passage, already cited, at long last, from the Vestry Book of Bristol Parish, verbatim.  Concerning the Returns of Processioning for the Year 1739 (pp. 98-99) the notation reads as follows:

Miles Whweatt and William Batt[e] the Same (Processioned all the Lands in their Precinct) excepting two lines of Batte & Jordan they disagreeing & another piece belonging to the HEIR of ARTHER BROWDER the HEIR being an Infant.

That is all the text says.  But, in saying so little, as we have seen, it actually tells us a great deal.  One should here note that there is no reference at all as to the date of birth of this “infant.”  All we can discern here is that he was alive in 1739 (about the year Arthur Browder would have died, since this processing would not have been necessary otherwise) and was not yet twenty-one years of age.  This mention of the heir of Arthur Browder, I again submit, has to have been of Isham Browder (c1732-1784).  

             It seems, again, all too obvious that Arthur Browder, son of John Browder and the “Welsh Lady” Elizabeth, met Mary Isham Randolph, first during church functions within Bristol Parish, Virginia, and later again as he worked as a plantation overseer for Isham Randolph (she is known to have frequented that home).  The two wished to marry, but approval was not given by Mary’s mother.  So, they eloped and were married anyway.  They resided for a while with Mary’s mother and brother, as her father had already passed away and, during this time, William Byrd visited the family and found out about the affair, which he wrote about in his journal. 

The family all eventually moved to Goochland County, Virginia.  Later, some sort of altercation ensued between Arthur Browder and the Randolphs, during which Arthur was probably harmed, but not killed.  However, Mary was led to believe that both he and the child were indeed killed by her own family, and was never told otherwise for the rest of her life. 

These events took a great toll upon poor Mary, but she recovered and married the Rev. James Keith and had several children by him.  Arthur Browder surely knew that Mary had remarried and, in an effort to avoid causing her any more heartache, he changed his name and went away.  He could not simply reside in Virginia, for his presence would have become known.  So, I believe, he became a mariner and only returned occasionally and, finally, just prior to his own death for the last time, apparently dying along with his own grandfather, Edmund at what used to be Jamestown.  Later, however, some heartless prankster wrote Mary a letter which purported to be from Arthur.  After having read this letter, she plunged deeper into insanity, from which she never recovered this time.  Deep down, she must somehow have known that she was being lied to by everyone, but no one would ever tell her the truth.  

Mary eventually died, in December of 1772, and was interred, along with her husband, James Keith, under the chancel of old Elk Run Church, Prince William County (now Fauquier County), Virginia. 

             Finally, to help tie everything together, it can be shown that the Randolph and Browder families interacted with each other even before Arthur Browder was born.  For example, to reiterate a few things, on May 6, 1711, Edmund Browder I (c1651-c1739) of Bristol Parish, Prince George County, Virginia, purchased a 129 acre section of land from Henry Randolph III (1689-1726) of Bristol Parish, Henrico County, Virginia.  This deed was recorded on May 8, 1711 (Prince George County Wills & Deeds [PGCW&D] 1710-1713, p. 53).  This was only about three to four years prior to Arthur’s birth.

One thing that makes this purchase significant, even if a direct, continuous relationship cannot be proven for the families over the generations, is that John Green Randolph (1797/99-Feb. 24, 1880), who later moved to McNairy County, Tennessee, was descended from the Henry Randolph I line.  My Richard Browder (1801-1885), son of Isham C. and Martha Browder, made his very first land purchase in McNairy County from said Randolph. There is no way for us to know now with certainty whether they were aware of any family relationship or not, but it is possible. 

Also, Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth Browder were both members of the Bristol Parish, Church of England, and attended the Old Blanford (St. Paul’s) Church, located in present day Petersburg, Virginia.  The Randolph, Epes and Herbert families were also members of Bristol Parish, and members of these families also attended Blanford church along with the Browders.  We know this not just from records, but also because some members of these families are buried in the cemetery surrounding the church building.  In addition, Edmund Browder was a tobacco planter, as the others also were, so they held a similar status in society, although wealth between these families varied greatly.  They certainly would have interacted on that business level. 

Here I have to state that others posit that it is more likely that Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) married a daughter of Isham Epes, mainly because Edmund Browder and his sons owned land that adjoined land of Isham Epes, and that the daughter was, perhaps, named Mary.  The two potential problems with this are (1) just because they all owned land in proximity to each other does not mean that the families intermarried, although this was common, and (2) I have yet to find a definitive list of the children of this Isham Epes (especially one named Mary), and what I have found on the internet is sketchy, at best.  So, I, personally, cannot draw that correlation.  Without more evidence, I have to consider this mere speculation.

But, there is an even greater problem with this.  That problem is that the Will of Isham Epes, dated March 4, 1717 and recorded on August 5, 1717 (Henrico County Wills and Deeds 1714-1718, p. 191-207), does not name either a wife or children.  It makes absolutely no reference to any descendants that he might have had.  It is likely, then, that he had no descendants, and probably didn’t even marry.  And I have not been able to find any list of descendants for the son of William Epes, who also owned land in the area, either. 

That said, we do indeed have an Epes connection, if you will, which I detailed in my book Faces of Our Ancestors.  It has always been said that the wife of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) of Dinwiddie County, Virginia was Mary Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson (1696-1746/47).  In my search for her ancestry, I came upon rather startling information.  Mary Epes (c1700-c1753), daughter of Col. Francis Epes III and Anne Elizabeth Isham, first married a William Randolph, whose parentage was unknown.  She later married Dr. James Thompson on Feb. 8, 1724/25.  

Some further state that she remarried again, to George Robertson following the death of James, but I have yet to find actual evidence of this.

In any case, she had two children by William Randolph, Isham (bef. Aug. 1720-Aug. 1751), and Mary Epes (b. 1717/20).  And she had one daughter by James, Ann.  That would make both Isham Randolph and Mary Epes Randolph, step-children to Dr. James Thompson.  So, Mary was not a daughter, but a step-daughter. 

It is my conclusion that Mary Epes Randolph, daughter of William Randolph and Mary Epes, married Richard Browder after having first married a Green (but not Burwell Green as I had previously thought); their children being Mary Isham Green and Betty Green. 

I will state, however, that, although there is no absolute, documented, proof that Richard Browder married Mary Epes Randolph; to dismiss this link, one would have to dismiss the family tradition that he had married “Mary Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson”.  And, to dismiss the marriage of Arthur Browder and Mary Isham Randolph, one would have to dismiss all of the evidence presented here for it.  That would leave the researcher, basically, at zero, which is basically where I started way back in the 1980s.
