Earliest Descendants of Thomas Isham Browder and Talitha Cox

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected Dec. 24, 2024)

The known children of Capt. Thomas Isham Browder (c1732-1784) and Talitha Cox (c1745-July 3, 1828) were Isham C. (c1774-1832), Talitha Cox (1778-p. 1834), Mary Thompson “Polly” (c1779-April 23, 1793), Nancy Washington (c1781-c1815), Augustine Claiborne (c1782-1816), and Martha “Patsey” (February 27, 1783-April 14, 1872).  I state “known” descendants because it is entirely possible that this couple did have other children prior to their first surviving son, Isham C. Browder.  But, if they did, these children clearly did not survive to adulthood, nor even up to the time that Isham wrote his Will of 1779.

It is believed by some that Talitha Browder, widow of Thomas Isham Browder, did not want her daughters to marry into the Thrasher family. However, this is incorrect based on a misunderstanding of Talitha’s will. Regardless, it is also well-known that three of them did so. In all actuality, Talitha had no issue with the Thrasher family but may well have not wanted her daughters to marry so young. All three of these daughters married Thrasher brothers, all sons of Joseph Cloud Thrasher (c1757-c1810) and Margaret Lewis (b. c1756), prior to 1800.  The three daughters are not shown in the 1800 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina as residing within the household of Talitha Browder, so they were obviously already married. 

However, it is obvious that one cannot trust any of the various proposed birth dates for either of these Thrasher men found on the internet, soI have not consulted the census data to try to determine their birth years.  What is certain is that, not only did each of these three brothers die relatively soon after marrying the three Browder ladies, but nearly all of their children also died as minors, with no issue.

Joseph Cloud Thrasher, son of John Thrasher (c1725-c1788) and Ruth Cloud (c1723-c1814), married Margaret Lewis about 1773/74 in Guilford County, North Carolina.  Joseph and Margaret were the parents of these three men. 

The 1790 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina lists Joseph Cloud Thrasher (giving his full name) as 16 and above, along with one other male in the same age category, and two makes 16 and up, as well as 2 females.  Five slaves are listed for this family.  Interestingly, the 1751 Dan River Tax List lists a Joseph Cloud, as well as a John “Thresher”. 

Soon after their marriage, Richard Thrasher and his bride, Mary Thompson Browder  moved away to Georgia, settling in Clarke County.  The others followed at a later date, along with Joseph Cloud Thrasher himself. Only one daughter is listed as still residing with Talitha in 1800, and that was their youngest daughter, Martha.  

Talitha Cox Browder (1778-p. 1834), daughter of Thomas Isham and Talitha Browder, married (1) Isaac Thrasher (c1774-1815/16), prior to 1800.  This couple had three children, who were living when Talitha Cox-Browder, her mother, wrote her Will of December 29, 1827.  These children did live to adulthood, according to court documents.  They were Pinkney B. (1799-p. 1826), Martha “Patsey” Browder (Sept. 13, 1802-Oct. 14, 1868, Henry County, Georgia), and Andrew Jackson (c1808-1832).  All were born in Clarke County, Georgia. Sadly, there is no 1800 census for Greene County, Georgia to consult for further information on this family since the British burned this record during the War of 1812. 

“Patsey Nowlan”, is mentioned in her grandmother’s Will.  This is Martha “Patsey” Browder Thrasher, who married Thomas Nolan on December 26, 1821 in Clarke County, Georgia.  She did inherit at least something from her grandmother.  

Also, it becomes obvious that court documents were a bit in error concerning the children of Isaac and Talitha Thrasher. Still, Isaac Thrasher had died sometime in 1815 or very early 1816, since, in February of 1816, Herbert C. Thrasher (son of Cloud and Nancy Thrasher) entered Court, requesting that Talitha Thrasher be his guardian since Isaac was deceased.  This was granted. 

Talitha Cox Browder later married (2) Isaac Clarke on October 12, 1826, in Clarke County, Georgia (Clarke County Marriage Book B, p. 23).  They had no children. 

We know nothing further of either Pinkney B. Thrasher or Andrew Jackson Thrasher. It would seem that both died in early adulthood and there are no known descendants of either. However, Martha “Patsey” Browder Thrasher, having married Thomas Nolan (Aug. 26, 1801-Dec. 4, 1859) had ten known children by him. They were Col. Quince R. Nolan (Nov. 18, 1824-Jan. 1, 1876), Thomas Lovic Nolan (Mar. 6, 1827-Jul 13, 1887), Rev. Jackson Parisode Nolan (Jan. 25, 1830-Aug. 27, 1896), Judge Ludy Kelsey Nolan (Jan 18, 1832-Oct. 1, 1908), Col. George Marcellus Nolan (Apr. 26, 1834-Nov. 9, 1906), Davis Jasper Nolan (Apr. 18, 1838-1838), Martha Ann Amelia Nolan (Apr. 22, 1840-Mar. 9, 1880), Talitha Cox Nolan (May 15, 1843-1843), Alonzo Antoine Nolan (Jan. 10, 1845-Feb. 13, 1927), and James Emory Nolan, CSA (Oct. 2, 1847-Mar. 1, 1918). 

Mary Thompson “Polly” Browder (1779-April 23, 1793), daughter of Thomas Isham and Talitha Browder, was named after the only “mother” Thomas Isham Browder ever really knew.  She married Richard Thrasher (c1775-Apr. 22, 1793), probably in 1792.  This couple had two children (one, Nancy Cox, born about 1792, and the other, whose name and gender we do not know, early in 1793).  Nancy Cox Thrasher (c1792-1809/mentioned in the Will of Joseph Cloud Thrasher, dated June 5, 1807, and cited as deceased in the estate settlement of same), died without issue, leaving an estate administered by Isham C. Browder and William J. Meador. The other child died the same day as his/her mother. 

A very sad endnote to this family’s story is the tragic murder of Richard Thrasher and his wife, Mary Thompson Browder-Thrasher, in 1793.  This couple had moved to Greene County, Georgia soon after their marriage.  On April 22nd, Native Americans, having already burned Greensborough, suddenly attacked the Richard Thrasher home, murdering Richard and a slave woman outright!  Mary fled with her infant of only five or six weeks of age, managing to reach a nearby river.  But she was caught, brutally dealt with, and scalped!  She was eventually found by a scouting party clinging to a bush.  The infant was also found, having drowned in the river nearby. 

It is said that Mary lived for about 24 hours, suffering greatly, but remaining in pleasant disposition.  She is also said to have been 25 years of age at the time, but she was much younger than that (actually about 14).  This is demonstrated by the fact that she is not mentioned in the Will of Thomas Isham Browder, written in 1779, so she could have been born later in 1779, but not before said Will was written.  And she was certainly born prior to Nancy Washington Browder (b. 1781) in any case.  Although her birth date is an estimate, I stand by that estimated birth date. 

For this couple to have had two children prior to, or by, 1793, they would have had to have been born in 1792/93.  Their daughter, Nancy Cox Thrasher (c1792-1809), was the only survivor (White, Historical Collection of Georgia, p. 479).  The actual newspaper account reads as follows:

This county suffered much from the Indians. Besides burning Greensborough, the savages committed many other acts of violence.

On the 22 of April, 1793, the Indians, numbering thirty-seven, made a sudden attack upon the house of Mr. Richard Thrasher, two children, and a Negro woman. Mrs. Thrasher, to avoid, if possible, the fate with which she was threatened, fled with her infant, five or six weeks old, in her arms, and reached the river. The savages pursued her, shot her through each thigh and the right breast, stabbed her in the left breast with a knife, cut her arm nearly off, and then scalped her. In this dreadful situation she remained until the neighbors could assemble in sufficient numbers to cross the river and pursue the Indians. As the first canoe was crossing, she had strength sufficient to call for assistance. The poor woman was found hanging by a bush, in water nearly up to her chin, her infant at the bottom of the river, a few yards from her. She lived nearly twenty-four hours and when informed by her physician that it was impossible for her to survive much longer, she, with a fortitude that is rarely to be met with called her friends around her, and in a calm manner gave her hand to every one, wishing them a better fate than that which had befallen her and her family. This lady was twenty-five years old, of Highly respectable connections, handsome, and well educated.

It is interesting to note that this article states that Mary was “pretty” and “well-educated”.  It is obvious, then, that Talitha had indeed ensured that her children, including her daughters, were educated, just as her deceased husband had asked her to do.  It seems reasonable to believe that, although the children’s education likely took place under various venues, Talitha was probably their main educator herself.  And it is also reasonable to assume that Talitha was devastated when she received the news of this family’s murder (for one has to presume that she got the news somehow).  Talitha probably never got over it.

Nancy Washington Browder (c1781-c1815), daughter of Thomas Isham and Talitha Browder, married (1) Cloud Thrasher (c1777-1802).  It can be reasonably postulated that Nancy’s birth date fell soon after the British surrender at Yorktown to Gen. George Washington on October 19, 1781.  Therefore, like so many others, she was named, in part, after the victorious Gen. George Washington. 

Cloud and Nancy Thrasher had four known children (one named Isham Thrasher, mentioned in the Will of his grandfather, Joseph Cloud Thrasher, dated June 5, 1807, but not mentioned in his estate settlement in 1810), all of whom, according to court documents, died as minors with no issue.  

The known children of Cloud and Nancy W. Thrasher were Joseph (c1798-by 1801), Nancy Cox (c1800-by 1801), Isham (c1801-c1809), and Harbert “Herbert” C. (c1802-1820). .  

Nancy W. Browder later married (2) William Fears, Jr. (January 31, 1774, Virginia-February 9, 1821, Greene County, Georgia), son of William Fears, Sr., on August 8, 1809 in Athens, Clarke County, Georgia (Marriage Book A, 1805-1821).  William Fears, Jr. had first married (1) Mary “Polly” Griffin (b. September 11, 1781, Powhatan County, Virginia), daughter of John Griffin and Mary Ann Andrews, on January 25, 1802.

The children of William and Mary Fears were Jesse Griffin (February 3, 1805-January 11, 1901), William Sadler (January 31, 1807-January 3, 1903), and Edna Ann (b. c1808).  The son of William and Nancy Fears was Augustine (often called “Augustus”) Browder “Buck” Fears (August 9, 1810-February 9, 1879). 

Augustine B. Fears, again, is mentioned in the court documents concerning the estate of Thomas Isham Browder.  He is also named several times in the Will of his father, William, dated October 27, 1820.  An important mention reads,

I give and bequeath to my son Augustin B. Fears One Negro woman named Liddy and her increase if any also two hundred dollars of my Estate.  Also the rightful proportion of his GRANDFATHER BROWDER (sic) Estate of North Carolina coming to me for him in right of his Mother.  I bequeath the same to Augustin B. Fears as his right. 

It seems obvious that William Fears had not actually seen, nor read, the Will of Thomas Isham Browder.  Had he read said Will, he would undoubtedly have known that there was no bequeathment to Nancy W. Browder.  He obviously expected something, but, according to court documents, never received anything.  Please note that this Will was made about eight years prior to the death of Talitha Browder. 

Finally, William Fears, Jr. married (3) Joicy T. Rainey, on December 4, 1817, after Nancy’s death. 

William Sadler Fears first married (1) Levicy Smith, and later, (2) Sarah Kleckly-Whitsel, widow of John Whitsel.

It is interesting to note that, following Alexander Campbell’s (a founder of the Disciples of Christ Church) visit to Augusta, Georgia, Nathan Williamson Smith (1813-1899) and Dr. Daniel Hook rode together to evangelize Georgia.  In 1845, while at the County Line Church in Fayette County, they met the half-brothers, William Sadler Fears and Augustine Browder Fears.  Augustine was already a Methodist preacher.  These two brothers had moved to the Hampton area some years prior to this time and had recently heard of the work of Smith at County Line.  The two half-brothers had traveled together 25 miles from their homes in Hampton, Georgia to hear Smith preach.

Thereafter the two helped to found Berea Christian Christ Church at Berea, in Hampton, Georgia, in 1854.  This passage not only shows that the Augustine Browder Fears family resided in Hampton, Georgia, but also that they converted to the faith of the Disciples of Christ.  It seems possible that this Augustine Browder Fears had already known the Campbell family, perhaps through Augustine C. Browder, and was, therefore, previously acquainted with them and, perhaps, early influenced by Alexander Campbell himself.

Augustine Browder Fears (August 9, 1810-February 9, 1879) married Mary A. unknown (b. c1817).  He also served as a Private in the 30th Regiment of the Georgia Infantry, CSA, during the American Civil War.

The 1850 census of Henry County, Georgia (District 42) lists A. B. Fears (40), farmer, along with his wife, M. A. (33) along with children; S. L. (11), E. S. (10), W. C. (5), C. P. (3), and A. G. (0).  This census shows that A. B. was born in Georgia, that M. A. was born in Alabama, and that the children were born in Georgia.  Next on the same page is listed the family of Wm. Griffin (65).

The 1860 census of Campbell, Georgia lists Augustus Fears (48), farmer, along with his wife, Mary A. (42) and children; Walter (14), Othelia S. (18), Castella (12), Antonett (11), Mary N. (8), and Ada O. (1).  This census shows that “Augustus” was born in North Carolina and that all other family members were born in Alabama.

A diary entry (2nd diary, 1866-1867), dated February 5, 1866 (p. 55), of Ann Elizabeth Browder, daughter of Isham C. and Martha Browder, mentions Augustine, but not by name.  It reads, in part, as follows:

Mr. Fears, a cousin of Pa’s, came Saturday morning.  He is a minister and lives near Atlanta—he is going through the country getting contributions for about two hundred families who were left entirely destitute by the armies that were there for months—they all fled from their homes taking with them nothing but what they wore, and when they returned found nothing but their lands-their houses burnt down in a great many instances—fences used for firewood—all household furniture destroyed-not a bed, or a chicken or plow left.  It makes my heart bleed to hear of it. 

The 1870 census of Fayette, Georgia lists Aug. B. Fears (60), farmer, along with his wife, Mary A. (53) and children; Antionette (20), Mary N. (17), and Ada (10).  This census states that “Aug.” was born in Georgia, that Mary was born in Alabama, and that the children were born in Georgia. 

The 1880 census of Campbell County, Georgia (District 499) lists Mary A. Fears (63), widowed, along with children; Mary (28), and Ida O. (21).  This census shows that Mary A. was born in Alabama, that her father was born in Virginia and that her mother was born in Georgia.  Both children and their father are listed as having been born in Georgia and their mother is listed as having been born in Alabama.

Augustine B. Fears is buried in Red Oak Cemetery at Union City, Campbell (later Fulton) County, Georgia (U.S. Veterans Gravesites, ca. 1775-2006).

The known children of Augustine B. and Mary A. Fears were S. L. (b. c1839), Othelia. S. (b. c1841), Walter C. (b. c1845), Castella P. (b. c1847), Antonette G. (b. 1850), Mary N. (b. c1852), and Ada/Ida O. (b. c1859).

Mary N. Fears, daughter of Augustine B. and Mary A. Fears, married unknown Waters.  The 1885 census of Marion County, Florida (District 11) lists Mary N. Waters (33).  This census states that Mary and both of her parents were born in Georgia.

Augustine Claiborne Browder, Esquire (c1782-1816), son of Thomas Isham and Talitha Browder, was obviously named after Col. Augustine Claiborne of King William County, Virginia.  He married Elizabeth “Betsey” Booth (c1782-c1844), about 1800.  He was born in Guilford County, North Carolina and died in Rockingham County, North Carolina. 

I have not been able to find Betsey’s parentage, but believe that she must have been descended from Thomas Booth of Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  It is interesting to note that the original property of this Thomas Booth, called “Fairfield”, is still owned by his descendants today.  Thomas Booth purchased the property, of 400 acres, in 1725, which was located on Buckskin Creek, near Petersburg.  The present home there was built in 1828.  It was used by Gen. U. S. Grant during the American Civil War as a headquarters following the battle of Five Forks, which occurred from March 31, 1865 to April 2, 1865 (Jones, p. 224).

Augustine C. Browder was a Justice of the Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions and a tax collector.  In Rockingham County Court Minutes, by 1815, Augustine is listed as a Justice of the County Court. 

He also served as a witness, along with Isaiah Hancock and Collester Johnston, to a deed in which Collester Johnston sold 100 acres of land to Isaac Thrasher on January 27, 1808 (RCDB N, 1808-1810, pp. 55-56). 

During the November term of the Rockingham County court in 1809, Augustine was appointed foreman of the Grand Jury.  During the February 1810 session of the Court, Augustine was appointed as overseer of the road from the fork of Browder’s Road to Wolf Island Meeting House.

The 1810 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina lists “Augustin” Browder (26-44), along with his wife (26-44), and children; two males (under 10), and two females (under 10).  Also listed are two slaves.  His family is listed on the same page as Isham Browder, his brother. 

During the May session of the county court in 1813, Augustine was appointed as overseer of the road from Major William Bethell’s to the home of his brother, Isham Browder.  During the same session, Augustine, along with Andrew Foster, James Rawley and James Watt, were appointed to judge the next election for the moderator to serve in the Grand Assembly of North Carolina. 

During the May 1814 session, Augustine Browder, along with James Watt, was appointed to make a list of taxable property for 1814.  In addition, he, along with Andrew Foster, James Rawley, and James Watt were appointed as judges to ensure the election for the members of the next State General Assembly.  During the November 1814 session, Philip Brisenderie was appointed as overseer of the road leading from Col. William Bethell’s to Browder’s Crossroads, with the help of Augustine C. Browder and others. 

In February of 1815, Augustine C. Browder, Esquire, took the oath as a Justice of the Peace of the county court, and took his seat on the bench (although he may well have been a justice prior to this).  The May 1815 session selected Augustine as a judge concerning the election of s for that year, along with other esquires; John Forrest and John Morehead.  During the August session of the court, Augustine C. Browder, and other justices, returned their lists of taxable properties for 1815.  During the November 1815 session, Abraham Philips, John Forest, Augustine C. Browder, John Morehead, and William Bethell, Esquires, were appointed as a committee to settle with John Ellington, Executor of the estate of Daniel Tucker, dcsd., and report to the Court. 

The 1815 Rockingham County tax list shows that Augustine C. Browder had 808 acres of land, with 7 black poles.  In addition, Augustine C. Browder was granted a deed of trust (RCDB Q, p. 118) for two slaves to James Campbel, Jr. for payment of Sterling Ruffin on November 30, 1815.  Augustine’s signature had to be proven in court as he was deceased by the May Session of 1816 when this deed of trust was presented. 

So, Augustine died between Nov. 30, 1815 and May of 1816. His cause of death is not known. What happened to Augustine is something I have always wondered about, but I have never found anything that showed why or how he suddenly died.  But it is intriguing that he obviously died after November of 1815 and before the May session of the county court the next year, recalling the almost exact scenario for his father, Isham.  Augustine C. Browder is likely buried in Wolf Island Primitive Baptist Church Cemetery.

During the February session of the Superior Court, something interesting is noted, but the sense of the issue at hand is not obvious.  The Court decreed that money be refunded to Talitha Browder, which, apparently, at first was to be refunded to Augustine Browder (but Talitha is the one the money actually came from in the first place).  The money had, apparently, been posted as bail for John Lynn.  What this was all about is unclear from this record, but what is clear is the statement that Talitha Browder was the mother of Augustine Browder.  It does seem from this document that Augustine may still be alive.  So, he likely would have died between February and May of 1816. 

During the May session of the court in 1816, Elizabeth Browder and George Wilson were made administrators of the estate of Augustine C. Browder, dcsd.  Bond of $750.00 was made with George L. Wilson, William Bethell, and Thomas Hill.  The court ruled that they be allowed to sell his personal estate. 

On December 18, 1816, Elizabeth Browder, Augustine’s widow, entered into a deed of trust with George S. Wilson for monies paid to her by Green Berry Wilson, Jr. (RCDB Q, p. 282).  This deed was witnessed by Isaiah Hancock.  It was proven during the February session of 1817.  And, also in 1817, Elizabeth Browder, Administratrix of the estate of Augustine Browder, deceased, deeded two slaves to James Campbell (RCDB Q, p. 417).

The 1820 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina lists Elizabeth Browder (26-44), along with children; one male (16-25), one male (10-15), one male (under 10), one female (16-25), one female (10-15), and one female (under 10).  That year Elizabeth owned four male slaves and one female slave.

The 1830 census of Rockingham County lists Elizabeth Browder (40-49), along with two other females (15-19), one male (20-29), and one male (15-19).  That year ten male and four female slaves are also listed.

Elizabeth Browder is not listed as a head of household in any 1840 census and is not listed at all in the 1850 census anywhere in the nation.  

The children of Augustine C. and Elizabeth Browder were Talitha Cox (c1800-c1836), Nancy Washington (1801-c1836), Maj. Milton A. (January 17, 1802-July 25, 1859), Maj. Isham Claiborne (1805-1869), Ann Elizabeth “Eliza” (b. c1812), and Wilson Augustine “Augustus” (b. c1814).  From Minute Docket in Court of Pleas and Quarters, Session from May Term 1819 to November Term 1822, p. 4:

Ordered by the court that Elizabeth Browder be guardian for Milton A. Browder, Talitha C. Browder, Isham Browder, Nancy W. Browder, Augustine W. Browder, Eliza Browder, orphans of Augustine C. Browder; she entering into bond in the sum of $3000.00, with TALITHA BROWDER security.

On October 31, 1834, Elizabeth “Betsey” Browder, Milton A. Browder, Isham Claiborne Browder, Nathaniel and Talitha Roach, Nancy W. Browder, and Wilson A. Browder, et. al., sold 689 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, to Luke Palmer (RCDB 2ndG, p. 4).  Then in late 1836, about 23 years after the death of Augustine, Elizabeth Browder, along with her living children, moved to Barbour County, Alabama, where they all probably later died.  

Barbour County had been created on December 18, 1832 from former Creek territory and from part of Pike County.  It was probably about that time that the family began thinking about moving there.  There, these hardy and enterprising Browder pioneers helped establish Fort Browder in 1836, which served as a stockaded fort for settlers during the last Creek War in the area, as well as being utilized by local militia and which was named after Isham C. Browder, who became a prominent planter in the area.  Hostilities with local Native Americans was a very frightening fact of life there during those times, so a fort was necessary for settlers.  From RCDB 2nd G, p. 4 (Copy by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

Luke Palmer from Elizabeth Browder, Deed:

Talitha Cox Browder (c1800-1836), daughter of Augustine C. and Elizabeth Browder, married Nathaniel Roach, Sr. (c1797-June 18, 1860), son of James Roach, Sr. (May 27, 1753-August 23, 1841) and Ruth Spiers (c1757-June 9, 1817), on March 2, 1825, in Rockingham County, North Carolina (RCMBB B, p. 221/Ingmire, p. 60).  The bondsman was Richard Jarrell, Jr. and the witness was G. Poindexter. 

James Roach, Sr. was born at Guilford Heights, Surry County, Virginia and died in Rockingham County, North Carolina.

The 1830 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina lists Nathaniel Roach (20-29), along with his wife (20-29), and presumed children; one male (under 5), and one female (under 5).  Also listed is one male slave (10-23).  Listed on the same page are the families of James Roach, Sr., Richard Browder, and Isaiah Hancock.

It is understood that Talitha Browder-Roach is buried in Wolf Island Creek Primitive Baptist Church Cemetery, and she did die prior to the rest of her family’s move to Alabama, probably as a result of complications from child-birth.  I do suspect that other researchers may have mixed her up with Tabitha Roach, however, who has a headstone in said cemetery.  In any case, the Nathaniel Roach family moved on to Barbour County, Alabama prior to 1840, so the move would not have taken place long after her death. 

Again, the gravestone of Tabitha Roach in the same cemetery is not Talitha Cox Browder-Roach, but is instead the wife of Thomas Roach, who was Tabitha Caldwell (Feb. 1, 1799-July 1, 1862).  They are buried next to one another. 

The 1840 census of Barbour County, Alabama lists Nathaniel Roach (40-49), along with one male (20-29), one male (10-14), one male (under 5), one female (10-14), and one female (5-9).  Also listed are slaves; one female (36-54), two females (10-23), one female (under 10), one male (36-54), one male (24-35), and two males (10-23).  Also listed on the same page is the family of M. A. Browder (30-39).  But, obviously, Talitha is not listed in this census, which shows that she had already died.

Nathaniel Roach later married (2) Nancy Scarborough on December 24, 1843 in Barbour County, Alabama. 

The 1850 census of Barbour County (Division 23) lists Nathaniel Roach (50), farmer, along with his new wife, Nancy (42), and children; Milton (21), Nathaniel (13), Sarah (10), Ann (6), and Perry (0).  Also listed is David F. Scarborough (16).  This census states that Nathaniel and his children, Milton and Nathaniel were all born in North Carolina.  His wife, Nancy, is listed as having been born in Georgia as is David Scarborough.  The other children are listed as having been born in Alabama.

The known children of Nathaniel and Talitha C. Roach were two unknown children born about 1826 and 1828 respectively, Dr. Milton A. When I think of the past two months I can hardly realize what all has passed—it seems as though it is a blank in my existence. But then I know it is all real—it was God’s will to take one that was almost as dear as a father. Yes, he’s gone-he can never come again-I know it-and as though this will not be the end of all our troubles. To think of Aunt Mary, who doesn’t look as if she could possibly survive him much longer. She has now gone down to St. Andrew’s Bay, in company with Cousin Jane and Dr. Harrison Shipp and Ruth. She had to go in the carriage, as she can’t travel only very early in the morning and at night.

It is unknown to me what the circumstances may have been, however, Isham C. Browder applied for a special pardon on September 12, 1865.  No doubt, it had something to do with the American Civil War.  Also, the Alabama tax lists of Division 8, Collection District 1, list I. C. Browder as being taxed for two carriages, two gold watches, and one Pianoforte.  So, this family was still relatively well-off following the war. 

That said, his daughter, Ann Elizabeth, in her second diary (1866-1867), makes several remarks about the difficulties of interacting with “Negroes” during that time-period.  Obviously, everything is seen from only her point of view here.  But, she does say, in an entry dated January 15, 1866, that her father was “disgusted with the whole Negro race.  He had a great deal of trouble contracting with them.  He kept most of the old set”. 

Later, in an entry (2nd diary), dated March 16, 1866 (p. 62), Ann Elizabeth Browder mentions that, “Pa has bought him a lot of goods.  He is going to have him a little store at the Plantation-it seems so funny-we call him Mr. Storeman”. 

Later, in an entry, dated July 28, 1867 (pp. 77-78), she drew a picture of how life had changed for them, and how bad it had become for all during the Reconstruction Era.  It reads:

Hart came late last night. He has not been well in two or three weeks-he looks a little thin. It seems a pity that we cannot all live together when there are so few fo us. Someone though is obliged to attend to the plantation-we made no crop last year, and there are so many security debts hanging over us. If the crops fail this year we will have to give up everything. We are living very economically this year, spending just as little as possible. The country is in a terrible state now—no money—riots among the negroes and whites-the negroes cannot be controlled-they do not half work-they quit when they please-say what they please to us-a great many think there will be another war. I cannot bear to think of it. 

And, in an entry dated August 2nd (p. 78):

Pa brought Hart home from the plantation yesterday morning sick-he had a chill the day before and was very ill indeed-a burning fever after the chill, and delirious-no medicine on the place-was receiving no attention from the servants when Pa reached there. He sent off immediately for a physician, but did not succeed in getting one. He has had the highest fever all the evening and been so sick. I have been so sorry for him-I have been nursing him. I hope he will be well in a few days-he has exposed himself to the sun too much. 

Thankfully, “Hart” did recover.  The known children of Isham C. and Mary A. Browder were Ann Elizabeth (Jan. 18, 1843-Nov. 10, 1880), Hartwell “Hart” Hill Traver (January 30, 1845-p. 1910), Milton (c1846-bef. 1858), and Isham (c1849-bef. 1858).  As shown, both Milton and Isham died as infants.  They are not mentioned at all in Ann’s extant diaries, which begin in 1858.  Also, let us remember that “Anne Elizabeth” was a name that was common in the Isham family.  In addition, Hatwell’s birth date comes from her second diary (p. 55). 

Ann Elizabeth Browder (January 18, 1843-November 10, 1880), daughter of Isham C. and Mary A. Browder, attended “college” (presumably the “Union College” she mentions in her diaries) and studied several subjects, according to said diaries.  I read them and they are very interesting.  For me, one of her most fascinating journal entries is the following from her first diary, dated November 3, 1858 (p. 9):

Yesterday when I reached the college, I heard the girls laughing and talking about a report that they had heard about Lucy Patterson and Mr. Veal running away last night to get married. I think when such children think of marrying it is just because they do not know any better but before I proceed any further I will correct the grand mistake I made on the opposite page. I said children, but that was my pen that caused me to write it, for Lucy Patterson is eighteen, and six months on the next year. She had better take my advice, which is to come to school and gain knowledge before she thinks of marrying. 

For me, this is a wonderful example of an educated young woman during a time when most did not consider the education of girls and women to be of much importance.  But, again, she was descended from a family line that held education to be important for all.  

In addition to the above quote, on May 9, 1859, she wrote “The relative value of Good sense and Beauty (is) in the female sex” (p. 29).  She also mentions Ft. Browder often, including one, dated December 22, 1858, in which she mentioned a party being thrown there by the young men, which she had been invited to. 

The lady was conflicted in many ways, according to her own writings.  She was ill quite often, and even though she craved love, she was almost determined never to marry (and had several suitors all at once over the years).  But, after a non-and-off courtship of several years, Maj. Pruett finally got up the courage to ask her to marry him.  Here is the diary entry for that date, October 1, 1867 (p. 83): 

I was surprised yesterday evening to receive a note from Maj. Pruett asking permission to call last night. He came, and looked handsomer than I ever saw him and was pleasenter. This afternoon I had another note from him asking me to take a buggy ride with him this evening-and said he had something to tell me. I knew what it was, and did not want to go, but I could not decline, and I knew if I did it would be putting off only a short while an unpleasant meeting. I went-the secret was a declaration of love, and a question, “Miss Ann be my wife? [one has to wonder here whether she could barely write it or whether he could barely say it]. We were considerably excited. I could not think so I gave him no answer. When he bade me good-bye his hand was cold like ice-I felt it through my kid glove. He will be back next Monday to know his fate-what it will be I cannot say. I have been sick all day-a terribly weakening sick headache caused from not sleeping last night.

Then, in an entry dated October 20, 1867 (p. 83), only this:

Engaged to be married to Maj. Pruett! How strange it is-I cannot realize it.

Other entries concerning their upcoming marriage, as well as other things, including their early lives together, follow.  After moving the date from December 3rd to the 10th, Ann Elizabeth Browder married Maj. The known children of Richard A. and Martha A. Ellington were Alfred Jackson (c1850-1922), Martha “Pattie” Widdell (b. c1852), and Richard Miller B. (c1855-1913).

Richard M. B. Ellington (b. c1855) married Pattie J. unknown (b. c1857).  Their known child was Richard L. Ellington (b. October 1879).

James Sterling Whittemore, Sr., son of William M. and Martha Whittemore, left the Wentworth area, moving to Alabama.  He married Betty Rand.  This couple had a son, James Sterling Whittemore, Jr. (1876-1959) who married Carrie Alma Sumner (1896-1974).  Their son, Bill Whittemore of Oklahoma, collected the genealogical material, with the help of his wife, Margaret Phyllis Isaac Whittemore (a native of England), which is now in the library above cited. 

George Settle Whittemore (c1859-1932), son of William M. and Martha Whittemore, married Emma Pickard (1862–1946).  They resided in Alamance County, North Carolina. 

They had at least four sons, Floyd Sullivan (Feb. 21, 1886–July 27, 1956), Isham Browder (Nov. 12, 1891-Jan. 17, 1969), Clyde Atwood (March 21, 1894–June 10, 1964), and James Minor (Nov. 29, 1900–Oct. 13, 1972). 

Floyd Sullivan Whittemore married Martha Pearl Huffines, they making their living by farming.  Isham Browder Whittemore married Lottie Ray (June 7, 1894–Nov. 7, 1965) and served as a deputy sheriff.  Clyde Atwood Whittemore married Madeline Ray (Nov. 27, 1895-July 10, 1944).  He worked as a railroad employee.  James Minor Whittemore married Pearl Winfrey (Oct. 10, 1901-July 18, 1993).  All of these families resided in Alamance County, North Carolina. 

Isham C. Browder, Esquire (c1774-1832), son of Thomas Isham and Talitha Browder and my ancestor, will be dealt with separately. However, I will state that his middle name was most likely “Coleman”. Also, that he married his cousin, Martha Browder (c1774-c1811), daughter of Richard Browder and Mary Eppes Randolph, in 1799.
