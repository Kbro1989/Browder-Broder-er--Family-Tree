On Surnames and Coats of Arms

Posted byjmarkbrowderApril 21, 2020January 2, 2025Posted inUncategorized

It seems to me, because I am still being asked certain questions that I thought I had clarified for everyone, that it is time for what I hope will be a good-natured rant. See, there are times when you just realize that you still haven’t got your point across on something and you have to figure out another way to try and do that. So, here it is.

As in most cases where genealogy and family history are concerned, I concentrate on my own family name. That is not only the case because I am a Browder, but it is also the case because it is from that family line that most people’s questions still come from. I apologize for the fact that this is sometimes frustrating for me, and I sometimes let that be known a bit. The fact of the matter is that, with most questions, the information is, in fact, out there, and is easily found. The problem, however, is that there is also still a lot of misinformation out there that causes people to still have questions. I truly wish all of the misinformation could be eliminated, and I do think this will indeed happen over time. But, in the mean-time, I have also seen some of this misinformation get spread on brand-new posts, all because the person who made the post did not do due diligence when doing, um, “research”. It is literally like trying to stop a virus. I think we all have some idea how difficult that is now, due to recent events. Once it is out there, it is nearly impossible to stop or even contain. Hence, the meaning of “going viral”.

But, please don’t think that this is a recent phenomenon that has been happening only since the invention of the internet. No, it has been happening for a long time. An author would write something in a book, admittedly doing the best that he/she could, speculating about something, and on down the line people started taking it as fact. It’s very much like an old Pentecostal revival or tent meeting, during which, in one particular instance, a certain woman read one passage from the Bible that said people should be baptized in the name of Jesus, and from that she and others extrapolated that this was the only formula that people should follow, and created a whole denomination based on that. Never mind that other passages provided a variant formula of baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost – for these people, that simply MEANS the name of Jesus. And you can’t talk them out of it, because they have completely accepted this without reservation.

The same sort of thing has happened with reference to the Browder surname. Some author many years ago speculated, based on the evidence she had at the time (which isn’t everything we have today) that the Browder surname might have originated in Ireland and might be related to the Irish family name “Broderick”. But, because of this speculation, other people, later, who read her book, took that as fact and have run with it ever since. And you can’t hardly dissuade any of these people from the idea that the Browder family is indigenously Irish, no matter what sort of evidence you present. It has practically become a point of religious belief for them. It simply does not matter to them whether you find evidence to the contrary or not, since they have always believed this, they will continue to do so. And, after all, they themselves can find evidence for their point on the internet!

This is just a small picture, demonstrating what I have been up against in trying to correct things over the years. It is literally, probably, going to take a couple of generations for actual facts to be accepted by most people. But, let me make the effort once again in a way that I have not before.

Let us begin with the concept and development of surnames. Now, I have found my studies in history, generally, to have been of great help when doing genealogical research, and vice-versa. I, personally, think that anyone should have a good grasp not only of history in general, but also of family history, so that they can see how history unfolded and understand at least a little bit of how their own ancestors played a part. In that light, the combination of general history with family history becomes important if one is to truly understand how things unfolded.

Surnames are actually NOT a recent development in history. People had surnames way back in ancient times. Not every culture did, but some did, most notably the Romans. For the Roman, their family name, or surname, was of utmost importance to them. It literally tied them to their family, and to their inheritance. A Roman of the upper classes, especially, would likely have as many as 3, 4, or 5 actual names. So, he might have a name like Pious Opus Castellus Paaetextatus Magnus, or whatever. His family name, or surname, was Praetextatus. His given name was Pious (that is what the couple would have named him when he was born – “What should we call him? Let’s call him Pious”). All the other names followed from family names on his father’s and mother’s sides of the family automatically, with the exception of Magnus, which he would have earned for doing something great. So, with the Roman of noble birth, at least, if you knew all of his names, you automatically knew EXACTLY who he was. You already knew who his family was and everything. There could be no mistake.

Again, that was not the case with most other societies. Most people back in ancient, and on into medieval, times were lucky if they had one single name. Alfred, or whatever. Some children never were actually named, but came to be called something, which became their name. When I was a kid, I was often called “big ears” because I heard literally EVERYTHING. Back in ancient or early medieval times, that might have become my actual name, because there was nothing else to designate me by. Or, I might have made a name for myself by the things that I did. If I did something great, like conquer a certain city, I might begin to be called by that city’s name, with the reminder of “otherwise known as ‘big ears’”. That way, I would have still been identified as the guy who used to be called “big ears”, but was now known as Antinopolus, because that was the city I had conquered.

One knows that surnames had not developed, at least not very much, in ancient times in most cultures, because practically everyone in the Bible is called by only one name. Most of them literally only had that one name. They were often differentiated by the addition of a designation as to where they were from. Joseph of Aramathea is one of these. There were probably a thousand Josephs who were from that city at the same time as this guy lived, by the way, but he is the only one who made it into the Bible. Thus, he is forever known in this manner. As for the Romans mentioned in the New Testament, they are given only one name, not because they didn’t have more than one name (because we know they had several names), but because the biblical writer either didn’t know their full names, or didn’t care. But, trust me, Governor Felix had more than just the name “Felix”.

Anyway, once the Roman Empire fell, surnames generally came into disuse in Europe and surrounding areas. The barbarians who helped to cause the fall generally didn’t have surnames, and the ones who did generally only had that, plus their given name – thus two names rather than 3, 4, or 5. Some people continued to carry on their surnames, especially in Italy, but this was still a rarity, mainly among the nobility. Everybody else, again, was reduced to being lucky if they had one given name. So, Francus was exactly that – Francus. That’s all.

Slowly, over time, surnames began to develop once again during late medieval times. Most surnames developed from a person’s trade, so they were trade names. For example – Smith. This name came from the fact that the person who adopted it for himself, or came to be called by it, was, by trade, a smith. He forged items from iron and other metals. That’s what he did for a living, so he came to be designated by that term. The trouble with the surname Smith is that, since people worked as smiths all over the world, not to mention Europe itself, the term “smith” came to be used as a surname by a wide variety of people, many of whom were in no way whatsoever related to each other. John Smythe who lived in London, England was in no way related to Yohann Smithus who lived in Rome, Italy. But, over time, many, many families who adopted this term for their surname, regardless of variants in spelling based on language and culture, began to spell their name simply as “Smith”, because everybody else was doing this and it was just easier to fit in that way. So, if your surname is Smith, you may have a rather hard time tracing your family tree, because (1) there are so many Smiths in the world and (2) you don’t know, without a LOT of research just where your name should start being spelled “Smythe” or something else.

What that means, in a nutshell, is that we Browders are VERY lucky with our name. Why? Well, let me explain, first, that the Browder surname also comes from a trade, like the Smith surname does. One would think, then, that the same sorts of problems would exist for us too. But, they don’t. The simple reason for this is because the Browder surname was not adopted by all that many people at its beginning. We can see this by the progression of the name, which I drew out in both of my books. In so many words, we don’t have someone in Tuscany, Italy adopting the Browder surname, while at the same time having someone in Glasgow, Scotland adopting the same name because they had the same trade. It just didn’t happen that way for us.

But, might some unrelated families in different areas of Europe have adopted SIMILAR names, based on the same trade? YES. And, just as in the case of “Smith”, they would have been spelled differently. But, the fact of the matter is that I have found no actual evidence that this did, in fact, take place. It COULD have, but there is no evidence for it. There is ample evidence concerning the various spellings of “Smith” in different areas of Europe, but there isn’t for “Browder”.

What the meagre evidence that we do have demonstrates is that the Browder surname popped up in one place originally, and one place ONLY. And it wasn’t Ireland. We KNOW this because we have the documentation to prove it now, whereas, in earlier times, this documentation had not been found by others. So, they simply made mistakes because they did not have enough documentation. One cannot fault them for that because they did the best they could with what they had, just as I also do. BUT, we can fault those who continue to perpetuate the incorrect information when the correct information is out there and so easily accessible – because I have put it out there all over the internet. I KNOW it’s out there for the taking.

Anyway, getting back to our surname – The records that we do have demonstrate that Flemish weavers/embroiderers emigrated to England to ply their trade at the King’s invitation. There were lots of them. Many of them would have been related because relatives generally remained in the same trade. That’s just how it was back in medieval times. Eventually, some of these people began to be called by their given name, as well as being designated by their trade. This would ONLY have taken place with reference to the patriarch of the family, however, because he would have been the only one who generally interacted with the rest of society. The rest of the family simply worked and generally remained home. That’s just how it was back in medieval times. And every member of the family, generally, had some part in the same trade as the patriarch did. So, they were all in it together as a family unit.

Thus, the patriarch, who would have become fairly well-known in the local community, would have been known as, for example, William the Browderer, because “browdering” was his (and his family’s) trade. He was a “browderer”. His family members were browderers too, but they would not have been known in the wider community and would not necessarily have been designated as such if they even left home to, say, go shopping. Nobody would really have known them unless they were introduced. But, they would have been around, or with, the patriarch, so other people would have been able to know that they were of the same family.

During this time-period, inheritance was almost always a one-way street. The eldest son was automatically going to inherit practically everything – everything of any importance. That included the family name. So, the patriarch of the family, from the birth of this son, would have been grooming him to take over the family business. As he came to maturity and got married and began a family, he also would have been designated by his trade name just as his father had been. He would have, gradually, become known in the wider community also so that, when the patriarch died or could no longer do his job, this son would have been ready to take over. Over a few generations, dropping “the” from the patriarch’s designation took place, so that the grandson of William the Browderer might have been Hugh Browderer. This is how it happened.

Now, let me be clear; every son of the patriarch of any given family would have tended to continue in the same trade. But only the eldest would have inherited much of anything. Thus, the other sons would have had to, more or less, strike out on their own while continuing their associations with the rest of the family, all of them more or less working together, and sometimes also competing with each other. It would have been an interesting dance to see.

Sometimes younger sons would tend to move away to a different location so that they could become more independent. That way, they became patriarchs in their own right; still continuing the same business into which they were brought up. More than likely, this is exactly why we see the surname “Browderer” being shortened, at some point, to simply “Browder”. It was yet another way for a younger son to differentiate himself from the rest of the family, while still plying the same trade elsewhere.

Now, where exactly do we see this take place? In England, and NOWHERE ELSE. Family members from Flanders first settled in and around London where they started their trade and began to be referred to by the surname “Browderer”. Sometimes the name was also spelled “Browderere”, probably by mistake. You know, spelling was not all that important back in those days and few could do a really good job of it. So, mistakes were common. But, what we DON’T see in London and surrounding areas is the shortened version “Browder”. We only see that, for the very first time, in a marriage record from 1623 in Manchester, England.

Again, this is something that earlier researchers simply did not know about. If they had, it seems to me that all speculation about the “Irishness” of the Browder surname would never have taken place to begin with. That is not to say that people from Ireland did not emigrate to England, and vice-versa, earlier than this date. That is not at all what I am saying. What I AM saying is that there is absolutely no reason to posit that anyone with the Browder surname would have emigrated from Ireland to Manchester, England because there is no reason to correlate the Browder surname with Ireland at all that early. The actual evidence demonstrates a progression from those, in London, England, who went by the surname “Browderer”, to those in Manchester, England, who went by the surname “Browder”. You really can’t get away from that. There is absolutely no reason to posit anything else at all.

So, later, in 1664, when we find a record of a Thomas Browder who lived in Kilkenny, next to County Tipperary, Ireland, the logical progression is that members of the family from Manchester, England settled in Kilkenny, Ireland later and started their own patriarchal trade family unit there, just as members from London, England settled in Manchester, England earlier to do the same thing. Again, there is literally no reason to posit any other scenario, based on the evidence that we have. This latter family was just another later generation wishing to differentiate itself from those in Manchester, so they moved to a different location in order to do that. That’s just how it was done back then. That some of them, in order to further differentiate themselves, might have shortened their surname to “Broder” is entirely possible, and even logical. BUT, none of them had any connection whatsoever to the family Broderick, which WAS indigenously Irish. There is absolutely no reason to posit that they did. It simply does not fit.

Now, I am truly sorry that the internet is such a cesspool of misinformation, and doubly-sorry that people perpetuate misinformation there on a grand scale. People, as I see it, would much rather perpetuate a comfortable falsehood than an uncomfortable truth. That’s just human nature. People do not like to let go of cherished ideas and beliefs, especially if said ideas and beliefs have been held for a long time. So, please believe me, if there was ANY real connection with the Broderick family that could be demonstrated by actual evidence, I would gladly bring it out here and now. But, there isn’t. And that includes the fanciful idea, perpetuated all over the internet, that the Browder family can in any way lay claim to the Coat of Arms of the Broderick family. It simply is NOT ours to lay claim to, period.

When I was growing up, my grandparents had a Coat of Arms on their wall, which was supposed to have been for the Browder family. Eventually, after I asked about it, they found and let me make a copy of the certificate that had come with it. I still have a copy of that certificate. It explains that this particular Coat of Arms came from Rietstap Armorial General and also explains what the symbolism on said Coat of Arms means. It also explains that the original family member who bore this Coat of Arms is unknown. So, it is unknown who this Coat of Arms was originally designed for. But, it is known that he was a member of our family.

I should not need to even say that this Coat of Arms has no resemblance whatsoever to that of the Broderick family. It is described thus: “Une pique d’arg., ensanglantee de gu. D’arg.: au chef de sin., ch. de deux fers de pique de champ, ensanglantes de gu. Cq. Cour”, meaning, “Silver, upper third green with two heads of a pike stained with red blood”. It is also to include a crest, which description reads: “Une pique d’arg., ensanglantes de gu.”, meaning, “A silver pike head stained with red blood”. No motto is recorded with this Coat of Arms, indicating that no battle cry was registered with it. It is fairly safe to say, then, that this Coat of Arms had nothing to do with battle. What the symbolism actually means is not quite certain, but violence of some kind seems to be indicated. And, it is known that foreigners, such as Flemings, did endure persecution and violence in England at times simply because they were foreigners and Roman Catholics. This Coat of Arms, by the way, can in fact, be found on the internet, but a truly diligent search has to be made in order to do so because most of the Coats of Arms that are shown are of the Broderick family, with our name on them. I have, in fact, reached out to some of these entities, trying to get them to correct this, but they simply will not do it.

Finally, the fact that the Browder surname, correctly spelled as we do today, is found in that one instance in Ireland DOES lend credence to the idea that the family immigrated FROM Ireland to the American Colonies, but does not even suggest that the Browder surname is indigenously Irish or even related to that of the Broderick family. So, YES, our family most likely did sail from Ireland to the American Colonies. THAT is our connection to Ireland – living in Ireland for, perhaps, one or two generations, and then moving on to the Colonies. It really doesn’t have to be more complicated than that. They set out once more to differentiate themselves from those they left behind, but this time didn’t change the spelling of the name. Members who had presumably changed their name’s spelling to “Broder” did the same thing separately. It’s that simple.
