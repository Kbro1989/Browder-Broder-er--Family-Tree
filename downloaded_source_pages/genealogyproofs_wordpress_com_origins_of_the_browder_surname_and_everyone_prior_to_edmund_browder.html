Origins of the Browder Surname, and Everyone Prior to Edmund Browder

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected Jan. 1, 2025)

Speculation about the Browder surname and its origins runs rampant all across the board, especially on the internet.  Some have posited that the surname “Browder” may derive from the Germanic term, “bruder”, which meant “brother”.  While this is not impossible, there is actually no direct evidence that our surname derives from this term, although a distant link can be accepted as a possibility.  Conversely, those who hold to the supposed Irish origins of the family claim that the meaning likely has to do with an Old Norse term for brother, i.e., “bróthir”.  And it is, frankly, little wonder, as we will see, that the Browder surname appears to be of Irish origin on the surface, due to its similarity to certain other Irish surnames. Regardless, the ancestors of the family who presently go by the surname Browder actually originated on continental Europe, as will be shown.

My great-uncle, Herman Edward Browder, who had done a great deal of research on the family, and whose papers I obtained while I was still in high school, clearly believed the Browder surname to have been of Germanic origin, according to what few documents that I obtained which referred to this, and also according to my Browder grandparents and others, whom I talked with extensively during my early days of family research.  Indeed, I was fortunate to have begun my research while still young, long before most begin. 

Now, without going into any further speculation, including that which I toyed with during my early years of research, which I mainly derived from Herman’s papers, I will make the point here that all speculation is rendered moot by brand-new evidence, which completely changes the scenario for our family from anything which either myself, or others, had come up with before.  First, it will be shown that the Browder family were of the Roman Catholic faith when they entered England, rather than of some Protestant faith, as I had believed.  And, second, that the name is of occupational origin, like so many other surnames, such as Turner or Smith.  But, first, it must be shown that those who tenaciously hold to the belief that the surname Browder is an anglicized form of an Irish surname, such as “O’Bruadair” or “Broderick”, are very much mistaken.  Such a belief would posit that those who go by the surname Browder today would have originated in Ireland, and would have changed their name from O’Bruadair or Broderick or some other related Irish name, to Browder.  The fact of the matter is that there is strictly no evidence for this, no matter what some researchers may state.  In fact, it appears from the extant records that the surname Browder was spelled exactly the way it is today, even in Ireland, with the exception of the surname “Broder”, which appears to be a shortened version of the surname Browder, having simply dropped the “w”.  Thus, the fact that one can find names such as Broder in Irish records is meaningless when attempting to find the origins of the surname Browder because there is no other necessary connection here other than that someone may have dropped one letter from the name.  Once in a while in the records, even in America, one sees an error such as “Broader” or “Browden”, but never anything like a Browder/O’Bruadair/Broderick combination or correlation.  So, to blindly follow what others have postulated here is folly because it cannot be supported by documentation.

In addition, it must be emphatically stated that the Coat of Arms of the Broderick family is completely their own.  The Browder family is in no way whatsoever connected with it, regardless of what can be found on the internet. 

I have also tried, rather fruitlessly, over the years to make contact with anyone with the surname Browder in Britain or Ireland, mainly using the internet.  I have also searched modern day records, such as phone lists, available on the internet.  All of this has been fruitless.  However, I have been told that members of the family either still do reside in these areas, or did so within my own lifetime (and, according to facebook.com, they indeed do).  I have managed to make contact with two persons over the years who personally knew Browders in Ireland and Scotland.  Both of these persons stated emphatically that, first, the name is NOT very common in those areas and, second, that the Browders they knew told them that their families did NOT originate in either Ireland or Scotland.  One of these persons, my fellow unit member during deployment to Iraq in 2008-09, Vinnie King (who was brought up in Ireland), added, without prodding from me, that he had been told by the family he knew that they originated somewhere in Europe, probably the Netherlands.

Now, back to proposed Irish roots.  Those who hold to the theory that “Browder” and “Broder” are variants of “O’Bruadair” or “Broderick” seem to hold this belief based solely upon a similar spelling and pronunciation of these surnames.  Some even postulate that Broderick may mean “Rick son of Broder”.  Those who believe this will even go so far as to state that the name was originally Viking in origin, originally spelled as “Brodair”, later adding the Irish “O”, making it “O’Brodair” and still later evolving into “Broderick”.  In fact, most references to the name “O’Brodair” or “O’Bruadair” will state that the name comes from an “original Norse variant”, but will provide no evidence of this (because they can’t).

They seem to derive this idea from the fact that the Danish Viking, called Brótar/Bródir, invaded Ireland and killed the Irish King, Brian Ború, during the Battle of Clontarf in 1014.  Thus, the original Norse word from which the surname is supposed to have come.  Some also propose that Brótar/Bródir had descendants from whom the above families can trace their ancestry.  Unfortunately for Brótar/Bródir, and any descendants he could have had, he and his brother, Óspak, were hanged by the Irish after their capture following this battle (Njal’s Saga, ch. 155).  So, he likely had no descendants residing later in Ireland.  And “O’Brodair” is known to actually have been an ancient Irish surname in use well before any Norse invasions, so it does not originate from any Norse forename or variant, whatever some may postulate.  These are simply facts that must be accepted.

In addition, it is known that the surname “Broderick” is actually derived from both the Irish and the Welsh languages combined.  It is a little-known historical fact that many Welsh settled in Ireland at an early date.  Benjamin T. Hudson, Ph.D., an American Medievalist, states that Broderick can be an Anglicized form of the Welsh name “ap Rhydderch”, which means “son of Rhydderch”.  And “Rhydderch” originally meant “reddish brown”.  So, it does not mean “Rick son of Broder” as some postulate.  But in other cases, it apparently can be an anglicized form of the Irish surname O’Bruadair/ O’Brodair, which means “descendant of Bruadar” (Wikipedia.com, “Broderick”).  Even so, it does not mean “Rick son of Broder”.

Now, this would seem to indicate that these names might connect to the Viking called Brótar/Bródir.  But there is one main problem with this.  Benjamin T. Hudson, Ph.D. also states that the word “bródir” was not a proper name at all among the Norse, but that the term among them does mean “brother”.  Dr. Hudson, by the way (whom I corresponded with on this matter via email in 2013), specializes in the history of Celtic-speaking peoples in the British Isles and the Norse-Gaelic Irish Sea region during the Early and High Middle Ages, so it is obvious that he knows something of the history and languages of the time.

So, the term “brótar/bródir/brother” is not a proper name after all and it is entirely possible that the Irish didn’t even know this Viking’s real name, and simply called him by the Norse term for “brother” since he was the brother of the other Viking, Óspak, who took part in the same battle.  They were somewhat familiar with Norse words by then, after all, and would have had no trouble using a Norse/Viking term to describe one of them, especially if they had overheard someone refer to him in this way. 

This “name” itself has only been found once in the records directly concerning the Norse, and that was with reference to this Viking the Irish called Brótar/Bródir.  Later a similar name is again actually found, spelled as Bródur, only once more; this time as the actual name of a Viking King of Dublin, who was the son of Porkel.  But Bródur was killed in 1160.

Frankly, as alluring as a Viking ancestor here would be, this theory still does not pass the evidence test.  In fact, the evidence refutes, rather than supports, the belief that the Browder surname emanates from the Norse word for “brother” at all.  Pure, unemotional, reliance upon the extant evidence does not allow for this possibility, however much one may like to connect the dots here.

However, what it does allow for is, again, that the Browder surname is of Germanic origin, as Herman Browder believed.  The Germanic/Norse term “brótar/bródir/brother” is at least as good of a possibility for the distant origin of the surname Browder as the Irish surname “O’Brodair” is – actually better.  In other words, we know of a Danish Viking term (but not a proper name) that is spelled much like “Browder” already, just from this episode in history, and the Vikings were of Scandinavian origin.  Germans and Scandinavians are from the same basic gene pool and share similar names in many instances.  But, as will be seen, we have still not arrived at the actual origin of the surname Browder.  And, frankly, it must be made clear that there is actually no necessary connection between the Irish surname “O’Bruadair/O’Brodair” and the surname “Browder”, whatever the actual origin of the Browder surname may have been.  And there is no necessary connection to the surname “Broderick” either.  NONE!  This simply needs to be accepted if progress is to be made in finding the truth. 

But, again, some connect the Browder surname to surnames such as “O’Brudaire”, “O’Brudair” and “Broderick”, with no logical, necessary, connection between these names and the surname Browder, other than the fact that they possess similar spellings.  So, the “O’Brudaire” and “O’Brudair” surnames somehow became transmuted into “O’Browder” and “O’Broder” by some American researchers; a name that I have never ever seen any evidence of as a surname that anyone actually, really, possessed in any extant record.  And, again, even in the earliest extant Irish records, our surname seems to have been spelled simply “Browder”.  

If there is any fact here, it can only be that the ancestor or ancestors of the Browder family in modern day America sailed from Ireland during colonial times to settle in the American colonies, as family tradition has, in fact, always posited.  So, we Browders are not descended from the Viking called Bródir.  We have essentially no connection to the Broderick family.  And we are unlikely to be descended from the O’Bruadair/O’Brodair families.  What is likely is that we descend from some unknown individual, who probably spoke a Germanic language.

Still, it is tempting indeed to believe that we Browders could be descendants of such notable Irish figures as David O’Brudair, a seventeenth century poet who wrote works of satire concerning the Cromwellian presence in Ireland, or Father Anthony O’Bruadair, who was martyred for his faith, or even that we may have had ancestors who had some hand in founding, or at least naming, the Irish town of Ballybroder in the Parish of Kilmeen, Ireland.  Still, the links to these persons and places, even if it were somehow proven that we originally had an indigenous Irish Gaelic surname, may now be impossible to prove, or even construct, due to the lack of available records from Ireland since they were regularly white-washed, and the paper reused over and over again, thus destroying any original records. 

And I must add here that I once had a friend in the army who possessed the surname of “Bruder”.  He knew well his own ancestry and where his family had come from, and it in no way connected to Ireland or even Britain.  His family had emigrated to America from the former Yugoslavia.  Once again, then, we have a potential Eastern European connection here, if the Bruder and Browder families have any connection at all.  But, once again, we are engaging in determining things by way of similarities of names, which essentially takes the researcher nowhere.  The point, however, is that Ireland is not the only place where similar names can be found.  There is absolutely nothing that says that the Browder surname can be connected to the foregoing Irish surnames, or to an Eastern European surname like Bruder.  They simply have similar spellings and pronunciations.  That is all.

The fact of the matter is that the Browder surname is either originally Irish, or it is originally Germanic.  It can’t be both, as some seem to wish to have it.  Frankly, the idea itself that the Browder surname might be “Irish with a Norse forename or variant” seems to have come entirely from American researchers, who also tend to state that the name must somehow be Irish or Scottish just because an ancestor from this family sailed from one or the other country.  This is still believed by some, while those who still reside in those countries seem to know better, according to what I have uncovered.

Still, all of this is even better than what can be found on certain genealogical websites, where it is stated that the Browder surname is an “Americanized” form of the Dutch name broeder, meaning “brother” or of the German bruder, meaning the same thing.  In fact, in Old Norse, the word for brother is, again, “bróthir”; in Gothic it is “brother”; and in Irish/Gaelic it is “bráthair”.  So, these terms, with similar sounds and spellings, originate from a variety of areas. 

But there is nothing “Americanized” about our surname at all!!!  It existed, as we will see, well before members of our family emigrated to America.  The idea that our surname is “Americanized” suggests that the spelling “Browder” is something that evolved first in America, which is entirely false and fictitious.  It also suggests that persons with the names “Brouder”, and/or “Broder”, came over to America and changed the spelling of their names to “Browder”, either changing one letter, or adding a “w” to their surnames.  This is actually absurd and preposterous and deserves no further comment.

Having stated these things, it is a virtual certainty that, if I had had the opportunity to travel to Britain and Ireland and do any research there, I might have been able to trace the family line a few generations further back with some certainty, and clarify some things better.  But that has not happened.  So, that which is provided here is the best I have been able to produce, given the records I have been able to obtain.  Frankly, the only way to come to a definitive conclusion as to the origin of our actual family line would be to connect Edmund Browder I (c1661-c1739), our immigrant ancestor, to his parents and grandparents, wherever they actually resided.  That, so far, has not been possible.  While speculation is here provided, proof still eludes us. 

Again, my great-uncle, Herman E. Browder, according to my grandparents, Frank and Esther Browder, believed that the Browder family was of Germanic origin, but most of whatever notes or documents he may have possessed concerning this seem to have been lost even as early as my high-school years when I first learned of his research.  For all of these years, there just wasn’t any evidence to prove anything, other than the fact that some members of the Browder family resided in England and Ireland prior to immigrating to the English Colonies in North America.

In any case, having worked through some of the various scenarios that have been postulated by others in the past concerning the origins of our surname, we have now come to the point at which real answers can be brought out.  Because of the previously mentioned statement by Benjamin T. Hudson, found on Wikipedia.com, I decided to make contact with him via email to see what his belief might be concerning the origin of the surname Browder.  After all, I thought, if he were an expert in the Gaelic and Norse languages and history during Medieval times, he might be able to help.  And I was thrilled to have received the following email response to my questions:

Dear Mr. Browder,

Thank you for your most interesting note.  I am hardly an expert on this matter, but, after doing a bit of research, your suppositions appear to be correct.  The name ‘Browder’ seems to be a contraction of the English word ‘browderer’; this was an individual who carried out complicated sewing; a variant of the word is ‘broider’ as in ‘embroider’.  The fourteenth-century theologian John Wycliffe [1320-December 1384] used the word in his works as did the poet Geoffrey Chaucer [1343-October 25, 1400].  I have not been able to trace the word further back in time, but there may well be older examples.  The spelling of names can vary wildly at different times, so any examples you can collect are all useful.

My best wishes for your researches and the success of your book.

Yours Truly,

Benjamin Hudson

Suddenly, the solution to this perplexing, long-term, question became as simple as one email response, and one Old English term!  Instantly, all of the other theories as to our surname’s origins were called into question, as if they had not been already.  In fact, they were virtually dispelled. It became obvious to me that the origin of the surname “Browder” derived specifically from an Old English term of occupational origin, which meant “weaver” or “embroiderer”.  The key for me was the fact that the entire contraction “browder” was inherent within the term “browderer”.  There was no change in spelling at all – only an elimination of the final two letters of the original English term (which I have since found was also sometimes spelled “browderere”).  This, for me, was a much better source for the surname’s origins than any of the other previous possibilities!

The website, Dictionary.com, states that the archaic term, in Old English, was actually spelled variously as “brotherer”, “broiderer”, “borderer”, and “browderer”; the first term, if one contracts it, becoming “brother”.  In addition, its etymology shows that it emanated from the Old French term “brosder”, and, further back, to the Germanic term “brosd”.  

It would seem, then, that the term itself may well have been carried from the continent, along with the people who used it, to England.  And, since the term does not seem to have been used at all in England prior to the early 1300s, it would seem that those who used this term, first went into England during the early 1300s.  They brought a term with them, which quickly came to be used by the English and, thus, became an Old English term.

Even further research, conducted on-line (for this was the only way I could conduct it), revealed even more extraordinary support for this.  I first found a reference to a new, possible, ancestor in Transactions of the East Riding Antiquarian Society, Vol 3, p. 99, in which it was stated, “And paid to William Browderer for the expense incurred by him at London for the purchase of said clothing”.  This reference was for the years 1439-40.

Now, at first, I questioned this reference as probably not really signifying an actual surname.  I presumed that the clerk had probably meant to write “William the browderer”.  And I did find references to “William the browderer” several times in other sources on-line, in separate documents.  And while it would be impossible, from these various sources, to conclude that they were all referring to the same person, it did seem possible. 

In addition, from the St Hilary Parish Council: St Hilary Parish Website the following can be gleaned.

This place was important during the middle ages, being the home of the Trevarthian family.  One story states that in the latter half of the 14th century John Browderer, the Kings sergeant at arms, and Roger Trewynnarde fell upon a priest called Sir Walter Sancre here, and having bound his arms they then beheaded their unfortunate victim with a sword.  They then took the head to London, carrying it publicly on a spear as though it was that of a foul traitor or murderer.  Sir Walter was known as a man of good character and for their vile crime both offenders were excommunicated.

But, then, the truly extraordinary find took place.  Posted on-line can be found the Last Will and Testament of Sir Robert Hungerford, Second Baron Hungerford (May 14, 1404-1459), dated April 22, 1459.  This Will names many persons, including Robert’s brother, Sir Edmund Hungerford.  And among those who are given legacies, the following reference is made “To William Browderer 53s, 4d”.  This time, there could be no doubt, the term “browderer” was being used as an actual surname!  So, there actually was a person who resided in, or close to, London, England, who went by the name William Browderer during the 1400s.

Then, in a book entitled Entries in The Papal Registers Relating to Great Britain and Ireland; Papal Letters, Vol. IV (p. 488), under the title “Lateran Regesta, Vol. XXXIV”, a notation is made thus: “1394 4 Kal. May John Browderer, citizen of London”.  So, as early as May 4, 1394, a John Browderer resided as a citizen in London.  Once again, the term “browderer” is clearly being used as an actual surname.  And, it is obvious that this person, and presumably his family, were Roman Catholic at this time.

Further, in the book Original Documents; Extracts from the Ancient Accounts of Mettingham College, Suffolk. Communicated by the Rev. C. R. Manning, on the very first page, one can find this reference: “Thome Browderer”, having to do with the years 1406-07.  And, for the years 1412-13 (p. 63) reference is made to “Joh Browdere”.

Finally, from the website British History Online; “Two Tudor Subsidy Rolls for the City of London: 1541 and 1582” (p. 102), in the 1541 Roll, entitled “Strangers in Saynt Peters Somersettes Parysshe” reference is made to “Gyllam Browderer (£250) £12 s10”.

These are the only references to this surname I was able to find at that time (although I have found more since that time, which do not add to our knowledge), other than repeats.  From this evidence, it seems quite clear that the surname “Browder” did indeed emanate from the surname “Browderer”.  There can be little to no question of this anymore.  Every time the name is used, by the way, it includes the letter “w”, which demonstrates that the current spelling follows the original and was certainly not made-up by those who settled in America.

Thus, we have persons with the given names of John, Thomas, William and Gilliam (a Latin form of William), sharing the surname of Browderer, who lived at least during the years 1394 to 1541.  Whether any of these individual persons were our ancestors cannot be determined only with this evidence.  But the evolution of the term into a surname can be.

This having been said, it does seem possible that William Browderer is a key person among these.  The reason for this is because he is named in Robert Hungerford’s Will.  Because he is named in said Will, he already has a connection with given names such as Robert (Robart) and Edmund.  So, here, there actually is a very good possibility that William Browderer could have had descendants with the given names of Robert and/or Edmund.  In other words, the given names Robert and Edmund might have actually been introduced into the family initially because of the association that William Browderer had with both Robert and Edmund Hungerford.  Tenuous as this connection may be, it does raise this distinct possibility.

It seems obvious, as will be shown, that it was indeed from immigrants; weavers from Flanders in the Netherlands, mainly from the city of Ghent, who spoke a Germanic language; that the ancestors of the Browder family came.  This would tend to vindicate the research of Herman Browder.  Some of these Flemish weavers eventually adopted the term “browderer” as their own surname, and that surname was eventually contracted into “Browder”.  This can no longer be doubted, since evidence conclusively demonstrates that it did happen.  And it is also known that the Flemish weavers who came over into England, and from there to other parts of Britain and Ireland, created the very first textile guild, which was very powerful, since the cloth industry was the basis for England’s economy during medieval times.  These Browderers helped to create the cloth industry in England during this time-period.

The history of these Flemish weavers and embroiderers is also revealing.  It is known that Flemish weavers entered England from Flanders as early as directly after the Norman re-conquest of England in 1066, although it is also known that people from Flanders, and the rest of the Netherlands, had been entering Britain as early as Roman times for various reasons.  But, at first, they were few in number.  Before this time, in England, every household did their own weaving and made their own clothes.  But these Flemish weavers changed that.

One of these Flemings, who entered in, or after, 1066, was Drugo of Bruere, who received a large estate at Beverly from William the Conqueror.  Soon afterward, a rather large group of Flemish weavers were also allowed to settle in England.  “Gilbert the weaver” and “Baldwin the tailor”, listed in English records of the time, were undoubtedly among these.  And no question can arise concerning the fact that both Weaver and Taylor are used as surnames today, deriving from people just like these two.

With the establishment of guilds, the merchant class came into being.  It is known that one of the most important of the medieval guilds in England was that of the weavers.  The word “guild” comes from the Anglo-Saxon word, “gild”, which meant “payment”.  The guilds, however, actually had their beginnings earlier than 1066, essentially as fraternities that were somewhat religious in nature, and which formed during the time of the reign of the Anglo-Saxons, close to a given city’s churches.  In fact, they regularly presented open-air, public, performances or “mystery plays”, which served not only to strengthen the community, but also to present their guilds to the public as benevolent societies, often utilizing Christian themes for these plays.  The Freemasons is the most important surviving form of these medieval guilds.

These fraternities developed into associations following the Norman re-conquest.  They were made up of several related family groups of workshops, who all cooperated with each other to produce the product and to keep outsiders from doing so.  Originally, they were considered “aliens” or “strangers” and were organized as separate communities in the towns where they were found, and were placed under the special protection of the English crown.

The members of these guilds were the wealthiest and most influential of the citizens.  The “Apprentice” was the first level, and the “Journeyman” had to present his “masterpiece” in order to have proven himself worthy of progressing to the next level within the guild, which was the level of “Master”.  By this time period, only a cloth merchant who was also a leader of a given city’s weaver’s guild, was allowed to sell cloth.  In other words, he would be the only one allowed to receive payment for providing cloth to others and, thus, would have been the only one actually mentioned in any records concerning such payments.  He acted as a middleman between the tradesman weavers and the purchaser.  The importance of this will become clear as we proceed with the family line because it would be this person who would have been the head of the clan.  No other family members would be mentioned in such records at all. 

Now, it is known that in 1253 a group of Flemish weavers were allowed to immigrate into England.  And in 1270, King Henry III induced even more Flemish weavers to settle in England, encouraging them to “exercise their mysteries in the kingdom”.  A bit later, when he issued a proclamation ordering all Flemings to leave England (prejudice toward various groups was rife during those times, so expulsions of one group or another took place fairly often, not just in England, but throughout Europe), he made an exception for “those workmen, who with our leave shall come into our land to make cloths” (Llewellyn, p. 116).  Imagine being among the families that had to suddenly pick up and “go back where you came from” because of some perceived reason to hate you by others.

Later, King Edward III reversed this, encouraging even more Flemish weavers, the largest number to that date, to move into England in order to bolster the industry there, and to serve to cripple that industry in Flanders, where most of England’s weaving and manufacturing competition was located.  And, after all, it wasn’t difficult to induce these Flemish weavers to move into England at that time, since their industry was in turmoil, with constant conflicts between the weavers themselves and the merchant companies there, not to mention because of religious strife.  Edward, by the way, was the son of King Edward II, who had fought the Scots and had been defeated, time and again, by them.  But, in the end, he was to capture his arch enemy, William Wallace, and to have him brutally executed on August 23, 1305.

Now, the funds that were collected from the members of these guilds were used for social, charitable, and trade purposes.  These funds were also used as a contribution to the Exchequer in order for the guilds to be allowed to hold a Charter, which would confirm certain privileges, rights, and liberties from the king to said guild and its members.  This was the way they were protected by the crown.  The very first such recorded payment establishes the Weavers as London’s oldest company.  This entry is found in the Pipe Roll of 1130, and was entered for payment of £16 by Robert Levestan on behalf of the Weavers.

In 1155, King Henry II of England granted a charter to the Weaver’s Guild of London.  By the mid-1100s, the Weaver’s Guild had established a monopoly of its craft in London, and even had its own court.  The only other guild to have its own court by this time was that of the fishmongers.  All of this made the Weaver’s Guild very powerful.  But this privileged and powerful position tended to cause animosity among other citizens, and attempts were made by these other citizens to have this guild dissolved in exchange for a higher payment to the king, made by these citizens. 

In so many words, the citizens were willing to pay a higher tax in order to dispel the power of these guilds.  But the Weaver’s Guild was too wealthy, and they were able to pay a higher price than the citizens who opposed them were able to raise and, thus, kept their privileged position.  Still, citizens continued to make such attempts, so much so that its Charter had to be deposited in the Exchequer for safe-keeping, away from the civic authorities, who sought to arrest members of the guild and destroy it.

By the early 1300s, the Weaver’s Guild had finally submitted to the authority of the mayor, and gradually lost its pre-eminence due to the development of other textile guilds, which competed with it and took away some of its business, thus rendering the members somewhat less wealthy.  In 1321, the Court of Husting declared that it was lawful for all Freemen to set up looms and sell cloth as long as the King still received his yearly ferm, or tax.  Because of this, it became impossible for the Weaver’s Guild to maintain complete control of its craft. 

In addition, some independent weavers moved to the countryside to avoid control by the guild.  In their place, more “aliens” or “strangers” moved in.  This, again, was particularly so during the reign of King Edward III, when in 1327 Flemish weavers were encouraged to settle in England and bring their trade skills with them.  But the previously established weavers resented these new weavers.  They attempted to force the new immigrant weavers to join their Guild and to pay dues to it, but the King prevented this from happening.  Instead, the newest Flemish weavers set up their own guild, which was protected by the king, just as previous guilds had been.

Sadly, this turmoil resulted in the mass murder of Flemings at Snettisham and Yarmouth during the late 1300s.  Disputes between these two Weavers Guilds continued until, in 1497, a pact was entered into, which protected the interests of both sides and absorbed the “aliens” into the original Weaver’s Guild.

In addition to all of this, the Black Plague swept throughout Europe from 1347 to 1351.  Most notably for our purposes, in 1348 it swept through England and London, and was very devastating there!  One of the casualties of this horrible plague was Joan, favorite daughter of King Edward II, who was on her way through France to marry Pedro of Castile, and who died of the plague on July 1, 1348.

Later, during the early 1500s, even more foreigners were absorbed into this Weaver’s Guild, as more weavers from continental Europe, again mainly from the Netherlands, came over into England, fleeing religious persecution there by the Roman Catholic Spaniards who had taken over.  Hatred between these groups and other citizens reared again, resulting in an organized attack on foreign rivals by the apprentices and journeymen of London on “Evil May Day”, 1517, in which many were murdered.

Then, in 1665, the Black Plague swept through England again, and killed one-third of the population of London itself!  The areas where the weavers resided and worked were particularly hard-hit by this plague (The Worshipful Company of Weavers.com).

So, our family, according to this scenario, soon after their arrival in London, England, presumably during the early 1300s, would have survived the Black Plague, and then the various upheavals that resulted from disagreements over the cloth trade.  They are referred to at least twice in extant documents as “strangers”.  This shows that they were indeed Flemish weavers and not yet considered citizens.  And, again, like so many others, they adopted an occupational term for their surname.  They were among the most prominent and wealthy people who resided in London, at least at first.  They resided within their own communities, tended to arouse suspicions because they were “strangers” and, therefore, different.  And they participated in “mystery plays”, which were religious in conception, and which were not secret during that day in time, but open to the public.  Still, they were hardly accepted completely by the general populace.  They provided a necessary commodity and service to the general population, but that did not always make them accepted by that population themselves.  Hatred is always a real thing where the “other” is concerned and seen as somehow profiting at one’s own expense. 

According to this scenario, potentially, what we have here is an actual family line, mentioned rather sparsely in extant records, but mentioned nevertheless.  One should keep in mind that these groupings of immigrant weavers were all related, residing in the same places, and working together toward a common goal.  In addition, it would have been the patriarch of each related family grouping who would have been referred to in the records, because it was he, and he only, who would be allowed to be paid for the product produced.  He was the one in control.  And, concerning London and nearby areas, we actually have at least some of their names!  So, according to these sparse records, a skeletal family line could run as follows:

                John Browderer (b. c1327) – London (the King’s Sergeant at Arms)

                John Browderere (b. c1355) – London

                Thomas Browderer (b. c1375) – County Suffolk

                John Browderer (b. c1392) – County Suffolk

                William Browderer (b. c1419) – London?

                William Browderer (b. c1439) – London? [might have married a

member of the Hungerford family].

                Richard Browderer (b. c1466) – Coldharbour, Cornwall (in 1502 record there)

                Unknown Browderer (b. c1495) – London?

                Gilliam [William] Browderer (b. c1521) – London

                Unknown Browderer or Browder (b. c1565) – London? or Manchester?

                Unknown Browderer/Browder c1585) – London or Manchester?

                Robert Browder (b. c1604) m. Mary Hulme Dec. 26, 1623, Manchester

                Thomas Browder (b. c1624) in Irish records

                John Browder (c1642-p. 1701) m. a Havercomb?

                Edmund Browder I (c1661-c1739)

Again, the fact that the term “browderer” is not found in any extant record as being used in any way in England prior to the early 1300s and is not used as an actual surname until about 1394, with reference to John Browderer, strongly suggests that the family who adopted this as their surname came over from Flanders as weavers in or shortly after 1327.  They began their trade in London, and quickly established themselves there to such a degree that the term “browderer” soon began to be used as their surname.  It would later be contracted, likely by a descendant of the Gilliam Browderer found in the above record, into the surname Browder.

So, the records show that the surname actually originated in or around London, England in County Suffolk, wherever these Flemish weavers actually came from genetically (so, it isn’t an Irish name).  Today, the Browder surname, as far as I have been able to determine, is found ONLY in the British Isles and Ireland, as well as most of the former British colonies, including Australia, Canada, and the USA.  That is, with the exception of some who reside in Germany, who appear to have come from, or been related to, Browder family members from the USA (most being or having been in the military).  As will be seen, it appears that the majority of Browder family members today can be found in the USA, and most of them are descended from one couple – Edmund and Elizabeth Browder of Virginia, whom we will get to. 

In the meantime, the first known record of anyone with the actual Browder surname is that of a “Robart” (obviously Robert) Browder who married “Marye” (Mary) Hulme, on December 26, 1623, in the Cathedral in Manchester, County Lancashire, England.  The existence of this marriage record places the Browder family firmly in Britain at least by the early 1600s.  Whether this Robert and Mary Browder were the ancestors of the Browders to whom we can trace descent is an open question.  The answer is simply not certainly known just by looking at this record, or anything previously presented.  However, the spelling of this surname “Browder” is exactly the same as it is, and has been, spelled throughout all later generations (again, with the possible exception of the surname Broder, which might be a contraction of Browder), to my knowledge, and it obviously descends from the Old English term “browderer”.  One simply cannot deny that, based on the evidence.

This Robert Browder is almost certain to have been a descendant of those who emigrated from Flanders in Belgium, into Britain.  In fact, it seems reasonable to assume that Robert Browder could well have been a grandson of Gilliam (William) Browderer.  The surname Hulme, by the way, is of rather ancient English, pre-Norman, origin.

So, perhaps with this couple the connection actually has, finally, been reestablished, and the evidence which my great-uncle, Herman Browder, had found has possibly, once again, been uncovered.  But exactly what he knew is no longer certain today as the information that still exists is incomplete.  What is certain is that Robert Browder fits rather perfectly with the previous scenario, not just because of the timeframe within which he would have lived, but also because it seems reasonable to presume that he was also a weaver and, thus, a descendant of those who first coined the Browderer surname.  The reason this may well be the case is rather simple – a community of Flemish weavers is known to have settled in Manchester during the 1300s.  In so many words, Robert Browder may himself have migrated there from the London area, or, more likely, he may have been born there.  Either way, he was surely part of that community.

This influx of Flemish weavers is known to have sparked such growth that Manchester became Lancashire’s major industrial center.  By the early 1500s, Manchester had become a flourishing market town due to the wool trade.  Then, by 1620, the beginnings of a process called fustian weaving, and a newer influx of Flemish weavers, increased this growth and prosperity even more.

Manchester already had strong connections with London in regard to the textile industry.  This connection, in part, also caused Manchester to become a center of Puritanism.  This city sided with Parliament in its disagreement with King Charles I.  “Indeed, it might be said that the English Civil War started here” in 1642, when Richard Percival, a linen weaver, was killed during an attempt by Lord Strange to seize the militia magazine stored in the old College building.  It is believed by many that Percival was the first casualty of this war (Wikipedia.com, “History of Manchester”).

Now, one must note here the fact that County Lancashire, where Manchester is located, is directly across from Ireland, as can be seen from maps.  And it is also known that, by the early 1600s, some Flemish weavers had settled in the Irish county of Kilkenny, which is located next to County Tipperary.  And, it is in County Tipperary, Ireland that we find our next possible Browder ancestor.  It is quite possible that Robert and Mary Browder were the parents of Thomas Browder, who is listed in the Ballynoran 1664 Hearth Money Rolls for the Baronies of Iffa and Offa, County Tipperary, Shunraghan Parish (now in County Cork), Ireland.  He is listed as possessing one antall hearth (Burke, History of The Town of Clonmel, “appendix”), which determined the amount he was to be taxed.

It seems reasonable to surmise that Thomas Browder may have moved to Ireland, having become engaged to, or having married, an Irish woman.  I would also estimate that, by the time of this record, he would have been about forty-one years of age.  In any case, we find a definite correlation between the Flemish weaver’s arrival in this area and the fact that Thomas Browder resided in that same area; both taking place during the same time period.

It must be emphasized here that Thomas’ surname is not spelled in the record as “O’Browder”.  It is simply spelled “Browder”.  This clearly demonstrates that he was a descendant of someone else with the surname of Browder.  One cannot escape this obvious conclusion, based upon the evidence previously presented.  And, although the Browderer family had been quite prominent, as important members of the weaver class, while at London, England, they may not have been so prominent by the time they arrived in County Tiperary, Ireland.  But they were still likely part of that community.  They were probably still weavers/embroiderers.

Frankly, it seems that members of the Browder family resided all over England by the late 1500s, but it still appears that they were not as numerous as some other families.  The first baptismal/christening record found is of a Thomas Browder, son of Katherine Browder, baptized November 25, 1575 at Saffron Walden, St. Mary The Virgin Parish, Essex, England.  Alphonse Browder, son of Thomas Browder, was baptized in 1610 at Stoke Parish, Warwickshire, England.  Thomas Browder, son of William Browder, was baptized February 22, 1634 at Brockdish, Norfolk, England.  Katherine Browder, daughter of Matthew Browder, was baptized October 26, 1634 at St. Giles Parish, Cripplegate, London, England.  Thomas Browder, son of Mary Browder, was christened on April 14, 1659 (born March 29th) at St. Botolph Parish, Aldersgate, London, England.  William Browder, son of William Browder, was baptized May 13, 1660 at St. Savior Parish, Southwark, Surry County, England.  Tho. Browder, son of Thomas and Mary Browder, was baptized June 19, 1670 at St. Olave Parish, Bermondsey, Southwark, Surry County, England.  Maria Browder, daughter of Rob. Browder, was baptized in June of 1673 at Halifax, St. John the Baptist Parish, Yorkshire, England.  And Cather. Browder, daughter of Robert Browder, was baptized May 2, 1686 at Norwich, St. Augustine Parish, Norfolk, England. 

Two things appear obvious here.  There were several Thomas Browders and several Robert Browders.  It would seem reasonable, but still unproven, that most, if not all of the later Browders mentioned here descend from Thomas Browder, son of  Katherine Browder, baptized in 1575.  One has to surmise that something had happened to Katherine’s husband and, so, he is not named in the record.  But that is not the only possibility here.  Still, this may be exactly where the surname Browderer was shortened to Browder, but this may not be the only instance of this event.

It is also obvious that by the time if the first record found the family had become adherents of the Church of England since these baptismal records are found in the records of said church.  Sadly, there is no mention of Edmund Browder so far.  But, neither is there mention of the Thomas Browder of Ireland in these records, as far as I can tell – none of these Thomas Browders fit.  Still, that only lends credence to the possibility that Robert Browder and Mary Hulme are the likeliest candidates for the ancestors of Thomas Browder of Ireland since Robert Browder is also not mentioned in any of these records – none of the Robert Browders here fit.  But the preponderance of the names Thomas and Robert demonstrate a family connection, at least. 

Having stated this, “Marye” Hulme, daughter of “Adame” (Adam) Hulme, is mentioned in said records as having been christened on February 4, 1601 (baptized June 17, 1609) at Manchester Cathedral, St. Mary, St. Denys, and St. George Parish (Roll 745), England.  So, we know she was born in 1600/01. 

Now, to repeat some family tradition, it has long been held that the Browder family immigrated directly to the English Colonies in America from Ireland.  It is also said that information exists that a Robert Browder and a Thomas Browder can be found in some Irish church records, but to date I have not confirmed this latter information.  In fact, I have found nothing, other than surnames such as Broder and Broderick in said records.  There is also a family tradition of three Browder brothers emigrating to the Colonies from Ireland together.  This information is exactly why some draw the mistaken conclusion that the Browder family is indigenously Irish – just because they sailed from Ireland and seem to have an Irish name.

The Browder family seems obviously to have originated somewhere other than Ireland itself.  So, the “Irishness” of the Browder family is essentially an American label, which the Browder family may well have perpetuated themselves well into American colonial times for their own reasons.  After all, the American Browders likely were part Irish, as I have shown.  And, claiming to be Irish was probably somewhat better than admitting to having been Flemish, even by the time family members emigrated to the Colonies.

In addition, Thomas Browder of Ireland may very well have been among the first Browders to breed and race horses, very likely having been influenced to do this by his, presumably, Irish wife.  And relatives of this couple were probably instrumental, along with others from Ireland, in bringing the horse racing industry to the Colonies.  But no evidence really supports the contention that the family was indigenously Irish.  And, if Robert Browder and Thomas Browder are both to be found in Irish church records, as is suggested by some, the most likely scenario, again, is that they both emigrated to Ireland from England.  Otherwise, if Robert Browder is to be found in some Irish records somewhere, why would Robert Browder and Mary Hulme have been married in Manchester, England rather than Ireland?  It should also be noted that County Tipperary, Ireland, is even today famous for its horses and, as the Tipperary County Tourism Committee website states, even today it is considered to be “a Mecca for the equestrian enthusiast.” 

Again, as will be noted later, it seems that the Browder family in America was very much into horse breeding and horse racing, something they could well have first picked up during their sojourn in that particular area of Ireland.  In fact, once members of the Browder family arrived in the Colonies, there is no indication that they continued their ancestral trade of weaving (although that is not impossible), but there is clear indication that, along with tobacco planting and, later, farming, they bred and raced horses.

I will add that it is a distinct possibility that at least one ancestor of our Browder clan, other than the immigrant, could have been named Edmund or Edward.  I have reason to suspect that Robert Browder’s father may well have been named Edmund Browder and that our immigrant ancestor was essentially named after him.  I do not claim proof here, but it is a good possibility. 

Part of the reason for this is the fact that the name Edmund was very common during that time period.  But the main reason for this is that many persons were named after St. Edmund, who was King of Anglia.  He was killed by Danish Vikings on November 20, 869, and is considered a Saint of the Roman Catholic Church.  Since his shrine had been destroyed in 1539, that places his distinct memory in close proximity to the known beginnings of the Browder family.  Then on December 1, 1581, Edmund Campion, a Roman Catholic Priest (who, incidentally, wrote a history of Ireland), was tortured and executed in England for his faith.  And one must not forget that the main influx of the Flemish weavers entered England by way of invitation from King Edward III, and that Edmund and Edward were often interchanged in the records as names for the same person.  And, again, the first members of our family in Britain were Roman Catholic, if we accept that they were of these Flemish weavers.

Blanche Penland Browder (Penland Browder, p. 3; Nugent, in Cavaliers & Pioneers, Vol. 2, p. 152) stated that,

The first occurrence of the name [in the American colonies] was in September of 1674 when a JOHN BROADAR was brought to Virginia by William Havercomb.  Havercomb held a grant for 950 acres on the east side of the Elizabeth River in Lower Norfolk County, acquired for the transportation of eighteen persons (including the said JOHN BROADAR and self at fifty acres each – an allowance regardless of the relationship to the transporter, or even the number of times the same person was brought in).

Now, having seen the actual record here, I can state that, while it is true that this William Havercomb was granted 950 acres of land for the transportation of eighteen persons, including this John Broadar (see spelling in the actual document), it is a bit misleading (no doubt, unintentionally) to indicate that 1674 was the year that this “John Broadar” was brought over.  In fact, he could very well have been brought over earlier, during an undetermined year, and the grant made later, in 1674, as often happened.  So, we don’t know exactly when John Broadar was brought over, or even whether this was his first trip or not.  All we know for certain is that John was in the American Colonies by 1674.  From Virginia Land Patents, Book 6, p. 526, follows the actual, above-mentioned, grant:

Havercomb 950:  To all of who may of now know yee that . . . Wm. Berkely . . . doe give and grant unto Wm. Havercomb nine hundred and five acres of land in the County of Lower Norfolk on the South side of the Easterne Branch of Eliza River . . . the . . . and Beginning att Mr Jno. porter senr his Corner marked tree being an ashe in the ashen swamp, from thence Running 30 poles S by E to a marked white oake standing neare the ashen swamp Runs sou by the side of said River severall southerly Courses to a marked oak from thence 80 poles SE by S to James Jopling’s Corner marked tree being a gum, and by the sd Joplings line of marked trees NNE 250 poles and Continuing the NNE line 70 poles further to another marked Gum, then north 120 poles to another marked tree att the head of the other . . . branch from thence again North 690 poles along the side of a thick . . . to a marked beech standing on the James and Jno. Kemps lyne of marked trees, then by the sd Kemps line 200 W B N then N 6 W 8 poles to Issack Hockers land & from thence by the sd Hockers land 100 poles W & S to the first station the sd land being Due to the sd Havercomb by & for the transportation of Eyghteen persons hereto to have & to hold . . . to bee held . . . yealding & paying . . . provided . . . datted 23th Sept 1674

Jno. Joplin Jnr.      Mary Robinson       Rich Walpoole       Jno. Alford              

JNO. BROADAR      Owen henry?

2 Indians        Eliza. aynt                 Sage Ashe               Jno. Marshall  

Jno. LClery?                   Jno. ?

Ione a negro                   Gabriell Jacob       George Suth?        Mary Couton  

Sam. H…ing

Although this “John Broadar” may, or may not, have been in any way related to William Havercomb, or his wife, there can be little doubt that the person mentioned here was indeed a member of the Browder family (although his name was misspelled in the record), and, therefore, one of the original Browder immigrants to the American Colonies.  And, during that time period, it would have been James Fort (later known as Jamestown), Virginia, to which John would have been brought.

Please note here that there is no mention whatever of Ireland.  It is unknown where the ship, or ships, in question sailed from.  One has to presume that the journey began somewhere in Britain and/or Ireland, but that is all that can be determined here with any likelihood of certainty.  So, once again, it is family tradition that the ancestor or ancestors of the Browder family sailed from Ireland to the English Colonies in America, but there seems to be no actual record of this presumed fact.  Is it possible?  Yes.  Is it proven?  No.

This John Browder, it would seem, would have been exactly the right age to have been a son, or brother, of Thomas Browder of Ireland.  But, who exactly John Browder may have married is still unknown.  It is at least possible that he might have been married to a relative of this William Havercomb.  Headrights were sometimes relatives of the person who brought them over.  Sponsors often paid the passage for those headrights they brought over, especially for relatives, often in return for some repayment later.  And many came over as indentured servants and, thus, worked off their debt to freedom.  In fact, it has also been family tradition that the first Browder did come over as an indentured servant.  That could very well apply to this case.  

It is also possible that this John Browder could have married a Native American, as several of the earliest settlers of James Fort are known to have done.  The main reason for this was simple – not many women came from England along with these men to colonize the new world.  The fact that so many men arrived, having no women with them, compelled many of them to marry Native Americans.  This, however, can probably never be proven with reference to this John Browder.

Again, it is virtually certain that John’s name was misspelled in the 1674 record.  Another piece of information which lends credence to the probability that this “John Broadar” is one and the same as John Browder is a record of a John Browder who received some land in 1701 by headright (Sides-Dye, p. 5).  Who he himself may, in turn, have had transported to the colonies is unknown.  This John Browder’s property, though located in Virginia (not Maryland as some incorrectly posit), was situated in an area which later became a part of the State of North Carolina when the boundary was officially set.  In fact, it seems to have been located in an area almost disturbingly close to the area where the original English colony of Roanoke had been located (but not on the island itself).  Is it at least possible that this could signify a relationship between this John Browder and someone who resided in the “lost colony” of Roanoke earlier?  There is no way to know, as far as I can tell. 

Regardless, under the headright system, persons were given 50 acres of land for each person brought to this country.  How much total land John Browder may have received (based on how many persons he had transported into the colonies) is unknown due to lack of additional supporting records.

Now, John Browder, the son of Edmund Browder I, was born about 1686, which would have made him only about fifteen years of age in 1701.  So, unless his birth date estimate is completely off the mark (and it isn’t), it would seem that he must be eliminated as a possibility here.  Even if one were to add five years to his age, he would only have been sixteen, thus still making it unlikely that he would have been the John “Broadar” mentioned in this record.  The only real likelihood is that this John Browder was either an uncle, or the grandfather, of John Browder (b. c1686).  The John Broadar/Browder in the two records already cited must have been one and the same person.  So, he seems to have made at least two to three trips to and from the colonies, which was a fairly common occurrence by that time.  Once the colonies were firmly established, travel really became commonplace.

In addition, there was an “Edward Browder” who was mentioned by name in the early court records of Surry County, Virginia; this mention being made as early as 1682 (making him at least twenty-one years of age at that time due to the nature of said record), and also of Charles City County (later Prince George County), Virginia as early as 1693.  Edmund Browder is mentioned by name as early as 1695 in the same county records.  Initially, it seems possible that these three, John, Edward and Edmund, could have been the three Browder “brothers” of family tradition.  It seems equally possible that Edmund’s name could have been misspelled in the 1682 and 1693 records and that there are, therefore, only two Browders mentioned in these extant records, John and Edmund.  The latter is, in fact, the case, as will be shown.

However, there is also a reference to a “Geo. Browder” in Charles City County, Virginia Orders, Deeds, and Dispositions,1655-1665, p. 164.  Therefore, it does seem as if there may indeed have been three original Browder immigrants, John, Edmund and George, residing in Virginia at an early date, who could very well have been closely related after all.  And it is indeed rather romantic to conjecture that these three Browders were, in fact, the “brothers” of family tradition.  But, on closer inspection, it seems obvious that the “Geo. Browder” mentioned in the 1655-1665 record can’t be a brother of Edmund, because Edmund was born about 1651.  This George Browder was obviously the perfect age to have been Edmund’s father or uncle, rather than his brother.  And there can be little doubt that George was an immigrant himself. 

So, we know that there were at least two Browder immigrants, John and George.  Thomas may also have emigrated from Ireland to Virginia, although there is no extant record naming him.  Thus, three possible, and related, Browder immigrants, John, George and Thomas!  Well, perhaps.  At least we are certain that there were two immigrants, John and George.  And, again, I am virtually certain that these two were sons, or brothers, of Thomas Browder of Ireland, if the Browder family did indeed sail from Ireland, which is likely. What this shows is that John and George definitely immigrated to the Colonies, and Edmund may have also – and probably did, as an infant.  Edmund seems to have clearly been younger than these other two, according to the records, so he would likely have been the son of one of them, and a nephew to the other. So, it wasn’t three Browder brothers, most likely, but it was three related Browder men, at least.

The truth of the matter is that Edmund and Edward are indeed one and the same.  And it is also certain that today’s generations of Browders in America descend, as far as is known, only from Edmund Browder and his wife Elizabeth (Prince George County Deed Book [PGCDB] III) with the exception of some who emigrated to these shores at later dates during the 1800s; one, at least, known to have been born in Scotland, according to census records.
