Earliest Known Descendants of Richard and Mary Browder

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (revised Dec. 28, 2025)

The known children of Richard Browder (c1719-1807) and Mary Epes Randolph (c1720-p. 1778) were Arthur (c1742-c1786), Susanna (c1745-bef. Apr. 1834), William (c1748-c1802), Littlebury “Littleberry” (c1754-1846), David (c1755-c1813), Thompson (c1758-c1804), John (c1759-c1800), Mary “Polly” Thompson (Dec. 7, 1762-p. 1844), Wilmuth (c1763-bef. Apr. 1834), Nancy A. (c1764-p. 1844), and Martha (c1768-c1811).

It is now absolutely certain that Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) was a son of Richard and Mary Browder, as will be shown.  This is something that previous researchers failed to properly connect, although the evidence was there.  But it is possible that they had not seen the evidence that will be presented.

This Arthur Browder is believed to have possibly married a Harrison, who may have been the daughter of Gabriel and Grace Harrison, according to some researchers.  I do favor this belief but have found no proof myself. Even so, it is also now known that this Arthur Browder married Rebecca Jones. It is entirely possible, but not proven, that the older brothers may have had a different mother than their youngest brother had, and this would explain things. Be that as it may, the record is clear that the mother of Jones Browder was Rebecca Jones.

The Dinwiddie (formerly Prince George) County, Virginia taxpayer lists shows that this Arthur Browder owned 50 acres of land, paying personal property taxes in Dinwiddie County in 1782. 

The children of this Arthur Browder are known to us.  They were Harrison (c1760-February 26, 1778), Frederick (c1761-November 28, 1778), Samuel (c1762-1778), and Jones (c1763-Oct. 2, 1826). 

The fact of the matter is that descendants of Lt. Arthur Jones did have some descendants with the given name of Jones.  The given names Fredrick and Samuel were also common within the Jones family of the area, thus indicating another possible link to the Browder and Jones families.  Harrison Browder may well have been given his mother’s maiden name as his own given name, as was common in those days.  And Jones Browder, the youngest son of this Arthur Browder, was almost certainly given the surname Jones as his given name because of his mother, Rebecca.

It is at least interesting to note a little information concerning Gabriel Harrison here, showing that he resided in Brunswick County, Virginia.  From BCDB 2, pp. 93-94:

Indenture [Lease] made the 6th day of August, 1741, between Gabriel Harrison of the County of Prince George, and Abraham Cook of the County of Hanover, for 5 shillings, conveying 300 acres on North side of Roanoke River a little below Swan Pond.  Witnesses were John Dodd and William Baker.  Acknowledged in Court on August 6, 1741.

And, from BCDB 3, p. 322:

Deed of Gift dated the 6th day of May, 1747, between Gabriel Harrison, of Bath Parish, County of Prince George, Planter, and Nathaniel Harrison, his son, of St. Andrews Parish, County of Brunswick, conveying 180 acres on both sides of Sturgeon Run, and being same land granted to Gabriel Harrison, by Letters of Patent bearing date of July 7, 1726.  Acknowledged in Court on May 6, 1747. 

Unfortunately, the three eldest brothers, Harrison, Fredrick, and Samuel, all died in 1778 while serving as soldiers in the Continental Army during the American Revolutionary War.  It is generally believed that neither of them ever married, and that they, therefore, had no descendants.  But this is not a certainty.  Still, they have no known descendants. 

All three Browder brothers, Harrison, Fredrick, and Samuel, had enlisted together in the Continental Army on September 6, 1777 for a term of three years in Capt. Peter Jones’ Company of Continental Regulars, 14th Virginia Regiment, which was commanded by Col. Charles Lewis.  All three were later reported in the records as being ill prior to their deaths.  Fredrick and Samuel were first reported ill at Lancaster, and Harrison was first reported ill at York. 

Harrison is known to have participated in the war at Valley Forge, White Plains, West Point, and York prior to his death, according to his service record (Penland Browder, pp. 77-78).  Fredrick and Samuel probably did also. 

Jones Browder was too young to take part in the war, so he had the fortune to survive it.  Jones later inherited all of his brother’s Revolutionary War service money in 1786, and also received their bounty, Warrant #4173, for 300 acres of land altogether, on June 15, 1786, thus winding up with a relatively large estate.  We know of these relationships and these facts because Jones’ three brothers, and his father, were named in a sworn statement concerning his brothers’ property (ibid, p. 49).  The record of this is found in the folder of Harrison Browder in the Virginia State Archives and reads thus (ibid, p. 51):

John Lewis and Littleberry Browder personally appeared before us James Greenway and Edward Pegram, Jr., Justices of the Peace of Dinwiddie County, and made oath that Jones Browder is the only surviving son of Arthur Browder late of this county, dec. and that as far as they really know and believe is the only survivor and heir of his three brothers who died in the service of the United States viz: Harrison, Frederick and Samuel Browder.  We do certify that we verily believe also that he is the heir of these persons his brothers as above mentioned.   Giving under our hand this eighteenth of April 1786.                                                                                                             James Greenway

                                                                                                                           Edward Pegram, Jr.

Sir:  Please to . . . certificates for the pay vouchers of my three brothers above mentioned and send them over to me by Sherwood Vaughn.

                                                                                                                              Jones Browder                                                                                                                April 19, 1786

Because of this family tragedy, we have extant records that help to prove family relationships.  Please note that one of the above witnesses was Littleberry Browder, son of Richard and Mary Browder.  These two were brothers and it is known that Jones Browder and Littleberry Browder owned lands in Dinwiddie County which were close, or which joined.

Jones Browder, son of this Arthur Browder, began to pay taxes on his property in Dinwiddie (formerly Prince George) County, in 1788, and continued to do so until 1826, when he died.  He still owned land located on the branches of Gravelly Run (at old Petersburg) at his death, and he was also one of the few members of the Browder family who still owned land in that area.  Also, it is known from said records that he had purchased some of his land from James H. Boisseau (Hughes, p. 51).

The 1810 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia indicates that he was, in fact, married by that time, but by the time the 1820 census was taken, as Blanche P. Browder has pointed out, neither a wife nor children seem to be listed within his household.  The 1810 census of Dinwiddie County lists Jones Browder, along with his wife, but no children.

The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County lists Jones Browder (45 and over) and an almost unique household.  Again, no wife appears to be listed.  But several free colored persons are listed; six males, and five females.  And it also lists two male slaves and one female slave.  The 1820 tax list confirms Jones Browder as owning three slaves that year. 

The Above: Jones Browder’s death notice, “The Charleston Daily Courier”, Oct. 12, 1826

Please note that the above notice states that Jones Browder was in his 64th year of age, which would have made his birth year 1762/63. I have had to allow for his three older brothers and arrive at about 1763 on that basis. However, he still could have been born in 1762. Regardless, this proves (as if this was necessary) that Jones Browder’s father, Arthur, was not the Arthur Browder who died prior to 1739 who had an unnamed heir mentioned in the Bristol Parish Register.

Jones Browder left no will, and appears to have had no heirs, so some family members litigated over his property which, by the time of his death, totaled 345½ acres of land near Gravelly Run, as well as two slaves and six horses (ibid, p. 50/Dinwiddie County Will Book [DCWB] 1, 1830-1835, p. 208).  He was not exactly rich, but he was pretty well-off. 

Thus, sadly, appears to end the line of this Arthur Browder.  Capt. Isham Browder (c1732-1784), by the way, died about one or two years before the above sworn statement was taken, so he was not around to have witnessed it, even if he might have been asked to. 

But this does not end of the tale of Jones Browder’s life.  A close reading of the information contained in the above mentioned 1820 census seems to me to indicate something entirely different from what has always been held by other researchers, who may well have not wanted to get into this.  It is my belief that Jones Browder did, in fact, have a female companion, at least, and children by her, who are listed in said census.  It appears to me that his wife was the oldest free colored woman (45 and over) listed within his household, and that the other free colored household members; one male (26-44), one male (14-25), four males (under 14), one female (26-44), two females (14-25), and one female (under 14), are all his children! 

So, other family members sued for shares in his property after his death because these free colored persons were not allowed to inherit anything.  Such a scenario practically never happened in old Virginia, but there is really no other logical reason that Jones Browder would have had a household of free colored persons, as well as three slaves. 

Again, Jones Browder, son of Arthur Browder, died in 1826.  Some family members sued for shares in his estate, beginning in 1832.  Every person, save just a few given permission to join in the suit, who sued for shares were descendants of Richard and Mary Browder. This demonstrates that the descendants of Richard and Mary Browder had the most direct right to the property of Jones Browder. And ALL of them were descendants of John and Elizabeth Browder. In 1836, after Jones Browder’s estate had been litigated over for four years by these other family members, the children of Thompson Browder (son of Richard and Mary Browder) also petitioned to become legatees.  The following petition is copied, verbatim, from Halifax County Land Records Book 43, 1836, p. 73:

Mr. Benjamin A. Browder, Jesse Browder, Venis Browder, Jonathan Browder, William R. Bostick, and Drury Wall which said Benjamin A. Browder, Jesse Browder, Venis Browder, and Jonathan Browder, and children and kindred distributees of Thompson Browder, dec., and which said William R. Bostick and Drury Wall married Jane and Tabitha Browder who are also children, heirs, distributees of the said Thompson Browder have ordained constituted . . . Assigned and by these presents do ordain, constitute, appoint William R. Pulliam also of the County of Halifax our true and lawful attorney for us and our benefit to ask, demand sue for . . . Recovery from John Sydnor – executor or administrator of David Thacker, dec., all money, property, or estate which is in the hands of John Sydnor in his character as representative aforesaid of Jones Browder, dec., which may be due or coming to us  from the estate of Jones Browder in any manner whatsoever either as  legatees, devisees or heirs or distributees of the said  Jones Browder, dec.,  and we do hereby give our said attorney full power and authority to receive the same and we also empower said attorney to do every act or acts necessary to be done toward the accomplishment expressed in these presents as fully and effectively as we or either of us could do in our proper persons.  In testimony whereof we have set our hands and affixed our seals 28 day March 1836, signed sealed in the presence of

             Benjamin Browder

             Jesse Browder

             Jonathan Browder

             William R. Bostick

             Drury Wall

The Above: Jones Browder estate notice, the Richmond Enquirer, Aug. 3, 1832

One should note that, by this date, Capt. Isham Browder I had already been deceased for about 51 years.  His wife, Talitha Cox-Browder, had been deceased about 8 years.  Their son, Isham C. Browder had been deceased about 4, years and his wife, Martha Browder, had been deceased at least 15 years. 

Again, Arthur Browder died about 1786 in Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  No doubt Arthur was deceased by the date of April 18, 1786, when Jones Browder received his brother’s pension monies, because he is not mentioned in said document as receiving anything.  It seems that this Arthur Browder resided in this area of Dinwiddie County, Virginia all of his life, as did Jones Browder. 

But, make no mistake. It is very likely that descendants of Jones Browder are alive today, as we speak, descendants of this “free colored woman” who was residing with Jones Browder, according to census data.

Earl Russell Browder (the son of a teacher and the grandson of a Methodist minister) was born in Wichita, Sedgwick County Kansas and died at Princeton, Mercer County, New Jersey, having moved late in his life to Yonkers, Westchester County, New York. 

Earl left school before the age of ten (at the third grade) due to the illness of his father.  Earl then worked to help support the family with his brother.  Earl’s father, a Populist and later a Socialist (like many Kansans at that time), encouraged political discussions within the family (Olson, 625).  This was formative for young Earl. 

Earl moved to Kansas City, Kansas while in his twenties, where, in 1907, he joined the Socialist Party and, in 1914, completed a correspondence course in law.  He left the Socialist Party in 1912, thereafter associating himself with the Syndicalist League of North America, led by William Z. Foster, at which time he served as associate editor of the Toiler, a syndicalist magazine (ibid, 625).  He was later elected president of the local Bookkeepers, Stenographers and Accountants Union (Whitman, 3).

Earl Browder was a political activist, who was imprisoned for his opposition to United States participation in WWI.  He was first arrested in 1917 for “conspiring to defeat operation of the draft law and for non-registration.”  For the second charge he spent a year in prison.  He rejoined the Socialist Party when he was released from prison and became the first editor of “The Worker’s World”, a pro-Communist paper which circulated in Kansas, in 1919 (ibid, 3).  

He was later imprisoned at Leavenworth Federal Prison for a total of sixteen months, from 1919-1920, on a charge of “conspiracy” (but later pardoned in 1933).  He was made an honorary charter member of the new Communist Party while in prison and became active in the party in 1921 upon moving to New York City (Olson, 625). 

In his book, “The Communist Party of the U.S.A., Its History, Role and Organization”, Earl Browder wrote: “The Communist Party [in America] was born in Chicago on September 1, 1919” (Whitman, 8).  This nostalgic reminiscence well illustrates Browder’s fondness for the Communist Party in America at that time.  

Browder later also served 14 months in a prison in Atlanta for certain passport irregularities (sentence commuted in 1940).  Having joined the American Communist Party, he served as director of the Pan-Pacific Trade Union Secretariat in Shanghai, China.  There, he helped to organize the Communist drive in China. 

He and Mr. Foster, whom he worked with extensively during his early years in the party, met Lenin for the first time in 1921 at the first Congress of the Red International of Labor Unions (the Profintern) in Moscow.  In 1926 the Profintern sent Earl Browder, as one member of a delegation, to China.  There he helped form the Pan Pacific Trade Union Secretariat.  Mr. Browder then served as Secretary of this organization.  After this stint was completed, he returned to the United States in 1929 (Whitman, 3).

Later, due to factional strife among two divergent groups, the Trotskyites and the Lovestones, a special Comintern Commission, under Stalin, was called in Moscow to put it to an end.  During this meeting, Mr. Lovestone was dismissed, and the party leadership passed to Earl Browder due to the fact that Mr. Lovestone and William Z. Foster had had greater roles in the previous factional strife than had Browder.  However, Browder said he never really trusted Stalin, whom he was only acquainted with via this one meeting (ibid, 4).  

Earl Russell Browder married Raissa Borisbova Berkman (1897-Jan. 8, 1955), of Russia, in 1926.  Afterward he served as General Secretary of the Communist Party of the United States from 1930-1946.  In 1930, he was the Communist Party candidate for the U.S. House of Representatives from New York, 6th District. 

Earl Russell Browder and Raissa Borisbova Berkman became divorced, after which he married Gladys Grove (1891-1981). They were also divorced and Earl later married Catherine “Kitty” Harris (Mar. 25, 1899-Oct. 6, 1966).

Earl Browder was the Communist Party candidate for the Presidency of the United States in 1936, and again in 1940.  He lost all of these political contests.  Even so, in 1932, he had been the first U.S. Presidential candidate in history to select an African-American, James W. Ford, as his running-mate.  Ford later continued to work as a Communist Party organizer from 1936 to 1940.

Earl Browder served as General Secretary of the Communist Party in the United States during the years of its largest membership and greatest influence.  However, by 1935, he began backing down from the party’s former revolutionary line in the interest of world peace, and urged cooperation with the socialists and other groups, who were demanding more radical change.  Browder and the Communist Party backed the “New Deal” and Roosevelt, beginning in 1935, until the Soviet-German peace pact was signed in 1939, and the outbreak of World War II ensued (ibid, 4).

As Communist Party candidate for president in 1936, Browder had visited 26 states, traveled 26,000 miles, and received 80,160 votes.  This was the most votes he would ever receive.  It was in 1941 that Browder was later convicted of unlawful use of his passport, and sentenced to four years in prison, following a series of appeals (Olson, 625).

By June of 1941, the Communist Party changed course and began to support the war effort because of the invasion of Hitler’s forces into the Soviet Union.  In December of 1941, President Roosevelt commuted Mr. Browder’s sentence, and he was released from prison.  “I was released by President Roosevelt personally,” Mr. Browder said in a 1971 interview.  He called it a “political pardon” (Whitman, 4).

In 1943, the Communist Party in the United States was officially dissolved, due to Browder’s influence, after which the Communist Political Association was formed in 1944, with Browder as its leader.  This, however, proved unsatisfactory to many American Communists.  One in particular, Jacues Duclos, a French columnist, was infuriated!  In 1945, he published an open letter in which he criticized Browder, calling him a “right deviationist” because Browder had dissolved the American Communist Party.  What followed was a fierce campaign to unseat Browder from within the ranks of the American party. 

Browder, however, continued to believe that the letter was a hoax, which had actually been written in Moscow.  Of this incident, Earl Browder stated in the 1970 interview: “To be a dictator, Stalin required a respectable opponent.  Hitler was gone, and he had to be replaced.  It had to be the United States, the leading capitalist country.  Stalin picked a quarrel, and I was the victim of it.  Of course, he had plenty of provocation…” (ibid, 4-5).

Because of these events, Earl Browder was expelled from the Communist Party in 1946.  In 1949 he was named in “treason trials” in Budapest and Prague as the originator of “the heresy of Browderism” (Britannica, “Browderism”, 556).  This “heresy” is defined as the belief, held by Browder, that Communism and Capitalism could coexist in the same world peacefully without the one destroying the other.  This was an idea which Moscow could not tolerate.  

However, even after this devastating blow, Browder was appointed Soviet Publishing Representative in the United States, where he continued to serve until 1950, when he resigned because, he said, “to the embarrassment of the American Communist Party, he could not make enough money” from this job (Whitman, 5).  Thus, it would seem that Earl Browder fist rose from a youth of little education, to the highest position in the Communist Party in America, and later fell from that position due to his multiple imprisonments and, in the end, due to one man, Joseph Stalin.

What we know for sure about Jesse Browder begins with the fact that, according to later sworn testimony, Jesse Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, having recently enlisted in the southern army (unusual for someone from Virginia), takes part in the Battle of Sullivan’s Island or the Battle of Fort Sullivan near Charleston, South Carolina on June 28, 1776. The British received significant casualties and were repelled, not to return until 1780.

Also, according to the same sworn testimony, Jesse Browder took part in the First battle of Savannah on Dec. 29, 1778. The British achieve victory and capture the city, occupying it and opening up their southern campaign. Jesse Browder, along with two others, is forced to flee and escape in order not to be captured by British forces, they making a raft and going across the Savannah River by this means.

After Jesse returned home to Dinwiddie County, Virginia, he married his cousin, Mary Thompson “Polly” Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder, on Dec. 15, 1780.

She further stated that he often spoke of being in the Battle of Sullivan’s Island and in a battle (name forgotten) near the Savannah River in which the Continentals were defeated. She further stated that in order to save his life and avoid capture he and several others had to swim the Savannah River to get away, which he attributed his bad health and early death to. She further stated that she did not know of these instances as direct facts, but had heard him speak of them often and did believe what he said. Also that she had seen his discharge many times, but that it had since been lost or destroyed. She further stated that they had been married on Dec. 15, 1780, and that he died on Feb. 7, 1783. She married Thomas Pentecost later on Sept. 23, 1784, and that he died on Feb. 7, 1830, and that she had been a widow ever since, etc. Some corroborating dates from a family Bible are also included in this record.

NOTE: Even though some of the deposition material above is a bit confusing, a few things are made clear within said deposition statements. (1) Certain dates and ages are provided that would not otherwise have been known to us, even if they may be a little off as memories had faded. (2) Jesse Browder had served in the southern army as a regular while Charles Browder and Peter Eppes served at about the same time in the northern army in the regular army as infantrymen. That Both Jesse and Polly had been Browders and cousins, and both brought up in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Several more things are made clear too. But one might have thought that Mary Williams might have stated that Polly Pentecost, formerly Browder, was her own sister, but she did not. Yet, this becomes all too clear in the texts of court cases concerning the division of Jones Browder’s estate. It seems odd, but one of the sisters was referred to as Mary and the other Polly most of the time, but sometimes both were referred to as Mary. Yet one is sometimes referred to as Nancy also. It is JUST possible that these two were not sisters. But if they were not, then one has to figure out the parentage of one or the other.

I have also not detailed Isham Browder (1762-1830), his possible brother, here because that is done in another section.
