Providing accurate information on the Browder and related families

Captain Thomas Isham Browder, Esq. and Talitha Cox of Guilford/Rockingham County, North Carolina, Our Founding Pioneers

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 3, 2019October 4, 2025Posted inUncategorized


By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M. S., M.T.S., M.Div (updated and corrected Dec. 23-27, 2024)

Thomas Isham Browder (I will show why I give him the first name “Thomas” later), son of Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739) and Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772), was born about late 1732/early 1733, probably in Goochland County, Virginia, although Prince George County (the part later made into Dinwiddie County) is possible.  He died in late 1783, mid to late 1784 (most likely), or early 1785, depending on how one looks at the evidence (most likely about August 1784), aged about 51 years, in Guilford County, North Carolina.

Researchers should remember that the usual later birth date presented for this Isham Browder is purely speculative, mainly based upon the belief that he must have been born not many years prior to his wife, Talitha Cox (c1745-July 3, 1828).  I arrive at the year 1732/33 mainly due to the evidence presented concerning the likely marriage of Arthur Browder and Mary Isham Randolph, as well as evidence of his first mention in any extant record, rather than the date of about 1736/40 that some others propose.  

Isham would have inherited whatever lands and other property his father had designated for him at the age of 21, about the year 1754, but there is NO extant record of such inheritance, or receipt of land.  That he had lands in Virginia cannot be disputed.  How, or when, exactly, he received said lands, and what exactly he did with them, is unknown.  But, obviously, he later sold them. In any case, it does seem reasonable to assume that he inherited the lands of Arthur Browder mentioned in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book.  

Isham Browder appears in extant records in Virginia in 1756, about two years after he would have reached adulthood.  So, he could not have been born any later than 1736, in any case because he would have had to have been at least 21 years of age in these first extant records. 

Now, Isham certainly served in the Virginia militia in some capacity, since service was compulsory for all free and able white males in the Colony.  In fact, even William Byrd of Westover, with his status and wealth, had to serve in the Virginia militia.  Isham could well have served during the French and Indian Wars prior to 1757.  And I think that this is highly likely and that during this time he became acquainted with Griffith Rutherford, who is known to have served during these campaigns and who later moved to North Carolina just as Isham did. In fact, Rutherford rose to the rank of Captain by the end, which is exactly what seems to have been the case for Isham browder also. The very fact that there is about a three to four-year gap in Isham’s extant record (if he was born in 1732/33) lends credence to that possibility.  

So, he certainly served in the militia at some point during the years 1752/53-1757, and could well have also taken part in the French and Indian Wars.  In fact, as I have mentioned, it seems that there is reason to believe that during this time, Isham may have risen to the rank of Captain.  If not, it becomes obvious that he acquired that rank later in life, as will be discussed later.  Still, it also seems that, given the number of years he may have served in the militia before doing anything that we can find an extant record of, he may well have been, more or less, making a career out of the military during his early adult years.  And, since he served in the militia in Virginia, he is likely to have at least also known George Washington. It is also likely that during this time Isham became acquainted with Maj. Augustine Claiborne and also with Capt. John Cox. 

Isham first married (1) Agnes Tatum (b. April 1, 1740/christened May 4, 1740, godparents; Cheney Tatum, Frances Rives, and Rebecca Roberts), daughter of Christopher Tatum II and Elizabeth, in Albemarle Parish, Surry County, Virginia (Mecklenburg County Marriage Bonds).  Isham and Agnes Browder thereafter may have resided in Sussex County, Virginia, although Halifax County, Virginia (the part formed from Lunenburg County, which was earlier part of Brunswick County, and earlier than that, Prince George County) is more likely.  He and Agnes were probably married about 1758.  We know that Isham resided in Halifax County prior to his second marriage to Talitha Cox because the marriage license states as much. 

Isham and Agnes Browder are known to have had one son, Isham Browder, Jr., who was born on March 17, 1759, and christened on June 3, 1759, in Albemarle Parish, Sussex County, Virginia (Albemarle Parish Register [Colonial Dames], 44).  Isham, Jr’s godparents were Nathaniel Beddingfield, William Weathers, and Phoebe Pettway (Penland-Browder, p. 7).  

The Will of Christopher Tatum II, father of Agnes Tatum, names neither his daughter, Agnes, nor his grandson, Isham.  So, this indicates that they were both deceased at the time of Christopher’s death in 1769. Frankly, they would have to have been deceased by 1766 for Isham’s second marriage to take place in any case.  Both Agnes Tatum-Browder and Isham Browder, Jr. died for unknown reasons in about 1761.  This left our Isham Browder without a wife or children. 

Isham Browder is mentioned in extant records when, on Feb. 15, 1756 he served as a witness to the marriage of Peter Lee, son of Samuel Lee and Celia Pettway, daughter of Robert Pettway, in Sussex County, Virginia. Other witnesses were Augustine Claiborne, Robert Pettway, Jr., John Chambless, and Sarah Chambless. He was also a witness, along with Thomas Young, Peter Jones, and Nathaniel Raines, to the marriage of Frederick Raines (b. June 9, 1732) and Frances Wyche (b. c1739), on May 3, 1756 in Sussex County, Virginia (Sussex County Marriage Register, p. 2).

On the same date Isham Browder, along with these three other witnesses, witnessed the Will of Frederick Raines in Sussex County, Virginia.

The above Peter Jones is likely the Peter Jones (Dec. 17, 1720-Oct. 13, 1799), son of Col. Richard Jones (c1688-June 1759) who married Sarah Stratton (c1694c1725), daughter of Edward Stratton and Martha Shippey. 

The above Thomas Young was the husband of Mary Tatum, daughter of John Tatum (son of Christopher Tatum I and Bridget Scott). John Tatum married Mary Eppes, daughter of Daniel Eppes and Mary. It seems obvious that this Thomas Young was a son of George Young. George Young had a brother, Thomas B. Young, Esq. (c1720-1791), who had a son, Maj. Thomas Young of the Revolution. Maj. Thomas Young had married Catherine Brandon, sister of Thomas Brandon (1741-Feb. 5, 1802). Thomas Brandon first went, along with his two brothers, to Rowan County, North Carolina about 1755. Thomas Brandon moved on to South Carolina about 1768. During the Revolution, Thomas Brandon rose from Captain to Colonel. After the war he was made a Brigadier General. He is known to have been in the battles of Musgrove’s Mill, Cowpens, King’s Mountain, and Old Nonety-Six, as well as some others. Maj. Thomas Young served during the Revolution under Col. William Washington. Both Brig. Gen. Thomas Brandon and Maj. Thomas Young are buried in the same cemetery in Union County, South Carolina. 

Interestingly, Richard Brandon (c1722-c1790), son of John Brandon and Elizabeth Cathey, remained in Rowan County, North Carolina. He married Margaret Locke, daughter of John Locke and Elizabeth Patton. One of their daughters, Alice, married John Erwin. John and Alice Erwin migrated to McNairy County, Tennessee. One of their daughters, Mary Elizabeth Erwin (Jan. 7, 1780-Jan. 13, 1847), born in Rowan County, North Carolina; died in McNairy County, Tennessee, married Nathaniel Erwin (1776-Sept. 11, 1845). One of their daughters, Narcissa Locke Erwin (Aug. 27, 1810-Jul. 11, 1894) married John H. Black (Feb. 11, 1805-Apr. 12, 1865). It seems reasonable to speculate that John H. Black was descended from or related to the William Black who was associated with Capt. Isham Browder. And John H. Black and his wife, Narcissa, were neighbors in McNairy County to our ancestor, Isham Richard Browder. 

Isham Browder is then mentioned in the Albemarle Parish Vestry Book in two separate references, dated for June of 1757. These references are here reproduced verbatim:

 Ord. that the Churchwardens pay to ISHAM BROWDER the Sum of fifteen shillings for Transcribing the 39 articles of the Church of England. . . . [and] ISHAM BROWDER paid P. order of vestry (pp. 105-06).

Once again, Isham would have had to have been an adult by this time, making his birth date no later than 1736 at the very latest, as already indicated.  And, obviously, he was still a loyal member of the established Church of England at that time.  One wonders if he might have been studying for the ministry.  If so, he did not pursue this ultimately. 

This Isham Browder was also obviously the one who served as a witness to three deeds performed in the town of Blanford, Dinwiddie (previously Prince George) County, Virginia during the years 1759 and 1760 (PGCW&DB 1759-1760, pp. 107, 121 & 153) according to Blanche Penland-Browder.  On Feb. 2, 1760, for example, Isham Browder was a witness, along with John Butler, William Black, William Davis, and Henry Batte, Jr., to a deed in which William Poytress of Dinwiddie County, Virginia sold 105 acres of land to Samuel Gordon of Prince George County, Virginia. 

The above Henry Batte, Jr. would have been the son of Capt. Henry Batte (c1635-c1703) and Mary Lound. Capt. Henry Batte was a brother to William Batte, Sr. who married Mary Stratton, daughter of Edward Stratton and Martha Shippey. And the above William Black must be the same as the William Black who was deeded 157 acres of land in Orange County, North Carolina by George Lumpkin, this deed later being proved by none other than Isham Browder in November of 1764 (Orange County, NC Deed Book Zero, p. 191). The deed having likely been done in Dinwiddie County, Isham must have traveled there to prove it. In that time period, deeds were required to be proven within eight months of when they were made. 

I have not seen the other deeds she mentions. However,  on Oct. 27, 1762 Isham Browder, along with John Leigh and Edward Brisham, witnessed a deed in which Isaac Jackson of Raleigh Parish, Amelia County, Virginia deeded 196 acres of land to David Greenhill of the same county and state. This was land that Jackson and his wife had bought from Greenhill, who had purchased it from Alexander Howard for the use of Claiborne Lawson. 

It seems clear that Isham’s wife, Agnes, was deceased by this time, along with their son, Isham because those mentioned in this document are more clearly related to Isham’s second wife, Talitha Cox, than to his first wife, Agnes Tatum.

On Apr. 29, 1766, Isham Browder took out an ad in the Virginia Gazette which stated that he soon intended to relocate to South Carolina. Whether this was a mistake, or if, perhaps, he had really intended to move to South Carolina (and I think he did intend to), he never made that move but, instead, relocated to North Carolina. 

In any case, Thomas Isham Browder was obviously named after his grandfather, Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph (July 10, 1683-1730), and his mother Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772), Mary was named after her grandmother, Mary Isham (1659-Dec. 29, 1735).  It is, in my mind, this family lineage that gave him an advantage over other members of the Browder family during that time-frame.  Thus, it was natural for him to take a place among Virginia gentry. 

Following the deaths of Agnes and his son, Isham, Thomas Isham Browder married (2) Talitha Cox, daughter of Capt. John Cox (March 27, 1703-Sept. 13, 1764) and Mary Coleman (c1702-p. 1793) of Mecklenburg County, Virginia (Mecklenburg County Will Book and Marriage Records 3, p. 125).  Talitha Cox was born about 1745 (an estimate because no record has been found) in Brunswick (the part that later became Mecklenburg) County, Virginia, and died on July 3, 1828 in Rockingham (formerly Guilford) County, North Carolina, aged about 83 years.  Isham Browder and Talitha Cox were married on February 3, 1767, within Cumberland Parish, Lunenburg County, Virginia.  

It has become obvious to me that Isham Browder and Talitha Cox had been introduced to one another by none other than Col. Augustine Claiborne. Augustine had been very well acquainted with the Tatum family as records indicate. Augustine would also have known Talitha because she was a relative. So, following the demise of Agnes and her son, he would have introduced Isham to Talitha. This alone shows that Isham Browder and Augustine Claiborne likely knew one another early on and probably both served together during the French and Indian Wars. 

Isham and Talitha Browder probably first resided together in Virginia (but not for very long), probably in Halifax County (obviously on the homestead which Isham already owned) prior to moving to Wolf Island Creek in Rowan County, North Carolina, about 1767/68.  And it is likely that Isham had some part in the War of the Regulators not long after arrival there.  But, it becomes obvious that Isham Browder was active in politics there right from the beginning. 

Isham and Talitha Browder were originally among the landed Virginia gentry and, again, Isham had been a member of the Church of England.  But it seems obvious that he and Talitha had become dissenters from the established Church of England for unknown reasons.  Because of this, they would have faced religious persecution in Virginia and left, as many others, for the same reason, also did.  Isham and Talitha Browder moved to the northern part of Rowan County, North Carolina, again, about 1768, which later became Guilford County in 1771, and still later became Rockingham County, on December 29, 1785. 

It would have been just prior to, or soon after, the marriage of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox that Isham Browder would have purchased about 150 acres of land in Rowan County, North Carolina from Thomas Brandon (1741-Feb. 5, 1802), as he indicated in his extant will of 1779. But this is not the parcel of land upon which they settled. Thomas Brandon, then, soon moved on to what would later become Union County, South Carolina (just one indication that Isham Browder likely intended to move to South Carolina himself. Isham and Talitha made the decision to remain, while others moved on). 

They became very land-wealthy, owning much land and other property in North Carolina.  Again, it is unknown what property Isham may have owned in Virginia prior to their marriage, but it was probably substantial.  And Isham must have been instrumental to the formation of Guilford County, governmentally, since he was active in that county’s politics from the very beginning.  And while many families moved to this area over time, most of them eventually continued southward into South Carolina, or eastward into Kentucky and Tennessee, traveling on the Great Wagon Road, while this Browder family remained where they settled.  In fact, their property would have been in a prime location for travelers moving into, and beyond, that area.  It would have been the first place that many travelers would have likely passed through after leaving Virginia and, therefore, a prime retail location.  

So, it is likely that this Browder family sold merchandise to, and otherwise accommodated, travelers who were on their way to other parts of the country.  And it is known that people traveled freely between North Carolina and Virginia in that particular area, the nearest important Virginia town being that of Danville, in any case.  

According to local historian/genealogist, Bob Carter, the area where they settled had become known as “Browder’s Crossroads”, and the land that this couple owned was some of the best land in the county, which included rich bottom-lands situated along the creek, suitable for growing corn (which is what everyone wanted back then).  So, they got in the area early, made surveys, and purchased the best land. 

The major part of Isham Browder’s property in Rowan/Guilford County was located on the north side of Wolf Island Creek, a branch of the Dan River, which at one time included at least 3,818 acres of land altogether.  However, most of this was actually granted to Isham Browder by the State of North Carolina after his death, it seems, although he would have already applied for it, and paid fees for it.  

Wolf Island Creek is almost literally just across the border from Virginia, and it bordered the then Virginia counties of Henry, Halifax, and Pittsylvania.  In fact, it is situated in such a position geographically that William Byrd himself almost had to have traversed this very place when he and others surveyed the lines between Virginia and North Carolina some years before Isham and Talitha Browder moved there.  

In addition to land holdings, the Isham Browder family owned cattle, sheep and hogs, as well as five horses and eight slaves by the time Isham wrote his Will of 1779.  This is similar to the situation of the Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) family of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, but with one key difference; Isham Browder appears to have received significantly more land, and was of greater social prominence, evidently, than Richard Browder who, though he married well, could not be classed, as far as I can tell, as a member of the Virginia gentry because he did not own enough property and does not appear to have participated openly in either the Colonial or the Continental political process. 

It must be stated here that it has been surmised by some early researchers that Isham and Talitha Browder could have moved to Guilford County, North Carolina about 1779, because there was no fighting taking place in that area at that time, although there would be as soon as a year later.  However, newer evidence from extant records clearly shows that they resided in Rowan County as early as 1768, at the very latest. And they were certainly not attempting to escape areas in Virginia where fighting was taking place.  Again, they probably moved mainly for religious and economic reasons, as many others had also done.  

It is also true that land was somewhat less expensive in North Carolina, so Isham could have purchased more than he had owned previously in Virginia.  In fact, according to John Hill Wheeler, early North Carolina land grants were “more desirable, because, both as to terms of entry, and yearly taxes, they were less burdensome than the price and levies imposed by the laws of Virginia.”  And, again, according to Mr. Bancroft (Reminiscences and Memoirs), as quoted by Wheeler:

Tithes were imposed and exacted inexorably [in Virginia] and the Governor and Council were empowered to compel ‘non-conformists to depart the colony with all convenience.’ [And] . . . The earliest settlements in the colony [of North Carolina] were made by people who fled from religious persecutions in Virginia. [In addition, T]here were schools in North Carolina before the Revolution but they were not given Royal Charter due to being sponsored by those of Dissenting faiths. The County of Guilford, including the present counties of Randolph (formed in 1770) and Rockingham (formed in 1785), was settled about 1760, the south and west part by Quakers from Pennsylvania and Nantucket; the north and east by the Presbyterians and Baptists (Wheeler, p. 170).

So, one clearly sees here that Isham and Talitha Browder had almost undeniably adopted either the Baptist or, more likely, the Presbyterian faith, and had left Virginia, probably primarily for that reason.  Thus, even though the spark of Methodism had begun to sweep the counties of Virginia where Isham, Talitha and other Browders first resided as early as 1765, I do not believe that Isham and Talitha adopted the Methodist faith themselves. 

Still, it was likely part of the impetus that caused their move to North Carolina.  This religious momentum began primarily after April of 1765 when the Rev. George Whitfield preached a significant sermon on the subject of faith and taxation in the Brick Church near Blanford.  And the Rev. Devereux Jarratt had a singular influence, beginning in 1770/71, when Methodism had its true beginnings in Virginia.  It seems to me that Isham and Talitha moved just before Methodism began to really take hold in the areas of Virginia from which they had moved.  So, they were persecuted for adopting some other faith, again, either the Baptist or the Presbyterian.

It is also possible that they could have moved there along with some other family members, including Jesse Browder and John Browder, who had lands close to them.  But it is, again, not true, as is surmised by previous family researchers, that Isham and Talitha Browder moved to Guilford County to avoid the fighting which was occurring back in Virginia (because they moved prior to the beginning of the Revolution in any case).  

It has also been said that “in the mid-eighteenth century the land that became Guilford County was not easy to reach, and only the most determined settlers were willing to contend with the hardship and anxiety involved in such a journey.  But going to that land could be worth the effort” (Stoesen, p. 1).  Indeed, having visited the area where they resided myself, I can attest to the fact that it would not have been easy at all to reach back during the late 1700s, for much of the land there, and in-between, is even today very hilly and swampy.  Isham and Talitha Browder were true pioneers in every sense of the word, accepting all of the hardships that came along with that, while residing close enough to the border of Virginia to move freely back and forth between the colonies of Virginia and North Carolina, and interact with others in Virginia.  This, as we will see, will become important to their story. 

It also seems obvious to me that, aside of religious reasons, Isham and Talitha Browder moved primarily because they wanted better opportunity for their family, and Isham also wanted to separate himself from Tidewater Virginia society in general, even though Guilford County, North Carolina was, again, essentially just across the border from the area where they had already resided.  After all, Virginia was full of Browders and other relatives, not to mention a few others named Isham Browder. 

Isham Browder, then, likely moved, in addition to religious reasons, in order to set himself apart; something I suspect the Isham Browder (June 12, 1762-Feb. 19, 1830) of the American Revolutionary War may also have had in mind as he moved to Kentucky.  Being members of the landed gentry of Virginia (the first Browders to my knowledge to actually attain such a status) perhaps was not enough for Isham and Talitha Browder when faced with competition from so many relatives and others in Virginia. However, they still remained close enough to relatives in Virginia that regular contact could easily be managed. 

So, rather than moving on to South Carolina as originally intended along with friends Thomas Brandon and Thomas Young, Isham and Talitha Browder chose to remain on Wolf Island Creek. The reasoning is not known, but it was a place of such potential that it would seem to have been a good decision on their part. 

As a poignant aside, it is sad to consider that Isham’s likely mother, Mary Isham Randolph-Keith, died in December  of 1772, only about four years after this move to North Carolina. Whether Isham ever really knew of her or who she was is not known, but he probably did. But it seems certain that they never actually met. 

Again, local historian and genealogist, Bob Carter, found mention of Isham Browder in the Rowan County, North Carolina Court Minutes as early as July 21, 1768; Isham at that time being appointed as an overseer of the road from the Orange County line on the north side of the Dan River, crossing the lower Sorow [Saura] Town Ford, and from there to John Walker’s. 

Guilford County was made from part of Rowan County after this time, in 1771.  So, we now know that this couple was residing in this area as early as July of 1768.  So, they weren’t married long before they moved on to Rowan County. 

Thus, the earliest records from Guilford County, North Carolina, begin in 1771.  From Guilford County Deed Book 1:

January 5, 1771 – Joseph Tate, Sr. of Orange County deeded 650 acres to Robert Ralstone, Jr., his son-in-law, on both sides of Jacob’s Creek at the waters of the Dann River. Witnesses were Edward Hunter, George Peay, ISHAM BROWDER, and Phil Deatherage. This deed was proven in February of 1772 by Hunter (p. 74).

So, immediately upon its formation, Isham Browder served as a Justice of the Court of Pleas & Quarter Sessions of Guilford County, North Carolina; obvious since he proved land deeds there and, along with others such as Ephraim Potter, would sign deeds as “Jurat” (Jurist); essentially a notary public.  But, notary publics during that time period held broader powers than those today.  These Justices saw to the maintenance of roads, the upkeep of the courthouse, the supervision of records, and the levying of taxes (Stoesen, p. 9).  Isham served, then, in this capacity from January of 1771 to at least 1775, according to the records. 

Later, from the available records, it becomes clear that Isham Browder held the same position, and had his own district, at least during the years 1781 and 1782.  Since there is a break in the records during the war years, it seems obvious that Isham Browder was a Jurist from 1771 to at least 1782. 

And Talitha herself was often at Isham’s side, as demonstrated from the early records.  She even witnessed deeds along with him.  They were obviously a power-house couple of early North Carolina politics.  And following Isham’s early demise, Talitha doggedly carried on Isham’s legacy all the way up to her own death many years later, as will be shown.

According to Guilford County Deed Book [GCDB] 1, the following actions were early taken by Isham Browder (and once by Talitha, his wife).

Thomas (name smudged, but barely readable) Isham Browder was a witness to the marriage of William Donnell (son of Robert Donnell and Mary) and Mary Bell (daughter of Samuel Bell), license dated February 9, 1773.  The bondsman was William Denny (Thompson, p. 47).

The preceding is the marriage bond between William Donnell and Mary Bell, witnessed by Thomas Isham Browder (the name Thomas is smudged out, but can still be read, and is the proof of Isham’s first name).  From Colonial Records of North Carolina 1775-1776, (p. 913-914) Vol. X, p. 58 by: William L. Saunders; The Journal of the Provincial Congress of North Carolina, Held at Halifax, November the Twelfth Day, Anno. Dom. 1776.  

Isham was also a witness to the marriage of William Buchanan and Catherine McCaleb (daughter of Catherine McCaleb), license dated March 5, 1773, along with bondsman John Scott (ibid, p. 20).

Isham was also a witness, along with Andrew Scott, to the marriage of John Doak, Sr. and Agnes Ervin (daughter of Robert Ervin, who was also the bondsman), license dated October 29, 1773 (ibid, p. 46).

And Isham was a witness to the Will of Ephraim Potter, along with Thomas Norris, in Guilford County, North Carolina, dated February 28, 1774, and probated in November of 1775 (Guilford County Will Abstracts [GCWA], p. 47). Also, the following excerpts are from Guilford County Deed Book 1:

May 29, 1772 – a deed concerning William Garrott and his wife, Sarah, to Lawrence Bankson was proven by ISHAM BROWDER, but no probate date appears. Witnesses to this deed included both ISHAM BROWDER and his wife, TABITHA (sic) BROWDER (p. 149).

How often in colonial records does one see a woman as a witness to a deed?  Not very often!  This could certainly indicate her own personal position in society. Also,

August 20, 1772 – Ephraim Potter of Guilford County deeded 100 acres to Thomas Lovelatty of Guilford County on Wolf Island Creek. Witnesses were Seth Stubblefield, ISHAM BROWDER and Larkin Pierpoint. The deed was proven during the November 1772 term by Mr. Pierpoint (pp. 198-99).

November 27, 1772 – George Parks of Guilford County to William Wiley of same, planter, forty pounds, 600 acres, signed by George Parks and Anna Parks; witnesses: James Wilson, Ralph Gorrell; proven February 1773 Term by Gorrell (p. 207). 

February 9, 1773 – James Coleman of Guilford to John Leak of same, one hundred sixty pounds, 125 acres, on N. side of Dan River, signed: James Coleman; witnesses: Samuel Henderson, ISHAM BROWDER, William Henderson; acknowledged February 1773 Term (p. 207).

The above James Coleman would have been a son of Samuel Coleman, Sr. and Ann Mourning Christian. James Coleman married Elizabeth Leake, daughter of Walter Leake (son of William Leake and Mary Bostick) and Judith Mask. Walter and Judith Leake had another son, John Leake, who is the John Leake mentioned above. Samuel Coleman, Sr. was a son of John Coleman and Mary Claiborne and, therefore, a brother of Talitha’s mother, Mary Coleman. 

From Colonial Records of North Carolina 1775-1776, (p. 913-14; actual copy on previous page) Vol. X, p. 58 by: William L. Saunders; “The Journal of the Provincial Congress of North Carolina, Held at Halifax, November the Twelfth Day, Anno. Dom. 1776”: 

January 4, 1775 – Thomas Lovelatty, Jr. and his wife, Leah, of Guilford County deeded 300 acres to Moses Armistead, merchant, of Pittsylvania County, Virginia on Wolf Island Creek, a branch of the Dann River. Witnesses included ISHAM BROWDER, Sampson Bethel and William Grizsel. It was proven during the May 1775 term by ISHAM BROWDER (p. 360). 

August 15, 1775 – Wyatt Stubblefield of Orange County deeded 164 acres to John Stubblefield of Guilford County.

This latter entry was proven during the August 1775 term, but the jurist is not named (p. 360).  Based on the dates, it is quite probable that this jurist was Isham Browder.

August 19, 1778 – Edward Hunter and his wife Elizabeth of Guilford County deeded 386 acres to Isaac Whitworth and Alexander McClaran of Guilford County. Witnessed by ISHAM BROWDER and Thomas Henderson. Acknowledged August 1778 term (p. 464).

Now, it is known that those who had been members of the House of Burgesses in Virginia and North Carolina, which was abolished in late 1775 and early 1776, would still continue to hold clandestine meetings, calling themselves “The Convention.”  Isham Browder appears to have been a member of this “Convention” in North Carolina.  They had to meet clandestinely because their meetings were essentially no longer legal.  These secret meetings were probably the impetus which allowed Isham Browder to be in a position to later become selected as a delegate to the Fifth Revolutionary Provincial Congress of North Carolina.  Also, from Saunders:

At a congress begun and held at Halifax, in the county of Halifax, the twelfth day of November, in the year of our Lord one thousand seven hundred and seventy-six.

It being certified that the following persons were duly elected and returned to the respective Counties and Towns, to-wit, Guilford – Messrs. David Caldwell, Joseph Hinds, Ralph Gorrell, Charles Bruce, ISHAM BROWDER. . .

So, “Before the Declaration of Independence by Congress at Philadelphia, the Provincial Congress of North Carolina, then assembled at Halifax (on the 13th of April, 1776), appointed a committee of its ablest men to prepare a civil constitution” (Wheeler, p. 84).

Isham Browder’s election to the Fifth Revolutionary Provincial Congress, as indicated above, was as one of five delegates from Guilford County to the Halifax, North Carolina Constitutional Convention of December 12, 1776, which ensured that he was among those who were elected for the purpose of drawing up the original Constitution of the State of North Carolina, and to continue to meet thereafter to form the state’s laws.  In said capacity, Isham Browder is shown to have served as a witness during a hearing, on November 20, 1776, during which Ralph Gorrell also appeared with him, while the other delegates from Guilford County are not mentioned.  

In said capacity, Isham Browder also voted on a bill on December 7, 1776, according to the same records.  This bill concerned a proposed payment by the residents of Halifax County, North Carolina to the Continental Army in which Isham Browder was among several who voted “Nay.”  He was in the minority and the bill, therefore, passed above his objection.  Only two other members from Guilford County were present for this vote, Ralph Gorrell and Rev. David Caldwell, both of whom voted “Yea.” (p. 960).  Certainly, at least in this instance, Isham Browder failed to take the prevailing position.  

Isham’s colleague, Ralph Gorrell, later deeded the land upon which the Guilford county seat of Greensboro was established (Stoesen, p. 8).  I have a feeling that the two were actually friends, even though they obviously disagreed sometimes politically. The same goes for Rev. David Caldwell, who had established his Log College very early, and Charles Bruce. 

The record here presented shows that, again, in all likelihood, Isham Browder was, or had been, a member of the House of Burgesses “The Convention” in North Carolina, since it is financial matters about which he was called upon to cast a vote in this instance.  This, along with his being a Justice as a member of the Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions, proves his interest and activity in early Continental politics.  But this activity is not well documented, so the researcher has to piece these things back together, as I have sought to do.

Contrary to what I had proposed before, it seems obvious that Isham Browder was mentioned in the records sparsely not because he was not present, but simply because the records are not detailed enough to have mentioned every instance in which a vote was cast or a committee was formed. It seems obvious to me now that Isham Browder and the others were present the entire time, serving on various committees and that this one vote was detailed in the records because it was such a close vote that each member had to be recorded as to which vote he cast. So, it is simply not known exactly which committees he and others may have been on except for sparse mentions of such. Regardless, Isham Browder did take part in, perhaps, North Carolina’s most important political effort during the Revolutionary period.  And it seems obvious that he was well on the way to something bigger politically, but that illness may have circumvented this, and his untimely death ended it.  

There is also good evidence that Isham Browder served as a Captain in the county’s militia during the American Revolutionary War.  This evidence first comes from a paper entitled “Colonel Absolom Bostick” by Harry Z. Tucker, written in 1938.  In it Tucker writes:

Don Ferdinand Bostick [a son of Col. Absolom Bostick], who married Elizabeth Rand of Halifax County Virginia died in 1824 and left several children.  Only one is known, William Rand Bostick [1803-after 1870], who married Miss Browder, a descendant of CAPTAIN ISHAM BROWDER, a soldier of the Revolution.

Today, we know that William R. Bostick’s wife was Jane Browder, daughter of Thompson and Tabitha Browder, because court records show that.  Also, the Isham Browder (1762-1830) who is generally known to have served during the American Revolutionary War was not a Captain, but was a Private as long as he served in the war.  It is also certain that this Jane Browder could not have been his descendant, because we know who all of his children were and who their children were and none of them were named Jane.

Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) would surely have at least been an officer during the Revolution due to his status in society, and could easily have been a Captain in the militia.  However, for some time I had not found any documentation that referred to him in that way to corroborate the paper that Tucker wrote, although he was often referred to in various records as “Gentleman” and “Esquire” (and Tucker could have misread a document listing “Gent. Isham Browder” as “Capt Isham Browder”, an easy mistake given the handwriting of the day).

However, it is also certain that this Jane Browder was not his descendant either, because we know the names of all of his children and grandchildren, and none of them were named Jane.  And, as previously mentioned, she was, in fact, a daughter of Thompson Browder (son of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia), who is not known to have served during the Revolution (although he could have).  One must remember that this paper was written in 1938, more than 200 years after Isham’s birth, and Tucker’s sources are unknown.  So, there can be little doubt that it does contain some factual errors.  But, as with almost any document, it also contains some truths. 

William R. Bostick, son of Don Ferdinand Bostick and Elizabeth Rand, and Jane Browder were married in Surry (later Stokes) County, North Carolina, about, or just before, 1833.  There, they resided along the Dan River.  This was quite close to where Isham and Talitha Browder had resided with their family in Rowan/Guilford/Rockingham County, North Carolina. 

Getting back to Tucker’s paper, it is also true that Col. Absolom Bostick (c1740-1803) and his first wife, Bethenia, moved to Surry (later Stokes) County, North Carolina from Pittsylvania County, Virginia early on, where they had purchased land along both sides of the Dan River.  Their son, Don Ferdinand Bostick (March 9, 1772, Pittsylvania Co., Virginia-1824, Stokes Co., North Carolina), again, married Elizabeth Rand, daughter of William Rand, on May 28, 1799.  

Their children were Bethenia, William Rand, Anna Rand, Elizabeth “Betsy”, Absalom, Ferdinand, Wesley, James Pinkney, John Thornton, Louisa, and David Jackson.  None of this, to my knowledge, is in dispute.

The pitfalls of genealogy research can be exemplified here, in that previously I had assumed that Jane Browder, daughter of Thompson and Tabitha Browder, had married Joseph E. Browder prior to marrying William R. Bostick, and had had several children prior to her marriage to Bostick.  The fact of the matter was that I was in error on that point.  When I realized that she had been married to Bostick as early as prior to 1836, because he is mentioned in court documents as being her husband in that year, then I knew that I had made this error, and worked to change it (which is something that any good researcher should be willing to do when confronted with actual evidence).  Further evidence that she had had children with Mr. Bostick solidified the necessity for changes.  BUT, that did not mean that the entirety of my research here was of no value, as will be shown. 

Here it should again be emphasized that Jane was NOT a descendant of any Isham Browder whosoever, period.  AND, she is not one and the same as the wife of Joseph E. Browder, found in the censuses either.  That much is now clear. 

The idea that she was a daughter, or even a descendant, of any Isham Browder is something that Tucker had wrong.  BUT, he may have been on the right trail because, according to census records, we don’t know where Joseph E. Browder came from, other than that he was born in Virginia.  

It is of interest to note that Gen. William Bethell (also of the American Revolutionary War), friend of Isham, became an attorney and, later, a North Carolina State Senator from Rockingham County, serving in 1790, 1815, and again from 1817 to 1820.  He was also a member of the House of Commons from 1786 to 1789, and again from 1828 to 1829.  Also, Ralph Gorrell, Esq. was elected by the Court of Pleas & Quarter Sessions and appointed to the office of Register of Guilford County in November of 1784 (Stockard, p. 22).  One wonders if Isham Browder might have been elected to such a position also, had he lived just a few more years.

I have become certain that Isham Browder took part in the Rutherford Expedition during which Griffith Rutherford, by this time a Brigadier General, led members of the North Carolina militia from the Salisbury District, which included Guilford County, on a campaign against the Cherokee. This expedition started out on Sept. 1, 1776 but was cut short due to lack of supplies. For this expedition, it is known that every able bodied man was ordered to take part. If Isham Browder didn’t know Griffith Rutherford prior to this campaign, he certainly came to know him at this time. And Rutherford was also among those who formed the Constitution and Bill of Rights of North Carolina in 1776. Someone who was almost elected, but was not due to a dispute concerning his residency, was Charles Gilly/Gilley (b. C1750, Virginia), son of Francis Gilly/Gilley and Elizabeth. He is also known to have taken part in the Rutherford Expedition and was also one of the witnesses to the first will of Isham Browder (1778), they likely having met during this campaign. 

It is also likely that Thomas Isham Browder had some part in the Battle of King’s Mountain during the American Revolutionary War, which took place on October 6, 1780 and, almost certainly, in the Battle of Guilford Courthouse – later named Martinsville in honor of Governor Alexander Martin in 1785 (Corbitt, p. 113), which occurred on March 15, 1781, and had, therefore, possibly suffered some ill effects from those conflicts.  

It is obvious that Isham Browder would have frequented Guilford Courthouse in performance of his duties as a County Justice in any case.  So, he already well knew where it was located and how to get there.  And he could not very well stay out of the events that took place in that area during that time either.  After all, Gen. Nathanial Greene had recently crossed into Virginia from North Carolina by way of the Dan River in order to gather reinforcements, pursued by Gen. Charles, Lord Corwallis.  Once Greene returned, the Battle of Guilford Courthouse commenced.  Isham Browder had to have been a part of it (if he was physically able), again, probably serving as a Captain in the militia.  It was just too close to his home for anything other than this to be the case.  And they needed all the able-bodied men they could get.  

So, regardless of the errors made by Tucker, and, previously, by myself, it still seems obvious based on these things that the person Tucker had in mind was Thomas Isham Browder, and that he was indeed a Captain in the militia. 

In any case, preceding the Battle of Guilford Courthouse, Gen. Cornwallis had been determined to finally crush the rebellion in the American South, especially in the Carolinas and Virginia, where it had become strongest.  However, his southern strategy received a severe setback at King’s Mountain, South Carolina, when South Carolina militia carried the day against his forces.  Afterward his forces received another devastating blow at the Battle of Cowpens, South Carolina in January of 1781.  Cornwallis wanted a showdown, and pursued Gen. Nathaniel Green’s forces into North Carolina.  By mid-March of 1781, Gen. Greene acquired about 4,000 requested militia reinforcements from Virginia and North Carolina and began to prepare for battle in the area of Guilford Courthouse.  This battle is well known to have marked “the beginning of the end of the Revolutionary War” (Stoesen, pp. 3-4), because, although they actually won the battle, the British Army was decimated.  About 300 American troops were lost in that battle, while one-fourth of the British troops were wounded or killed.

This brings about another interesting “coincidence.”  The Isham Browder (1762-1830) who went to Kentucky with his family was wounded during the Battle of Monmouth Courthouse, which was his last battle.  Isham Browder (c1732-c1784), whose descendants mainly went into Tennessee, Alabama and Georgia, had to have taken part in the Battle of Guilford Courthouse and some others.  

This is intriguing.  But what is even more interesting is that it was said incorrectly that Isham Browder (1762-1830) not only fought during the Battle of Cowpens, South Carolina (October 14, 1780), but also fought during the Battle of Guilford Courthouse (March 15, 1781).  This was stated by Isham’s son, John, and can be found in the pension application made by Isham’s widow, Elizabeth.  BUT, again, Isham Browder (1762-1830) did NOT fight in either of these two battles because he had been discharged from service in 1779, and these battles took place in 1781.  The official record, at least, is clear on this point.  Isham Browder (1762-1830) was discharged in 1779, and there is no record that he ever returned to military service in any capacity whatsoever after this time, other than the mention by his son, John.  If his extant record did not exist, it would be easy to surmise, or guess, that he had. 

There is only one reasonable explanation for this discrepancy.  Isham Browder’s (1762-1830) son, John, had heard about these battles, and had probably seen evidence that an Isham Browder had fought during these battles, and assumed that the Isham Browder mentioned was his father.  So, this lends even greater credence to the possibility that it was, instead, Thomas Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) who fought during these battles as a Captain in the militia.  John simply made a mistake, that’s all.  

Since all able-bodied adult white males in North Carolina (unless they had religious scruples) were required by law to serve in the militia (just as in Virginia), it is virtually certain that Thomas Isham Browder took part in these battles, at least.  Even Quakers (probably even our Sanders relatives, discussed in my book, Faces of Our Ancestors), normally exempt from military service due to religious considerations, are known to have taken part in the Battle of Guilford Courthouse, having made the excuse that they were going “squirrel hunting.”  This, because, if they already happened to be in the area “squirrel hunting”, and a battle ensued, they had the right, and even the obligation, to defend themselves and others, according to their religion.  They were allowed to take part for defense, and they did!  And this was a big reason why American forces prevailed. 

So, if even Quakers took part, it has to be understood that everyone knew the gravity of the situation, and no one locally, who was able, refrained from taking part in this battle.  The only way that Isham Browder would not have taken part is if he had not been physically able to do so. 

To lend even further credence to this possibility, the Journal of Capt. Abraham Philips (June 25, 1755–March 23, 1836), who is known to have raised a company of local militia during the Revolutionary War, contains the following notation, “1782: I owe Isham Browder, Esq. £1-0-0”.  The reason the one pound was owed to Isham Browder is not stated, but one can infer that it may have had something to do with the war since Abraham Philips was a militia Captain in said war himself (information from Bob Carter).  

In addition, a notation found in Guilford County Court Minutes from May of 1786 states that William Bethell was to take taxable property to join it into Browder’s District with Philips’, O’Neal’s and Challis’ districts (this info also from Bob Carter).  According to the records, William Bethell was a General during the Revolution, and he was a neighbor of Isham Browder.  He also had a muster ground, where Isham Browder and others would have trained. 

Now, it is also known that an outbreak of smallpox occurred following the Battle of Guilford Courthouse (Stoesen, p. 5-6).  Whatever may be the case, Thomas Isham Browder died essentially just as the Revolution reached its culmination.  It appears that he saw the end of the Revolution, but he was not allowed to see the new nation blossom into greatness.  Although he lived for a few years beyond the time of these battles, he very well could have been wounded, or could have contracted smallpox, from which he certainly recovered, but perhaps not fully.  He could well have been weakened by either, and later died from the effects of either, or both.

A question arises here. If Thomas Isham Browder participated in the American Revolutionary War, why had Talitha not applied for any pension due to his service, as other widows had done?  Such a document would solve everything.  The answer shows itself to be rather complicated, once research concerning such pensions has been done.  But, the simple answer is that, up until the year 1818, a service member himself could not receive a pension unless he had become disabled and was unable to work due to this disability, although he would still receive land grants, etc. for his service. So, Isham himself had not filed for such a pension.  And it was not until 1832 that a widow could apply for a pension, if her husband had not been disabled due to his war service.  But, since Talitha died in 1828, she was unable to apply for such a pension in any case.  Thus, she never applied, and no such record exists to prove Isham’s service.  So, not only is it a sad truth that many who served received nothing more than their pay, while some died in service, but also that their widows and families received nothing, since it took so long for legislation to be passed with respect to this.

In any case, after the North Carolina State Convention was all over, and the North Carolina Bill of Rights and Constitution had been formed, Thomas Isham Browder seems to have been quite satisfied to return to his more or less ordinary life of a Jurist and, possibly also, teaching, since it is also fairly certain that he was an educator.  This is evidenced by later court documentation, in a sworn statement by Abraham “Abram” Philips (detailed later), in which Philips stated that he “went to school to Isham Browder”, meaning that Isham Browder taught him or at least tutored him.  I like to think that Isham was actually an educator and, as Talitha appears also to have had a part in political dealings along with Isham, that she also likely taught school along with him.  This is even more likely if they were both Presbyterians and both taught within a Presbyterian institution, such as the Log College. 

Guilford County is known to have had an early association with education and educational institutions.  The Rev. David Caldwell, a Presbyterian minister and educator (whom, one should remember, was also a member of the 5th Provincial Congress), initiated this educational association, beginning in 1767, when he opened his famous “Log College” (in Rowan County), which was located on a farm in an area that is now northwest Greensboro (Stoesen, p. 3).  There a classical education could be had.  And this was actually the second school offering a classical education that was started within the boundaries of a northern county of North Carolina.

It is entirely possible that Thomas Isham Browder, and Talitha Browder, taught at this very Log College.  If this is true, then it could prove an early association between the Browder family and the Presbyterian Church, as well as with education.  This association with the Presbyterian Church is, in fact, likely, as Isham and Talitha were, again, almost certainly “dissenters” from the Church of England and, therefore, moved to North Carolina mainly because of this. Ralph Gorrell, among others who were associates of this couple, was a Presbyterian. 

And, once again, it appears that Isham Browder was still well on the way to something bigger politically following the convention.  But, the truth is that I really do not know what Isham Browder did, or what his thoughts may have been, during this time.  If we still had his important papers, which Talitha kept until her death, we might know.  But the thing that is certain is that he died, at the latest, by early 1785.  Isham could still have been serving within the realm of the political after 1782, but the record, to my knowledge, is simply not extant now.  That having been said, it is entirely possible that, if he had lived long enough, he might have been selected to have been a delegate from North Carolina to the Constitutional Convention of the United States, which was held in Philadelphia in 1787.  Certainly, his status and background would have allowed for this possibility.  But we will now simply never know what might have been, had he lived even a little longer.

Interestingly, in addition to this, “As a genealogist of both horses and men, Fairfax Harrison wrote that the gentry began to” import and use ‘chariots’ for occasions of ceremony; to drink Madeira instead of bumbo; to ride formally to hounds; and to keep horses for racing only” (Randolph, p. 49).  Thomas Randolph owned a number of horses, which he used for breeding and racing.  Therefore, it is interesting to note that even Isham Browder’s horse ownership is exemplified in the following advertisement placed in the Virginia Gazette at Williamsburg on May 29, 1779 (my copy says 1778 in a note written on the margin) by Isham Browder himself.  The following excerpt (missing words due to a hole in the page), is here presented:

Jack the Bachelor a beautiful dark bay horse lately imported . . . mares in Guilford County, North Carolina, at twenty . . . the money to be paid by the last of July thereof thirty dollars. He is full fifteen hands high nine years old this grass sound and free from . . . and prettily marked. He was got of Thistle Whipper . . . Sire by old Bazaet who was reckoned the best horse in England. In 1752 he was brought into Ireland, and run a match against Black & all Black (esteemed the first horse in that Kingdom) for a thousand guineas on the currah of Kildare; His dam was a mare of Sir Edward O‟Brian, got by Crab, his grandam by Bustard. I have fifteen or twenty acres of timothy pasturage, under good fencing, but will not be answerable for escapes or other accidents.

ISHAM BROWDER

One should note that the reason the advertisement was placed in said newspaper was because it was the most popular and prominent newspaper in the area at that time, both in Virginia and North Carolina.  It is also interesting to note that said horse had been raced in Ireland and was imported from England.  This is the same horse that Isham Browder later left to his son, Isham C. Browder, through his Will.  It is reasonable to assume that, if this horse was not specifically used for racing, it was used for breeding. 

So, here we see that Isham and Talitha Browder owned a horse that they used for horse racing and/or breeding, and certainly made money from the profits therefrom.  This is the first indicator that the early Browders were already into horse racing and breeding, but it is interesting that, with this, there is in fact a direct link back to Ireland, however distant and tenuous.  And, it seems obvious, there is also a link back to the Randolph family.

But this is also possibly where the association between Abraham Philips and the Browder family began because Philips was born in England and came over about this time. It is entirely probable that Isham Browder hired Abraham Philips to help him take care of his newly imported English horse and continued to educate Philips as time went on. 

Now, as already shown, at least as early as January of 1781, Isham Browder appears several times throughout Guilford County documents.  He is often referred to in court documents indirectly, for example, when mention is made of “Isham Browder’s District” or “Mr. Browder’s District” for taxation purposes.  By 1782, he is referred to, along with others such as Ralph Gorrell and William Bethell, as “Esquire.”  The court proceedings, often held at Salisbury, show him and others as being present for several court hearings, Isham Browder being listed in this way as late as November 19, 1782.  These documents come from the Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions.  They mention, for example, that, in August of 1781, John Cook was appointed as tax assessor for Isham Browder’s District; that Archibald Yarbrough was appointed as tax collector in Mr. Browder’s District; or that Daniel Adkins was appointed as Collector of taxes in “Mr. Browder’s District” in August of 1782. 

Also, in May of 1782, Charles Dean, Flower Swift, and Matthew Mills were appointed as overseers in Mr Browder’s, Clark’s, and Callis’ districts, and assessments in those districts formerly made, be made null and void.  In addition, for the same month and year, the same three were appointed as overseers for the whole year.  In August of 1782, it was also ordered that Thomas Sparks, Henry Terril, and Benjamin Parrot be appointed as accessors in “Mr. Browder’s”, Clark’s, and Challis’ districts. 

In August of 1782, Esquires Isham Browder and Hugh Callis were appointed to settle with Archibald Yarbro, Executor of the estate of Isaac Hill, dcsd.  Also in August of 1782, the first assessment made of the districts of Esquires, Isham Browder, Hugh Callis, William Clark was confirmed, with collections and commissions empowered therefrom.  And, in August of 1782, Robert Coleman was appointed as accessor in “Mr. Browder’s” District. 

For the November term of the year 1782, the Justices of the Peace listed as present included Esquires, Isham Browder, James Brown, and Robert McKassie. 

Interestingly, Isham Browder is not listed as being present for any court hearings after this time, while his colleagues, such as Ralph Gorrell, are.  Still, during a Superior Court proceeding at Salisbury on September 15, 1783, Robert Garrott acknowledged a deed to Isham Browder for 100 acres of land.  This is the last time, as far as I can tell, that Isham Browder is referred to directly by name, showing that he was still living at that time, since he is not referred to as being deceased.  But, he was also not present. 

The fact that Isham Browder evidently ceased to participate at court hearings at least as early as September of 1783 leads to one inescapable conclusion – that there must have been a reason that he no longer went to court hearings, while his colleagues did.  The answer to the inherent question here has to be that, (1) either the family had moved elsewhere, or that (2) Isham was already in poor health and could not attend.  In fact, perhaps it was a combination of both, and he was, instead, away on business, expecting to return. 

But, actually, the family had not moved elsewhere, although they could temporarily have been away, perhaps visiting relatives in Virginia.  I believe that it is more likely that Isham was already ill, perhaps even too ill to write another Will, and that he simply never recovered from whatever ailed him.  

The problem with exactly when Isham Browder died becomes even more complicated when one sees in the court documents that his district is still referred to, as late as May of 1784, as “Mr. Browder’s District” or “Isham Browder’s District”, often mentioned (as shown, as early as 1782) in association with the districts of Hugh Challis and William Clark.  One would think that this sort of notation would pinpoint 1784 as the year of his death.  And this might be the case since, by November of 1784, his district is referred to simply as “Browder’s District”.  That notation in the records shows that William Bethell was appointed as collector in the districts of Mr. Callis, William Clark, and “Browder’s”.  It does not say “Mr. Browder’s” or “Isham Browder’s”, this time.  It simply says “Browder’s”. 

Still, never have I seen in any of the court documents a reference to him as actually being “deceased”, even after early 1785.  If such a reference was in said documents, it would be easier to pinpoint when he died.  And, as late as 1787, mention is still made of “Browder’s District”, indicating that it contained 970 acres at that time.  But, he was also certainly deceased by then, even though this is still not noted.  No further mention is made, as far as I can tell, of “Browder’s District” after 1787.  

There is, however, a mention of “Browder’s Executors” from the 1782-1788 Court Minute Docket of the Guilford County Court of Pleas and Quarter Sessions (May 1785 Session), as follows (Morehead, p. 37):

Nichlas McCubbin is appointed Overseer of the road from the Sorrow Town to Quaqua Creek; James McCoIium from thence to the County line of Caswell ; John Odell from the County line of Caswell on the Iron Works Road to William Bethel’s Muster Ground; Natty Jordan from thence to the roads at BROWDER’S EXECUTOR’S; William Hickman overseer of Henderson’s Road from Samuel Bethel’s to Cantrel’s Meeting House; Lawrence Bagston from thence to the Governor’s Road ; David Suttlcs from Manlcave Tarrant’s to Hugh Reeds’; and Hugh Reeds from his own house to Thomas Gray’s. Jacob Williams’ road.

So, we know he was deceased by May of 1785.  The Browder referred to here is clearly Isham.  And the book was written by John Motley Morehead (July 4, 1796 – August 27, 1866), who became Governor of North Carolina, and who clearly knew of Isham Browder. 

The last mention that I am aware of comes from Guilford County Court Minutes, and states that the court ordered William Bethell be allowed 2116 acres of land and twelve poles as a collector in “Browder’s”, Clarks, and Callais’ districts for the year 1787, due to an apparent mistake in the list, showing that Clark’s had 2146 acres and twelve poles; “Browder’s” had 970 acres; and Callais’ had 600 acres. 

Thanks to genealogist/local historian Bob Carter, probably Isham Browder’s initial claim in the area (other than his purchase from Thomas Brandon, which I did not previously have a record of) can here be presented.  It was entered on January 4, 1779, and was granted on October 22, 1782.  Note that the claim was for 640 acres, but that, once the survey was done, it turned out to be 569 acres (North Carolina Land Grant Patent Book [NCLGPB] 48, p. 63; Entry #155; File #479; Grant #470).  Note also that one of the witnesses was Isham Tatum.  The deed in question, plus the plat drawing, follows (courtesy of the North Carolina State Archives):

If, somehow, the reader did not notice – this document refers to Isham Browder as “Captain”.  There can be no mistake here.  I literally poured over the letters of the first bad copy I received, and looked at them on computer in a number of ways.  It says “Capt.”  Later I obtained a better copy. So, there can no longer be any doubt that this man, Thomas Isham Browder, was indeed a Captain in the local Guilford County militia during the Revolutionary War, just as his friend, Abraham Philips (who surveyed the above), also was.  Perhaps Tucker had seen this document; something I had not seen in all of these years previous. 

It must here be noted that these land survey documents from the state are not archived in inventory books like the deeds are.  Therefore, there is no reference source that can be given for this survey order other than copies are at the State Archives.  It should also be noted that the militia records for Guilford and Rockingham counties are missing, so they can no longer be consulted.  

In any case, after receiving this copy of said document from Mr. Carter, I ordered this and the four other land grant documents from the North Carolina State Archives.  The information I received contained several pages, including a copy of the sleeve that contained said documents.  Two copies of that sleeve, courtesy of the North Carolina State Archives, follows, clearly showing the name “Capt. Isham Browder”.  

There can be no mistake here.  Tucker must have seen this document and, from that, extrapolated that Jane Browder was the descendant of this Capt. Isham Browder.  In effect, Tucker assumed that Isham Browder was her ancestor, but he had no real evidence for that.   Tucker just made a mistake, that’s all. 

Please also note that the above Isham Tatum appears to have been a son of John Tatum and Mary Eppes. Again, John and Mary Tatum are known to have had a daughter, Mary, who married Thomas Young, Sr., friend of Isham Browder. 

When General Nathanael Greene’s army was encamped in the county at Speedwell Furnace on Troublesome Creek in March of 1781, Abraham Philips was serving as a scout and guide for Col. William Washington’s (a second cousin to Gen. George Washington) cavalry.  In the fall of 1781, the Guilford County militia was recruited in the campaign commanded by Gen. Griffith Rutherford, forcing the British to evacuate Wilmington.  During this campaign, Philips was a Captain of his own company.  Philips later wrote about his experiences, stating that he had been in five skirmishes with Tories.  He remained a militia officer after the war, becoming a Colonel in command of the Rockingham County regiment by November 1810.  In May of 1811, Philips was elected Brigadier General of the Fourth Division, serving until his resignation in 1817 (Butler).  

And concerning Gen. William Bethell (1744-1804), during the Revolutionary War, he had been a Captain in the Guilford County militia, which was commanded by Col. James Martin.  During the Moore’s Creek Bridge campaign of 1776, Bethell and the militia took part in the occupation of Cross Creek.  He was again in service by 1779, when the county militia was called upon to disperse a Tory force in the Yadkin River Valley.  By the summer of 1781, he was in command of a troop of mounted infantry from Guilford County during Gen. Rutherford’s successful campaign to force the British evacuation of Wilmington.  During this campaign, Bethell served under the command of Col. Robert Smith in the skirmish at Moore’s Plantation (Butler; Powell, pp. 147-48). 

From these two sketches, one can obtain a picture of two of Isham’s close associates and neighbors (and probable friends), demonstrating their respective parts in the Revolution.  One can picture Isham Browder doing the same types of things during the war, but what engagements he might have taken part in is unknown because the records are no longer extant.  However, it does seem safe to state that he must have taken part in the Battle of Alamance (War of the Regulation) in 1771, the Moore’s Creek Bridge Campaign (occupation of Cross Creek) in 1776, the Rutherford Expedition of 1776, the Battle of Cowpens, South Carolina in 1781, and the Battle of Guilford Courthouse in 1781, at least, and probably the Yadkin River Valley Campaign (the Battle of Shallow Ford) in 1780 and the Wilmington Campaign (skirmish at Moore’s Plantation) in 1781.

Since Jesse, son of Thompson Browder, was born sometime after 1786, the most likely probability is that this property was granted (posthumously) to another Joseph “Jesse” Browder, who married Mary Thompson Browder, daughter of Richard Browder and Mary, since the Jesse Browder had been killed during the American Revolutionary War about a year prior to the granting of said lands.

Finally, Isham Browder apparently wrote two Wills, the first made on September 21, 1778 (although it seems to have been lost), and the second made on September 27, 1779.  But this fact did not come to light until after Talitha’s death in 1828.  For whatever reason, Talitha chose to keep direct knowledge of the two Wills from the rest of the family during her lifetime, and if she had destroyed Isham’s estate papers, then the information would forever have remained a secret.  But she did not.

In the Will of September, 1779, Isham left all of his property to Talitha during her lifetime or widowhood, except for two horses, which he left to his children, Isham C. and Talitha Cox Browder, respectively.  Isham C. Browder inherited the horse named “Jack the Bachelor”, the imported English horse.

 It is possible that members of the family owned descendants of this horse as late as the generation of Lee Roy Browder, my great-great grandfather.  After all, horse racing was very popular during the early days of McNairy County, Tennessee, where the family later moved to, and it is obvious that the Browder family participated in this industry.

It should be noted that the extant Will of Isham Browder (the 1779 Will) names only his first two (then living) children, because the rest of his children were born after this Will was made.  Why he did not make a new Will later to include them is unknown, but he certainly never intended for them to be left completely out of his estate.  Still, Isham C. Browder did all he could to adhere to the provisions of his father’s extant Will, once his mother, Talitha, died.

It should also be noted that Isham’s Will does not mention Agnes or his first son by her, Isham, Jr.  This is more evidence to show that both had previously died, if that is even a question anymore.  So, the first Isham, Jr., therefore, died without issue as he was too young prior to his death, being between eight and nine years of age, to have had issue.  Whatever malady or misfortune which had befallen Agnes and her son, Isham, Jr., may never be known. but they certainly were deceased by the time Isham Browder remarried, and then later wrote his two known Wills.

It seems likely that Isham Browder made his Wills while Isham C. Browder was still an infant mainly because times were very frightening then and the specter of death, especially due to the war, was a very real threat.  I am certain that he was also so very pleased to have had another son he could name after himself, since his first son had died, and he wanted to make certain that the bulk of his estate was passed down to this son, thus keeping it pretty-much all together.  He was obviously determined to ensure that his dynastic legacy would be preserved intact.  The untimely death of Isham Browder ensured, however, that this would only take effect in part.  Either way, Isham Browder obviously made an effort to ensure that his eldest son, Isham C. Browder, inherited the bulk of his property after his death, but not all of it.

Strong and resourceful, Talitha Browder did a wonderful job keeping the family property mostly intact during her lifetime, regardless of what other family members may have believed after her death.  In fact, I submit that the very reason she never remarried was exactly so that her children could have the benefits of this estate as long as she lived.  So, if she hid Isham’s Wills, as some family members believed following her death, then she did it for that reason.

There was a lot of litigation over the estate of Isham Browder once his dual Wills came to light.  In fact, it is known as one of the first major probate cases in the United States.  A petition was first filed on July 31, 1830, which contains the names of all of Isham’s children, the names of most of their marriage partners, and the names of several of the grandchildren (Penland Browder, p. 131/Rockingham County Court Records Book B, pp. 86-89).  The case was in court from July 31, 1830 to 1868, and the defendants were Isham C. Browder and Clement Whittemore, Jr.

When Isham C. Browder died in 1832, not long after the suit had been initiated, his son, Richard Browder (1800/01-1885), continued the legal battle.  The suit was eventually aborted in 1868 “for lack of interested parties.”  Apparently, all suitors had finally given up.  After all, everyone, including Richard, had moved away anyway.  Still, the legal battle certainly took its toll on at least two generations of Browders.  But the original Isham Browder homestead property of about 1,280 acres of land did not go to Isham C. Browder, as his father had intended.  It was divided, as best as was possible, between the children of Isham and Talitha Browder.

Isham Browder’s extant Will of 1779 was not actually probated until November of 1833, due to the way these proceedings took place.  The following persons served on the jury which finally proved the second Will; George D. Boyd, James Roberts, Robert Strong, William M. Wall, Samuel Godsey, Coleman Brann, George F. Thomas, James Dillon, John Price, Thomas Barnett, Daniel Stone, and Jeremiah Hopper (Webster, p. ?).

Capt. Isham Browder resided in Guilford County for only about sixteen years altogether.  But Talitha resided there for a number of years more than that.  The Browder family, in fact, resided in Guilford/ Rockingham County for a total of about 76 years, at which point they had all moved elsewhere, to other frontier lands.  

As for “Old Miss Talitha”, as she came to be called, she performed several transactions of estate herself, about the time of, and following, Isham’s untimely death.  She especially seemed intent upon ensuring that her youngest son, Augustine, was to have property in his own right.  At least, that is how it would appear.  For example, on August 10, 1784, Talitha and her infant son, Augustine Claiborne Browder, purchased 640 acres of land, located on Wolf Island Creek, from John Browder.  This would have been land, situated on both sides of Wolf Island Creek, that John Browder was granted by the State of North Carolina on October 22, 1782, having been entered on January 4, 1779 (Guilford County North Carolina Land Grants Book 3, p. 84). In the end, since Isham had died early, Talitha had the greatest overall influence upon her children, who never really knew their father.  In fact, only Isham knew him at all, it seems, and that very little.  So, the family scenario played, in part, out once again in this generation since Isham Browder had also not even known his own father, Arthur, or even his likely mother, Mary.  He would not have known any of his grandparents either.  The exception to this was that, in this generation, they did know their mother well and would have known Richard Browder at least a little bit, if not also his wife, Mary.  

I believe that the fact that they had a strong mother, whom they knew, was to their advantage.  Talitha had to be strong in every way for her children.  She spent the balance of 44 years as a widow, no doubt fending off suitor after suitor over the course of all those years, from the end of a revolution, a very frightful time for a widow to remain unmarried (but she was by no means the only one to do this), into the early 1800s. 

Now, not only is Talitha not listed by name in the 1790 census of Rockingham County, North Carolina (or any other census, as far as I could find), she is also not listed in the county’s tax rolls for that year.  Isham C. Browder is also not listed in said census or tax rolls.  This clearly shows that the family was elsewhere at that time.

By 1800, however, the family is once again found in Rockingham County, North Carolina, so they had returned by that time.  This census lists “Tiletha” Browder as head of household, along with a female over the age of 45, besides Talitha, residing within the same household with her.  There was also one female between the ages of 10-15, and one male between the ages of 16-25, within the same household.  This listed male must have been Augustine.  The younger female must have been Talitha’s daughter, Martha.  

The older female is unknown.  Talitha’s mother, Mary Coleman-Cox, had died in Wilkes County, Georgia prior to this time.  And the unknown woman is unlikely to have been Mary Browder either, since Richard Browder was still living and, therefore, she is unlikely to have been residing elsewhere.  This older female could, in fact, have been Mary Herbert-Claiborne, although it is believed by researchers that she died in King William County, Virginia.  But there is a family relationship.  This is, of course, speculation on my part.  This family, in any case, had ten listed slaves in 1800.  And they are listed directly under the family of “Isam” Browder (Isham C. Browder). 

The 1810 census of Rockingham County lists “Telitha” Browder.  According to said census, there was only one female residing with Talitha in that year, aged between 16-25.  This, again, must have been her daughter, Martha.  “Old Miss Talitha” owned twelve listed slaves that year.  

This census also shows that Talitha could read and write.  Of course, we already know this since Talitha made her own Will after Isham’s death.  In fact, one would have to conclude that Talitha was well-educated for her day.  This is another indication of her abilities, which makes it likely that she was also an educator, like Isham probably was.  Other neighbors of note, listed in the 1810 census of Rockingham County, were Mary Hendricks, William Chapman, and William Wright, who established Wright Tavern at Wentworth in 1816. 

The 1820 census of Rockingham County shows Talitha Browder as residing alone as a single head of household.  That year Talitha owned eight male slaves and six female slaves.  Talitha is listed next to the families of Isham C. Browder and Elizabeth Browder (widow of Augustine).  Also listed close by are William Lawson, James Dillon and William Bethell, as well as several Wall families, among others.

Talitha Browder is known, therefore, to have sold at least 825 acres of land altogether.  But the question arises as to just exactly what land she sold.  In other words, did she sell any part of the 1,280 acres that was supposed to go to Isham C. Browder according to his father’s Will?  It appears that she did not, according to the reading of said deeds.  That would have left the 1,280 acre plantation (presumably two land grants of 640 acres apiece) intact, and she obviously continued to reside on this tract of land until her death.  But, once again, it was later divided among the children. 

The 640 acre tract she sold on November 6, 1795 was the acreage received on August 27, 1783 and was not part of the 1,280 acre tract.  The 640 acre tract that she purchased on August 10, 1784 from John Browder must have been acquired by the descendants of Augustine Claiborne Browder to become part of the 689 acre tract that his heirs sold in 1834/35.  It would appear that what she otherwise sold was the bulk of one of the 200 acre tracts granted to Isham on November 8, 1784.

So, it seems obvious that Capt. Isham Browder had passed away by August 10, 1784, since it seems doubtful that Talitha Browder would have entered into such a transaction on her own, along with her infant, Augustine Claiborne Browder, prior to Isham’s death.  

The North Carolina General Assembly enacted legislation which created Rockingham County from the northern part of Guilford County on December 29, 1785 (Butler, p. ?), so it is, therefore, absolutely certain that Isham Browder was deceased by this date because he is known to have died in Guilford County (that, according to testimony by his friend, “Abram” Philips, which we will also get to later).  And, again, he is shown to have been deceased by May of that year, in any case.  Beyond this, what is absolutely certain is that Talitha obviously handled personal and financial affairs with little or no difficulty, even in an age in which women basically had no legal rights by themselves; preserving the vast estate left to her on her own.  After Isham’s death, Talitha really proved herself because she had to!  She had to make things work for the sake of her children.  That was some woman! 

In addition, there is absolute proof that Isham Browder was deceased by May 16, 1787, since Talitha is called “Widdow Browder” in a deed from the State of North Carolina to John Linder, which was made on that date.

This certainty remains the case even though Isham Browder’s property is named directly in a deed, dated June 5, 1788, between William Spiers of Surry County, Virginia and John Harris, in which Harris received 300 acres of land, located on Lickfork Creek in Rockingham County, North Carolina, from Spiers.  Other adjoining tracts were owned by John Hancock, William Overby, and Silas Brock.  The witnesses were Daniel Atkins, Nathaniel Newman, and Mary Newman.

Some things become perfectly evident when an examination of Isham Browder’s Will is performed.  First, the horse mentioned in the newspaper article was one and the same as Jack the Bachelor, mentioned in Isham’s Will.  Second, it seems obvious that Isham expected to die fairly soon, since he bequeathed living animals to his children and wife.  If he had expected to live a longer life, then, surely, he would not have bequeathed living (and named) horses to Talitha (his wife), Isham and Talitha (his daughter).  Frankly, it is obvious that this is all the progeny he expected to ever have when he wrote his Will.  Most people in that day in time wrote a Will only when they were expecting to die soon.  So why would Isham have been any different?  The answer is that he wouldn’t have been.  But, that still does not explain why he never wrote another one.

It is also a foregone conclusion that, even though he, at that time, had no other male children, that he would not have changed his Will significantly in any case later once he did have another male child.  The reason is, because in that time-period it was still uncommon for someone to divide all of his property equally among his progeny.  So, even though he had more children later, his Will was exactly what he wanted it to be, and he left the bulk of his property to his eldest son and would not have changed it later even if he had written a newer, updated, Will, naming everyone.  The idea of division of property more or less equally among one’s descendants was still a very “new” idea, based on a more democratic mindset, during that time period.  Still, the other children would have received equal divisions of the rest of the property.  Since Isham owned at least 1,777 acres of land at his death, that would have left 347 acres to be equally divided among the rest of the children.  They weren’t being entirely left out. 

Frankly, if the truth be told, the most likely answer to the question about children is that this couple had had more children after all, prior to Isham.  But, for whatever reason, they had not survived to 1779.  So, Isham C. Browder was their first surviving child, most likely.  And it is also likely that Isham C. Browder himself knew this, whether any of his siblings did or not.  Why?  Exactly because there had to have been some reason that his father would have written a Will in 1778, and then another one just a year later.

The 1778 Will, which is no longer extant, would have likely contained the names of the other children.  This may, in fact, explain everything.  It would explain why there would have even been a Will prior to that of 1779, and also explain why Isham failed to ever write another one.  Perhaps, having had to write a second Will, grieving over the, presumably, sudden deaths of his previous children, he found it impossible to bring himself to ever write another one.

It is also interesting to note that there can be no doubt that these intrepid pioneers first built, and resided in, a log cabin.  But, sadly, the mansion which Blanche Penland-Browder believed to have been theirs was not, since it was actually built during the 1840s-50s, according to local historian/genealogist, Bob Carter.  Still, they certainly would have built such a house themselves once they became established in the area. 

Finally, in 1827, “Teletha” Browder is listed as a member of Wolf Island Primitive Baptist Church.  This is the only known extant record of her there that I have been able to obtain. 

In any case, as mentioned earlier, Talitha also wrote her own Will (and she was the only Browder woman in our line, to my knowledge, to write a Will).  The following is Talitha’s Will, verbatim, from (RCWB B, pp. 26-28), which contains many misspellings, no doubt, done by the person who copied it into the record:

The Last Will and Testament of “Telitha” (sic) Browder

I Will and bequeath to my Daughter, Telitha (sic) C. Clarke the Sum of fifty cents she having Married against my Will and wishes. The above Isaiah Hancock was a militia soldier from Guilford County during the Revolutionary War and a member of Wolf Island Baptist Church.  He had a son, Isham Hancock (some dispute this), whom I strongly suspect was named after Capt. Isham Browder. 

Some of Talitha’s children obviously believed she had deliberately concealed Isham’s “authentic” Will (of 1779), which was apparently found after her death among his important papers, all of which Talitha had safely kept.  It is puzzling to me exactly why family members sued for inheritances since, whatever Isham’s Will of 1778 may have stipulated, it is clear that his Will of 1779, which would have superseded it, passed all of his land that he had at the time of the writing of said will (1,280 acres), except lands designated to be dealt with otherwise, to his first son, Isham C. Browder.  So, regardless of which Will was accepted, they should have gained nothing in either case except shares of the rest of the land.  But, as we will see, family members did expect more inheritance for some reason.  The situation is stated in the following manner in Our Thrasher Heritage, to wit:

It would appear that there was some question as to the validity of the [ISHAM’S] will and some of the children appeared to feel their mother had deliberately concealed the authentic will, or that there was negligence or misconduct on the part of the Clerk, in not making the proper entries on the records. At any rate, the controversy over the two wills began when the heirs to TALITHA COX BROWDER found the conflicting will among her valuable personal papers following her death, in 1818, about fifty years after the wills were signed (p. 103).

Of course, we know that Talitha Browder actually died in 1828, not in 1818, and that she actually concealed nothing.  And the fact of the matter is that Talitha’s daughter, Mary Thompson Browder-Thrasher, was deceased by the time Talitha wrote her Will, as were both of Mary’s children.  In addition, Nancy Washington Browder-Thrasher/Fears was also deceased, as was William Fears, Jr.  Also, all three of the Thrasher brothers in question were deceased by that time as well.  Frankly, all of the Thrasher children were deceased by this time too, having all died as children. 

Further, Talitha’s youngest son, Augustine Claiborne Browder, was also deceased by this time.  So, Talitha left practically everything she had to pass on to her named living grandchildren through her Will.  There is nothing odd or spiteful about that, even if she did give her daughter, Talitha, only fifty cents because she married against her wishes.  

It should also be noted that Talitha Browder left absolutely nothing to her first son, Isham C. Browder, or even to his children.  Perhaps she felt that the property he already had acquired through Isham’s Will was enough for him and his descendants.  And she surely expected that he would inherit the property left to him in his father’s Will, in any case, just as that Will had specified.  She had no reason to believe otherwise.

But Talitha also left absolutely nothing to Augustine C. Browder’s widow, and also nothing to any to his children.  It is obvious, then, that, for whatever reason, the descendants of Augustine C. Browder were completely left out of any inheritance at all.  No doubt, they may have felt terribly wronged by this.  But, there are actually two possible explanations for this that I can offer.  First, it may have had something to do with the bond Talitha took out for Elizabeth Booth-Browder several years prior to Talitha’s death.  No doubt, Talitha would have expected repayment for this.  Perhaps Elizabeth had never satisfactorily resolved this with Talitha.  Or, second (and most likely), Talitha may have felt that the land that she purchased from John Browder while Augustine was an infant (and which his family seems to have retained through all of the family machinations that took place, until Elizabeth sold it and moved away) was enough for them.  So, frankly, neither of the families of the two brothers really needed for Talitha to bequeath them anything more than what they already had.  And was she not free to do with her property what she wished, after all?

Talitha had already ensured during her lifetime, following or just prior to Isham’s death, that both Isham C. Browder and his brother, Augustine C. Browder, along with their descendants, would have property of their own regardless of the provisions of Isham’s Will.  It is obvious that, again, Augustine and his descendants received the 640 acre tract of August 10, 1784, and that property was later sold by Elizabeth Browder before she and her family moved on.  And, once all land transactions are taken into account, it becomes obvious that Isham C. Browder received from her the 100 acres that Isham Browder had received from Robert Garrott, about September 15, 1783, the 168 acres deeded to Isham Browder on November 8, 1784.  This was all her own doing, no doubt with Isham’s approval, ensuring that her two sons received property while they were still children.  

So, it becomes obvious that, regardless of what family members felt or said about what Talitha had done, she simply remained resolute in her decision to give what property she still had to pass on at her death to her daughter, Patsey Whittemore (who had steadfastly remained with her and had taken care of her as she grew older), and to her other daughter’s living children, with the exception of fifty cents to her daughter, Talitha Cox Thrasher/Clark.  She had already given to her two sons and, through them, their descendants.  Through her Will, she gave at least something to her living daughters, and her daughter’s living children – all of them.  Any other conclusion comes from a misreading of the data.

The known children of Thomas Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) and Talitha Cox (c1745-July 3, 1828) were Mary Thompson “Polly” (c1769-Apr. 23, 1793), Isham C. (c1774-1832), Talitha Cox (c1774-p. 1834), Nancy Washington (c1778-c1815), Augustine Claiborne (c1782-1816), and Martha “Patsey” (February 27, 1783-April 14, 1872).

Now, the following excerpts from a letter by Blanche Penland Browder, dated May 1, 1984, in response to my own enquiries as to their burial places, follow:

So far as we have been able to determine from cemetery records in the State Archives and from our visit to the land ISHAM BROWDER, SENIOR, and later in the possession of his WIFE and descendants on Wolf Island Creek, there is no stone marking the burial place of either ISHAM or TELITHA (sic), or ISHAM, JUNIOR and MARTHA. As you no doubt know it was the custom in early days for families to be buried on their own property. . . . The cemetery near the old house (which we saw years ago, which may not still be there) contained only Palmers. . . . The cemetery of a Baptist Church (built on land sold to the church in 1805 by ISHAM, JUNIOR) contains the grave of Augustine’s Daughter, Telitha (sic), born 1 February 1799 [incorrect] who married Nathaniel Roach.

The frank fact of the matter is that there is no extant evidence that any of our early Browders ever had monuments at all.  In old Virginia and North Carolina, their graves would likely have been marked by temporary wooden crosses which, obviously, decayed over time.  Usually, according to conventional practice, these wooden crosses were later replaced by more permanent headstones, but not always.  It is also true that no markers have been found for early generations of the Browder family in Tennessee descended from Isham and Talitha Browder, with the exception of a very few, most of which were placed at the gravesites by later generations.

It seems that, although these early generations of the Browder family could certainly have afforded to have had stones placed to mark their graves, they must have seen this as, somehow, an unnecessary extravagance.  And gravestones had to be shipped from some distance away in any case and were quite expensive. 

Again, most of the earliest Browder gravestones in Tennessee, at least, were mainly placed in their proper locations by later generations.  Thus, in North Carolina the family burial places would also have been marked with simple, unadorned crosses or rocks, and in Tennessee were at first marked in the same way, or with bricks, as is still evident in some locations. 

And it is obvious from the records that Capt. Isham Browder had no tombstone from which anyone could have obtained his exact birth and/or death dates anyway.  Otherwise, Isham C. Browder, and others, could have provided this information to the court, rather than guessing about his death date. Why the family Bible, which we know was in existence, might not have been consulted is yet another question that cannot be answered. 

So, the thing that makes the early Browders a bit peculiar here is that they could afford gravestones, but they did not buy, nor erect, any, as far as is known, while other families of wealth and social status often did.  This only adds even more to the mysteriousness of our early Browder ancestors, which is something well-remembered even by some members of today’s generations.  Frankly, they didn’t like for anyone to really know about their business.

In any event, to totally wrap things up here as best they can be, after I published my first book, “Faces of Our Ancestors” in 2014, I received a letter from the afore-mentioned local historian/genealogist, Bob Carter, of Rockingham County, whom I had contacted.  I don’t recall how this all went down except for the fact that I had sent a copy of my book to the Rockingham County Museum and Archives.  He responded with a kind, and informative, letter.  I have been unable to locate a copy of that letter in preparation for publishing this book.  But, I did find the blurb I posted about it on Facebook (dated October 21, 2014), which reads as follows:

OK. For those who are descended from, or interested in, Isham Browder and Talitha Cox of Guilford/Rockingham County, North Carolina, as I am, as of today I have been in contact with Robert “Bob” Carter, a local genealogist/historian of the county (wish I had been able to make contact before my book was completed) and he told me a few things. First, most of what I have in the book concerning that area and the people who lived there is correct. He actually said that it was amazing what I had come up with, given that I am not from that area. The only big thing that is incorrect (and this could not be helped, given the info that I had) is the idea that the big house/mansion that the Palmer family lived in had been built by the Browders. He says that it was not built til the 1850s (about 20 years after they all had moved away), but that years ago “some woman” had gone to the area and “pronounced” that it had been the old Browder home. Still, this home was in the very same area as the older Browder homes would have been in. He also said that he has been all over that area, and to every cemetery, and there are no headstones at all for Browders, so it is indeed possible that they are buried in Wolf Island Cemetery, as I show. He also said that their house had been located at a crossroads, one road leading north to Danville, Virginia. So, it was indeed the perfect place for them to be located and entertain travelers, etc. through that area. And the thing that was most interesting to me was that he had seen a document some years ago doing research which showed that one of the Browders had put in for a license to run a tavern. He couldn’t remember which one, though. And, obviously, he thinks that the tavern may have been called “Browder’s”. And he thought he remembered that Isham Browder had returned to Virginia to sell some inherited land or something. And he thinks it was in Dinwiddie County (but this could well be Isham, Jr.). He is to try to find whatever documents he can, and send them to me by mail (not email) – the old-fashioned way again.

Now, up until recently, I had not heard back from Mr. Carter since that time.  It is possible that he got sidetracked and forgot to get back with me further.  Still, the information he initially shared went a good-ways toward wrapping things up.  But, as the reader can see, I made contact with him again recently. 

Because of his initial information, and later conversations I have had with Mr. Carter, we now know that Isham and Talitha Browder resided in a place that was on a crossroads (at “Browder’s Crossroads”), with one of the roads, which was called Browder’s Road (roughly corresponding to today’s Wolf Island Rd.) leading northward to Danville, Virginia, and the other roughly corresponding to the location of today’s Narrow Gauge Rd.  And we know that their home was not the one that Blanche Penland-Browder thought it was (because that one was built by the Palmer family after they purchased the land in the 1830s).  The area where they lived would have been at the southern point of their vast land holdings. 

In addition, an undated, hand-written, letter, entitled, “My Rockingham County Ancestors: The Browders”, which can be found in The Whittemore Collection of the Gerald B. James Library, Rockingham Community College, Wentworth, NC, written by one of the children of James Minor and Pearl Whittemore (third great-grandson of Isham and Talitha Browder, as he/she writes in his letter), states, “In a family cemetery out 704 from Wentworth my 3rd great grandparents and 2nd great grandparents as well as my great grandfather William Whittemore are buried”. 

Now, it must be noted that three generations are mentioned here as having been buried in this family cemetery off of Hwy 704.  That is, except for one person, Martha McCollum-Whittemore.  She is, in fact, buried close to her parents in McCollum Cemetery, just south of Madison, North Carolina.  And, this cemetery is off of Hwy 704.  But, apparently, none of these other family members are buried in this cemetery, since there are no gravestones for them.  Of course, that does not mean that they are not buried there, it is just suggestive.  So, if they are all buried in that cemetery, Martha is the only one with a headstone. 

But, since the letter writer specifically named everyone except Martha, that suggests that the rest are buried elsewhere.  In fact, Hwy 704 does lead to Reidsville, changing over to Hwy 65, where Wolf Island Primitive Baptist Church and Cemetery are located.  And the church is not that far from 704/65.  The fact of the matter is that the writer could have named this cemetery if they had been buried there, since he/she seems to have been quite knowledgeable about his/her ancestry.  This cemetery is, in fact, no longer just a family cemetery, there being little doubt that it could have started as a Browder family cemetery (since Isham C. Browder sold it to the Baptist Society at Wolf Island in 1805).  After all, if Isham and Talitha did have children prior to Isham C. Browder, as I suggest, they were buried somewhere.  And, Capt. Isham Browder’s body also had to be buried somewhere. 

Regardless, the writer of this letter apparently knew, or had been told, where these family members were buried, which is something I don’t have certain knowledge of.  But I have been to the Wolf Island Baptist Church Cemetery, and there is plenty of room for several unknown persons to have been buried right at the front of the cemetery, in the very area where the oldest extant gravestones are located.  And it is known that there are over one-hundred unmarked graves in this area. Sadly, whatever may have been there was cleared away several decades ago, as I was told, because somebody decided to clean it up. 

Isham C. Browder, and his wife, Martha Browder, are, in my mind, undoubtedly buried wherever Isham and Talitha are buried.  And there is nothing at all to say that Talitha left the home she had resided in all those years any time prior to her death, and that home was near this cemetery.  So, it is virtually certain that she died at her home, with Patsey Browder-Whittemore attending her through her final days, as her Will indicates. 

So, it would seem that she would have been buried close to their home, either in the Palmer family cemetery, or at Wolf Island Church Cemetery.  I doubt that she was buried in the Palmer family cemetery because it is so small and it is surrounded by a rock wall.  Plus, the house that was mistaken for Isham and Talitha Browder’s home is not the same one that was close to the Palmer Cemetery.  And there is very little area where she could have been buried outside of that rock wall either, because of the surrounding landscape.  I have been there and seen it myself. 

In fact, I would venture to say that the letter writer did not actually know, but assumed, that Isham and Talitha Browder were buried with the Whittemores, and that the family cemetery that the Whittemores are known to have established, near Mosses Creek, which is also surrounded by a rock wall, is not the location of any Browder family burials, except for Martha, wife of Clement J. Whittemore, Jr.  Obviously, he would also have been buried there.

Over the course of several weeks, she had been an enormous help to me in adding to, and correcting, several things, not to mention copying and sending to me the material from the Whittemore Collection there.  Her assistance was invaluable. 

In addition, thanks to the help of Bob Carter, I have come to understand that there is another location where they could possibly have been buried.  There was an old family cemetery which used to be located very close to where Isham and Talitha Browder would have had their homeplace, on the north side of Wolf Island Creek.  It is, frankly, unknown who was buried there, as Mr. Carter states he had never visited that particular cemetery, and only learned of its destruction after the fact. 

He states that about fifteen years ago he was told about this family cemetery, located in the area where Wolf Creek Golf Course (the western part of it) had been constructed.  The person, a lady from Yorktown, Virginia, he was talking with informed Mr. Carter that the small family cemetery had been destroyed (by accident or not, no one knows) as the area was being cleared.  And it was located off of Old Hwy 87, which ran from Riedsville to Eden, right through the area where Isham and Talitha had property.  The informant really didn’t know much about it, and did not know what family may have been buried there.  But, Mr. Carter states that the golf course was constructed on land that was likely owned by these Browders.  So, it could have been a Browder family cemetery.  She did state, however, that there were several graves, but only sandstone rocks marking them. 

We talked several times about this, and he sent me documents and maps of the location of this cemetery, as well as other points of interest concerning the Browder family.  Having looked over these, it seems obvious to me that the letter writer, combined with information from Ms. Gomez and Mr. Carter, creates a picture, showing that they are all partially right – the Browders (and, perhaps, others) were buried close to 704/65 along Old 87 (part of which used to be Browder Road), but in Wolf Island Primitive Baptist Church Cemetery because it is the closest burial place to their home locations. The other family cemetery was on the north side of the creek and it is not known even how old that cemetery was. 

Thus, it seems obvious now that Capt. Isham Browder and Talitha Browder are buried in Wolf Island Church Cemetery.  Also, Isham C. and Martha Browder are also likely buried there, as also, by the way, would be Augustine C. Browder too.  I wouldn’t doubt that members of the Harris family might also be buried there.  Also, Martha “Patsey” Browder-Whittemore and Clement J. Whittemore, Jr. are both buried in the Whittemore family cemetery near Moses Creek.  However, William M. Whittemore is more likely buried with his wife in McCollum Cemetery, which is not far from Whittemore Cemetery anyway. 

Thus ends the story of this remarkable couple, but not the story of the Browder family.
