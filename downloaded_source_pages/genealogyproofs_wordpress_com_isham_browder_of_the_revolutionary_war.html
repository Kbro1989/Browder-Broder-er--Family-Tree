Isham Browder of the Revolutionary War and His Earliest Descendants
By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Jan. 7, 2025)

Isham Browder was born on June 12, 1762.  This is a known fact based on documentation, so his birth date cannot be disputed.  And he is said to have, perhaps, been born in Goochland County, Virginia, although Dinwiddie County is also theorized as his birthplace by some and is more likely.  However, more likely than this is Brunswick County, Virginia. This is important in that a connection with both Dinwiddie and Goochland Counties is postulated.  Certainly, as we will see, he did have ties to Goochland County later in his life, but it is more likely that he was born in Dinwiddie County.  His parents are also believed by some researchers to have resided in Goochland County, Virginia, but, frankly, no one really knows.  However, it is certain that he is a descendant of John (c1686-p. 1741) and Elizabeth (c1685-p. 1741) Browder.  Of this one thing we can have no doubt. 

I once believed that this Isham Browder could have been the son of Frederick Browder (Feb. 22, 1744-1815), son of John Browder II (1717-c1814) and Mary, and that would place his birth county as Dinwiddie.  But he is certainly NOT the son of any Isham Browder and Martha, as some incorrectly theorize. 

Now, we can be absolutely certain that this Isham Browder is not descended from Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739).  That leaves descent from one of the other sons of John and Elizabeth Browder.  Surely, if he were descended from Joseph Browder, he would have been mentioned in Joseph’s will.  He was not.  The same holds true for George, who had another son named Isham (b. c1766) anyway.  They were obviously not one and the same person.  Edmund and John are unlikely for reasons to detailed to bring out here.

William Browder, Sr. is now known to have had three daughters and at least one son, William, Jr.  Frankly, Isham (June 12, 1762-Feb. 19, 1830) could have been his son. 

This Isham Browder enlisted as a Private in the Continental Army, Second Virginia Regiment, on June 3, 1776 in Capt. William Taylor’s Company of Col. Spotswood’s 2nd Virginia Regiment, at the age of fourteen (DAR Index, p. 90).  He served in Capt. Buller Claiborne’s unit and fought under Gen. George Washington (February 22, 1732-December 14, 1799), along with Lighthorse Harry Lee.  Isham Browder was also among those soldiers at Valley Forge during the bitter winter of 1777/78, during which time the Continental Army was finally made into an effective fighting force through the efforts of Gen. George Washington and Baron von Steuben.

Isham Browder fought well during the war, and was wounded in the arm during the Battle of Monmouth Courthouse on June 28, 1778, after which he was eventually honorably discharged from service on August 30, 1779, at the age of seventeen, by General Woodford.  He also fought along with Harbert Claiborne, an officer in his unit (whom he named a son after). 

All of this information comes directly from his military service record, combined with the pension application made after Isham’s death by his widow, Elizabeth.  The pension application information also states his discharge date clearly, and makes absolutely no reference to any further service on Isham’s part, except for a reference to Cowpens and Guilford Courthouse (and these references are clearly incorrect because he was discharged before these battles took place). 

It is readily apparent from this brief sketch of Isham’s military career that he certainly did not rise to the rank of Captain, as he was discharged by the age of seventeen and remained a Private.  The reason this is mentioned will become apparent in a post about Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) of Guilford County, North Carolina, whom some people tend still to mix him up with. 

Sometime after the war ended, during the 1790s, Isham Browder traveled first to Hopkins County, Kentucky, where he had been awarded Virginia Revolutionary Bounty Land Warrant #4165 on June 3, 1786, as well as his military back pay, on March 7, 1786, both for his Revolutionary War service (Penland Browder, p. 77).  Isham Browder had also been awarded land grants in Christian, Muhlenberg and Henderson Counties, Kentucky for his military service. 

He first homesteaded in Hopkins County, on the Pond River, about 1794 (Penland Browder, p. 206) and was a large land owner there, eventually owning at one time at least 2,100 acres of land altogether (rivaling Isham Browder of Guilford County).  Isham is found in the (July 11) 1799 Henderson County, Kentucky tax roll in which he is listed as residing in the settlement called Elk’s (at Elk Creek) in Ashby’s Settlement.  Very few people resided in this area of Kentucky during that time period.

Isham Browder later went temporarily to Ohio, where he had acquired other land grants for his military service, and still later on to Hickman County, Kentucky, where he eventually died. 

Isham Browder completed his life as a Methodist lay minister and church trustee, and served as a Justice of the Peace until at least 1816.  He also served as Sheriff beginning in 1815, but was removed from this position several years later for failure to settle certain tax matters in 1815, and for failure to execute bond for 1816.  Still, he was later appointed to other positions of trust, such as surveyor of proposed roads and, by the Governor, as Inspector of Tobacco at Browder’s (his own) Warehouse (requiring a bond of 100 pounds).  His taxable property in 1817 was valued at $5,491. (Penland Browder, p. 206-07). 

Just knowing this much, it is little wonder that people want to claim descent from such an honorable person.  But this only scratches the surface of his story.  It is unknown exactly where Isham Browder resided between the time he was discharged from the army and the time he is found in Goochland County, Virginia, alter moving to Kentucky, but it seems likely that at least he was already in the tobacco business while still in Virginia, perhaps in concert with the Slayden/Slaton family of Goochland County.  Four of his children were born in Goochland County prior to 1795 (Penland Browder, p. 220), so we know that he resided there for a few years.  And there is a tradition that he was in the area of Norfolk, Virginia when British troops raided the city during the war, supposedly residing in the home of a sister-in-law. 

So, we see that he had ties to Goochland County by marriage, at least.  Isham Browder is said to have first married (1) Rachel Elizabeth Slayton in Lickinghole Township, St. James Parish, Goochland County, Virginia on April 23, 1786.  They had their first four children while there in Goochland County.  Isham later married (2) Elizabeth Searce on December 20, 1810 in Woodford County, Kentucky, following Rachel’s death.  They were married by the Rev. Thomas E. Waller, a Baptist minister.  However, it is entirely probable that this is also the Isham Browder who married Frances Daniel on December 2, 1782 in Pittsylvania County, Virginia.  If so, this union produced no living children, and Frances, apparently, died before 1786.  

Rachel Elizabeth Slayton was the daughter (third child) of Lt. John Slayden (February
22, 1730-1814/17, New Kent County, Virginia/a Revolutionary War patriot) and
Susanna Hodges (daughter of Edmond Hodges).  John Slayden was the son of Arthur Slayden (d. c June 18, 1787). 

Rachel was born in August of 1764 in Lickinghole Township, St. James Parish, Goochland County, Virginia.  She died on August 1, 1808 in Madisonville Township, Hopkins County, Kentucky, and is buried there in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery. 

Elizabeth Searce was born on May 5, 1775 in Virginia and she died on June 14, 1866 in Madisonville, Hopkins County, Kentucky, and is also buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery. 

If Isham Browder was residing in Goochland and/or Pittsylvania Counties, Virginia then it would have been easy for him to have known of and, perhaps, even personally known, Isham Browder (c1732-1784) and his children.  Thus, another possible family connection and, perhaps, another reason that so many of the children of both families had similar names, as can be shown. 

In any case, Isham Browder purchased 300 acres of military grants (plat number 3310) in the Ohio territory on Deer Creek, a branch of the Scioto River, in 1805 (Beadles, p. 99).  Nathaniel F. Randolph (c1797-1875, Not of the afore traced Randolph line, but of a FitzRandolph line, thus the middle initial “F”/son of Malachi FitzRandolph and Elizabeth Fallon), who traveled with him to locate the claim during the winter of 1805/06, had surveyed it earlier in 1798, at which time he also entered and registered the property in the Land Office (Penland Browder, p. 209). 

Upon Isham Browder’s return to Ohio in 1806, he found that the boundary markers which he and Nathaniel Randolph had previously set had been destroyed, and that the entire allotment of the land was under cultivation by Duncan McArthur (Beadles, 99).  The two had both previously traveled to the claim along with Duncan McArthur (who later became Governor of Ohio). 

According to Isham Browder, during the visit in 1806 Isham and Nathaniel called upon Duncan at his home since Duncan had made a claim for Isham’s land.  Duncan viewed Nathaniel’s patent and realized that he had made an erroneous claim and offered to buy Isham’s land.  However, Isham was unwilling to part with his property, and no new contract was entered into.  The two then argued and eventually “agreed” that a lawsuit would ensue. 

It seems obvious from Isham Browder’s statement that his issue here was that he wanted the profits from those who were renting his property; profits which he would not receive if Duncan McArthur bought him out.  However, even after all of this, Isham Browder did not initiate his lawsuit until 1812.  What accounts for this delay is unknown.  Perhaps he hoped that he and McArthur would come to some sort of agreement. 

Duncan McArthur had remained behind while Isham Browder and Nathaniel Randolph returned again to Virginia.  Sometime after their return, on March 23, 1814, Nathaniel Randolph died.  No doubt, Isham sent word to Duncan McArthur concerning the death of Nathaniel Randolph.  So, in effect, one of three witnesses to the claim and survey was then deceased.

The case was taken to the Court of Common Law and went as far as the Ohio Supreme Court where Isham Browder won!  Duncan McArthur appealed the verdict to the Court of Equity, eventually taking the case to the Supreme Court of the United States where Chief Justice John Marshall decided in his favor in 1819 (Duncan McArthur vs. Isham Browder, Appellate Case No. 922, Supreme Court, March 12, 1819).  The case came to a final end in 1823.  Blanche Penland Browder goes into great detail concerning these events in her book, so I only touch the basics here.  It is in this manner that Isham Browder lost property which was rightfully his, property which he had been granted due to his Revolutionary War service.  No doubt, judging from the amount of land that he managed to acquire elsewhere, Isham Browder was among the wealthiest Browders in history anyway.  And he seems to have actually acquired most or all of it because of his military service.  But, in the end he allowed himself to be placed into a situation in which he was unable to win because he refused to sell.  

It is interesting to note that in a letter that Isham wrote to his son, Josiah, Isham mentioned that if he were to win his lawsuit in Washington that he would be able to afford to purchase clothing and other necessities for Josiah, who was at that time in Greenbriar County, Kentucky (now West Virginia), studying for the Methodist ministry (Beadles, p. 99).  Of course, this was never to take place as Isham Browder ultimately lost the lawsuit.

One very interesting point in this controversy is Isham Browder’s initial agreeability.  McArthur’s appeal to the Supreme Court was to cost him a bond of $500.00.  However, since both parties agreed to it, said bond was waived.  Thus, Isham Browder helped make it possible for McArthur to appeal the verdict and to eventually defeat him by agreeing that bond be waived (Penland Browder, p. 213).  Otherwise, the bond might have been too much for McArthur and he might have ceased to pursue the matter altogether. 

However, it was also Isham’s disagreeability that caused the lawsuit to be initiated in the first place because, had he accepted McArthur’s offer to buy him out, he would likely have lost nothing.  He simply would not have received the profits he wanted.  The man was flexible on the one-hand, but intractable on the other-hand.  It seems to me that Isham’s main problem was that his integrity had been called into question by McArthur, something that Isham could not accept. 

This is, frankly, a typical Browder response.  It is true, as Duncan McArthur pointed out, that Nathaniel Randolph’s survey was flawed because it was tied to no fixed landmarks.  Therefore, McArthur had a good argument.  However, McArthur also questioned the integrity and honesty of Isham Browder claiming, among other things, that the land in dispute was not even the same as that surveyed by Nathaniel Randolph, and that Isham Browder was well aware of that.  McArthur also accused both Browder and Randolph of intentional fraud in this matter.  McArthur further insisted that he had contacted both Nathaniel Randolph and John O’Bannon concerning said entry, had received no response from O’Bannon, and that Randolph had claimed no knowledge of the survey, stating that he had acquired the survey and entry directly from O’Bannon.  So, in effect, McArthur stated that Randolph had claimed that he had not surveyed it in the first place. 

Isham Browder’s response to these things questions whether McArthur had attempted to contact either person at all, as there was apparently no proof of said contact (Penland Browder, p. 211-12).  New surveys were then ordered by the court.  The final verdict reads verbatim as follows (Penland Browder, p. 213). 

“In the first case, Duncan McArthur Vs. Isham Browder (Appellate Case Number 922), the Supreme Court, on March 12, 1819, found in favor of McArthur by reversing the decree of the Ohio Circuit Court, and remanding the case to the Ohio Circuit Court for reconsideration.  Upon reconsideration, the Circuit Court decided in favor of McArthur.  This case is reported in the published United States Report (4 Wheat, p. 488).  Browder appealed this decision to the Supreme Court in Browder V. McArthur (Appellate
Case Number 1044), and the Court on March 13, 1823, affirmed the Ohio Circuit Court’s Decision (7 Wheat, p. 58).   The Supreme Court ruled in favor of McArthur “strictly on the basis of a LAW WHICH REQUIRED THAT AN ENTRY SHOULD BE MADE WITH SUCH CERTAINTY, THAT SUBSEQUENT PURCHASES MAY BE ENABLED TO LOCATE THE ADJACENT RESIDUUM” (Penland Browder, p. 213). 

Does this mean that neither Nathaniel Randolph or Isham Browder had any fault?  Unfortunately not.  First, however the survey had actually been done, it was faulty.  But most importantly, in my view, it seems that once Isham Browder saw that his land was under cultivation by Duncan McArthur he chose to fight rather than to settle amicably.  Perhaps he had hoped for some sort of amicable settlement at some later point.  But, evidently, he expected this settlement to be on his own terms alone and not McArthur’s.  Once again, vividly displayed in this situation is the characteristic Browder stubbornness!  In effect, I believe (and Blanche Penland Browder stated this as well) that Isham Browder lost mainly due to his own notable stubbornness because he was unwilling to compromise.  And, because Isham Browder spent so much on this lawsuit, clearly expecting to win, he eventually lost much of his wealth. 

But, fortunately, the story does not end even here.  It seems that Isham Browder, upon returning to Kentucky and to his family there, lived a satisfying and fulfilling life regardless of these events.  Prior to his unfortunate encounter in Ohio, Isham had been one of the first Justices of the Peace in Hopkins County, Kentucky, having been appointed to the post by the Governor of Kentucky.  This appointment signified high standing in the community and a man of high moral principles (Penland Browder, p. 207). 

During this time period, these justices sat as a County Court, administering the county’s business.  Isham Browder also continued to function as a Methodist lay minister and trustee the balance of the rest of his life after returning from Ohio.  He did not let these events get the better of him and he appeared to hold no grudges in the end, even though he lost.  As a Methodist lay minister, he forgave those who had wronged him and moved on with his life.  In fact, he was still quite wealthy as by 1817 his taxable property was valued at $5,491.00 (ibid, p. 206).

Here follows an actual description of Isham Browder as he appeared during a meeting of the Justices of the Peace for the first Hopkins County Court in May of 1807 (from Pearce’s History): “A pious Methodist Lay Exhorter from Elk, in his tall beaver hat, that weighed two pounds” (Penland Browder  208).  

Isham Browder also allowed his land in Kentucky to be used for religious camp meetings.  Later he allowed the building of the first log structure known as Browder’s Chapel on his land, about seven miles east of present-day Madisonville at the center of Hartford Circuit.  This was one of the first churches to be built in Hickman County and was used for a short period of time as a regional meeting place for Methodist Quarterly Conferences where candidates for the ministry were often admitted to trial (Penland Browder, p. 208).  And Isham’s home was just across the old Greenville Road from this original structure.  The church had first been organized in 1803, Isham having been a part of this organization.  However, as early as 1821 this log structure was described as being out or repair and unsuitable for use. 

On August 23, 1821 Isham and Elizabeth Browder gave the two acres of land for Browder’s Methodist Church and cemetery to a duly appointed body of trustees, which included Isham Browder, Jr., at which time a new log building was erected on the site of the old log structure.  The deed specified that the cemetery was to be available to those of all denominations (Penland Browder, p. 209).  These old log structures have since been replaced by a newer building and the church building was further relocated at a later date to a hill close to the original site.  The church is presently located near Madisonville, Hopkins County, Kentucky. 

Isham Browder’s property in Madisonville was heavily mortgaged, no doubt as a result of the court battle.  He was also indebted to others for loans.  Because of these debts the Marshal was eventually ordered to put Isham Browder’s property up for sale.  This was done, at Isham’s request, on Christmas day, December 25, 1826, no doubt to make those who gave the order to feel guilty for having done it.  Isham Browder, Jr. was the final and highest bidder having bid $750.06.  He paid cash for the property.  Isham Browder, Jr. was, then, entrusted with clearing the property of debt (ibid, p. 215/Beadles, p. 99). 

In 1826/27 Isham Browder, Sr. moved to Hickman County (later Fulton County), Kentucky where his sons David and Augustine already resided.  Isham Sr.’s land holdings totaled 788 acres at that time.  Isham Browder, Sr. and other family members probably moved to this location by water, traveling on a flatboat as they would have been familiar with this mode of travel (Penland Browder, p. 216). 

Isham Browder, Sr. died on February 19, 1830 (before the census for that year was taken) and is buried on a farm near Fulton, Kentucky.  This location was originally in the front yard of the home of one of Isham’s sons.  His coffin was made from a seven-foot long hollowed out oak log bound together by bark strips.  His gravesite, marked with a bronze marker and near his old homestead, has been a Kentucky historical landmark since October 26, 1966. 

The inventory of Isham Browder, Sr.’s estate was taken on March 13, 1830.  This inventory still included a considerable amount of property which, interestingly, included seven volumes of Ancient History by Charles Rollin (Fifth Edition, London, 1768), two volumes of the Laws of Kentucky, and one medical book (ibid, p. 217). 

Following Isham’s death Elizabeth filed a pension application concerning Isham’s military service.  Beginning in 1853 she received a yearly pension payment of $80.00 and in 1856 she received an additional 160 acres of land.

This family moved to Dyer County, Tennessee.  The 1840 census of Dyer County, Tennessee lists Josiah Browder (40-49), along with his wife (20-29), and children; one male (10-14), one male (5-9), and two females (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

The 1850 census of Dyer County (District 3) lists Josiah Browder (51), along with his wife, Rachel (36), and children; H. H. (21), H. C. (19), A. S. R. (14), R. A. (12), J. I. (8), S. E. (5), Thos. W. (3), and D. A. (0).  This census states that Josiah was born in Virginia.  Josiah Browder is buried near Dyersburg, Tennessee.

The known children of Josiah and Lucinda Browder were Henry H. (b. c1829), and Harbert C. (b. c1831). 

The known children of Josiah and Rachel Browder were A. S. R. (b. c1835), Rachel Annie (c1838-Aug 21, 1877), Josiah, Jr. (c1843-1865 as a prisoner of war during the American Civil War), Sarah “Sallie” E. (Mar. 2, 1845-Nov. 5, 1907), Thomas W. (Nov. 15, 1845-Mar. 24, 1886), Robert O. (Jan. 13, 1847-Oct. 2, 1847), Augustine D. (Aug. 28, 1848-Oct. 17, 1849), David Austin (June 8, 1850-Feb. 21, 1911), and John Isham (Sept. 22, 1853-Jan. 25, 1938).

Pvt. Pleasant Browder, Sr. (Feb. 16, 1794-c1831), son of Isham and Rachel Browder, married Lucy Jane Monroe (1800-1873), niece of President Monroe. Their known children were Capt. Edward Cabell (May 30, 1825-Mar. 24, 1878) and Pvt. Isham Bell (June 19, 1826-Oct. 7, 1863).

Martha Browder (September 14, 1795-August 13, 1877), daughter of Isham and Rachel Browder, married Joseph Gill on October 24, 1816 or November 13, 1823, in Hopkins County, Kentucky.  Joseph was born October 31, 1787 and died in March of 1855.  Joseph was postmaster in Ray County, Missouri where the family resided (Penland Browder, 214).  This union is supposed to have produced seven children.  Two of their known children were Mary Browder Gill and Ira Gill (Nov. 28, 1835-May 28, 1927).

Mary Browder (b. 1797), daughter of Isham and Rachel Browder, married Thomas Atterbury on October 13, 1823 in Hopkins County, Kentucky.  Thomas was a Methodist preacher.  Mary eventually moved to California later in life.

Thomas and Mary Atterbury had two known children, Kinchlow and Emily M. (Dec. 6, 1827-Mar. 30, 1875).  Emily married Jonathan Taylor Turpin (Apr. 7, 1823-Mar. 14, 1903) of Missouri.

Isham Browder, Jr. (May 3, 1799-June 8, 1864), son of Isham and Rachel Browder, married Lucretia “Lizzie” Langley (Mar. 2, 1799-Mar. 11, 1868) on December 21, 1820 in Muhlenberg or Hopkins County, Kentucky.  Lucretia was born in Madisonville Township, Hopkins County, Kentucky and is buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery beside her husband, Isham Browder, Jr.  Isham Browder, Jr. also died at Madisonville Township, Hopkins County, Kentucky.

The 1830 census of Hopkins County, Kentucky lists Isham Browder [Jr.] (30-39), along with his wife (20-29), and children; two males (5-9), two males (under 5), two females (15-19), and one female (5-9).  This family owned one female slave (10-23) that year.

The 1840 census of Hopkins County lists Isham Browder [Jr.] (40-49), along with his wife (40-49), and children; one male (15-19), three males (10-14), one male (5-9), one male (under 5), one female (20-29), one female (15-19), and one female (5-9).  Also listed is one female slave.

The 1850 census of Hopkins County (District 1) lists Isham Browder (51), farmer, along with his wife, Lucretia (51), and children; Jno. W. (26), Tho. C. (23), Wm. F. (21), Isham (19), Ann J. (17), Jas. E. (15), Lafayette W. (12), and Hanson K. (16) as well as John H. Key (17), Ann M. Key (12), Chas. D. Key (12), and B. B. Key (10).  This census shows that Isham was born in Kentucky.

The 1860 census of Hopkins County lists Isham Browder (61), farmer, along with his wife, Lucretia L. (61) and son; Harrison K. (20) all within the household of Jas. L. Oldham (26), farmer, which included his wife, Mary E. (23) and their children; Martha J. (3) and Joel D. (1).  Also listed is Anna L. Gill (1).  All household members are listed as having been born in Kentucky.

The known children of Isham Browder, Jr. and Lucretia Langley were Elizabeth W. (Sept. 27, 1821-Feb. 2, 1881), John W. (Dec. 13, 1824-Sept. 24, 1888), Thomas C. (Claiborne?) (b. c1827), William F. (Jan. 6, 1829-Oct. 18, 1834), Isham III (Feb. 16, 1831-Apr. 14, 1854), Ann J. (b. c1833), James E. (c1835-Nov. 25, 1913), Lafayette “Fayette” McKendree (Nov. 22, 1838-Jan. 31, 1851), and Harrison Key (October 29, 1841-April 25, 1911).

           

James Browder (May 9, 1802-1874), son of Isham and Rachel Browder, married (1) Harriett Smith (c1803-1874, California) on June 22, 1822 in Adair County, Kentucky, and later, (2) Minerva Turpin on May 15, 1832 in Russell, Kentucky.  Minerva Turpin was born on January 20, 1814 and died on November 14, 1879 in California.

The 1860 census of Branch, Stanislaus County, California lists James Browder (57), farmer, along with his wife, Minerva (46), and children; Victoria (13), G. B. (m. 24), farmer, Josephine (11), and Nancy (3).  This census states that James, Minerva and G. B. were all born in Kentucky.  And it states that Victoria and Josephine were born in Missouri.

The 1870 census of Branch, Stanislaus County, California lists Minerva Browder (56), along with daughter, Nancy M. (13).  Also listed within this household were Robert Warder (40), John McCondy (30), William H. Walker (32), and Wilson Harris (36).  This census states that Minerva was born in Kentucky. 

The known children of James and Harriett Browder were William (Jul. 11, 1826-Feb. 7, 1871) and James Harvey (Dec. 14, 1827-Mar. 22, 1852).

The known children of James and Minerva Browder were Roseline (c1831-p. 1860), Green Burgoyne (c1836-Dec. 20, 1907), Marianna “Mary” (b. c1839), Rachel Elizabeth (b. c1840), Clarissa “Clarina” Ann (c1841-Oct. 27, 1877), Cornelia Ann (Jul. 10, 1843-Mar. 20, 1870), Victoria (c1847-Feb. 19, 1934), Minerva Josephine (Mar. 13, 1849-Aug. 10, 1943), and Nancy Miranda (Sept. 13, 1856-Nov. 6, 1954).

Augustine Browder (Nov. 18, 1803-Dec. 31, 1873), son of Isham and Rachel Browder, was born in Hopkins County, Kentucky and died in Fulton County, Kentucky.  Augustine, along with the David Browder family, moved to Hickman County (now Fulton County), Kentucky in 1824, soon after the land was opened up for settlement.  This made them pioneer settlers of the area.

Augustine returned to Hopkins County in March of 1826 to marry Arena Avery Jackson (Feb. 11, 1813-Nov. 23, 1887), daughter of Beckley Jackson and Martha Brown, on June 16, 1831, after which he returned with her to Hickman County where they, as many others also did, resided in a log cabin which he had built for them during his initial visit. 

The 1840 census of Hopkins County, Kentucky lists Augustin Browder (30-39), along with his wife (20-29), and children; two males (5-9), three males (under 5), and one female (5-9).  Also listed is one male slave and two female slaves.

The 1850 census of Fulton County, Kentucky lists Augustin Browder (45), farmer, along with his wife, Arena (37), and children; Thomas (18), Martha (16), James (15), John (13), Isham (12), William (10), Andrew (8), Lewis (3), and Pleasant (2).  All family members are listed as having been born in Kentucky except for Arena, who is listed as having been born in Virginia.

The 1860 census of Fulton County, Kentucky lists Austin Browder (56), farmer, along with his wife, Aruna (47), and children; Isham (21), Wm. (19), Andrw J. (17), Lewis A. (15), Pleasant (12), and George L. (10).  Also listed is Martha Rennolds (17).  All household members are shown as having been born in Kentucky except for “Aruna”, who was born in Virginia.

The 1870 census of Fulton County, Kentucky (District 1) lists A. Browder (66), farmer, along with his wife, Arrena (56), and children; Louis A. (24), Pleasant (21), and George L. (18).  This census states that “A.” was born in Kentucky, as were all other family members except for “Arrena”, who is shown as having been born in Virginia.

The 1880 census of Lodgetown, Fulton County, Kentucky lists Arena Browder (66), along with her son, Pleasant (31), farmer.  This census states that Arena and both of her parents were all born in Virginia.  And it states that Pleasant and his father were both born in Kentucky, while his mother was born in Virginia.

Arena and Augustine both died in Fulton County, Kentucky.  They are both buried in Palestine Methodist Church Cemetery, Fulton County, Kentucky, beside one another.

The known children of Augustine and Arena Browder were Thomas M. (Mar. 28, 1832-Mar. 13, 1854), Martha Ann (Nov. 13, 1833-Dec. 13, 1854), James M. (May 27, 1835-Sept. 8, 1906), John Hunter (Nov. 27, 1837-Nov. 14, 1891), Cpl. Isham (September 22, 1838-September 12, 1907), Pvt. William (c1840-Nov. 30, 1864), Mary Jane (Feb. 2, 1842-Oct. 1, 1842), Andrew Jackson (c1843-April 10, 1862), Lewis T. (May 19, 1846-Jan. 9, 1920), Pleasant H. (Aug. 6, 1848-Oct. 3, 1924), and George Lafayette (June 27, 1851-May 30, 1923).

David Browder (June 6, 1806-December 21, 1894), son of Isham and Rachel Browder, married (1) Mary/Margaret “Polly” Smith, daughter of Elias Smith and Margaret Vaughn of Hopkins County, Kentucky, on September 1 (or November 15), 1827 in Hopkins County, Kentucky.  Mary was born on December 13, 1806, and died on June 25, 1883 in Palestine, Fulton County, Kentucky.  David also died in Palestine, Fulton County, Kentucky and is buried there. David later married Bettie Donoho (Jul. 5, 1846-Apr. 18, 1892).

The 1850 census of Fulton County, Kentucky lists David Browder (42), farmer, along with his wife, Mary (42), and children; James (21), Mary (19), Margaret (18), Albert (17), Thomas (15), Robert (10), Edward (7), Rachael (4), and Catherine (2).  This census shows that all family members were born in Kentucky.  This family is listed between the Jackson Bynum (31) and William T. Hicks (41) families.

The 1860 census of Fulton County, Kentucky lists David Browder (56), farmer, along with his wife, Mary (53), and children; Robt. A. (20), Edwd. E. (16), Rachel A. (15), and Hannah C. (12).  This census shows that all family members were born in Kentucky.

The 1870 census of Fulton County, Kentucky (District 5) lists David Browder (64), farmer, along with his wife, Mary (63), and boarders; J. P. Wade (white/19) and Sarah Freeman (mulotto/16).  All household members are listed as having been born in Kentucky.

Listed directly underneath this family is that of R[obert]. A. Browder (30), farmer, which included his wife, M. P. Hays (32) and children; Anna M. (4), Thos. M. (2), and John (5 months).

Also listed on the same page is the family of J[ames] A. Browder (41), farmer, which included his wife, Caroline E. (30), and children; James L. (19), Mary C. (15), Albert L. (13), Virginia A. (11), John B. (9), Margaret S. (6), Benjamine (5), Mollie V. (3), and Lula (6 months).  This census shows that all these family members were born in Kentucky.

The 1880 census of Fulton County, Kentucky lists D. Browder (74), farmer, along with his wife, Mary (73), and one granddaughter, Marietta (11).  Also listed within this household were Laura Hammond (white,16), cook; John Elbert (black, 28), servant; C. C. Martin (white, 27), and I. W. Deyer (white, 23).

The known children of David and Mary Browder were James Alpheus (Aug. 6, 1828-Oct. 5, 1871), Ellen Mary (b. c1830), Elizabeth Margaret (Aug. 18, 1831-Jan. 20, 1852), Albert David (June 29, 1833-Sept. 6, 1854), Thomas Melvin (May 9, 1835-Sept. 14, 1900), Arena J. (b. Apr. 8, 1837), John W. (Aug. 11, 1838-Nov. 12, 1839), Col. Robert Augustine (August 10, 1840-August 23, 1918), Edward Elijah (Nov. 8, 1842-1917), James (b. c1845; died as an infant), Rachel Ann (Feb. 26, 1845-Sept. 25, 1943), Sarah Katherine (Mar. 1, 1846-Feb. 4, 1903), and Hannah C. (b. 1850).

Finally, let it be completely understood that this Pvt. Isham Browder is by no means a son of any Isham and Martha Browder, because no Isham Browder, who married a woman named Martha, could possibly be his father, period.  Process of elimination proves that. Also, this Isham Browder is NOT descended from Isham and Talitha Browder.  This sort of misinformation is still rampant all over the internet, and it needs to be cleared up.
