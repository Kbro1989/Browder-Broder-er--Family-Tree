The Earliest Isham Browders and Other Related Persons:

By. John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div.

The plain fact of the matter is that the Isham Browder (c1732-c1784), who married Talitha Cox (c1745-July 3, 1828), was the very first person in the Browder family to be given the name Isham.  This Isham Browder was a colonial planter and a politician.  He was very prominent in society, very wealthy, and even had a mansion built in which he and his family dwelt.  How could this be if he were the son of a plantation bailiff who was by no means wealthy?  And, how could this be if he were brought up by his uncle, Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, within a not so very prominent family household?  How did he acquire his wealth and prominence, both of which were decidedly more than any other Browder had during that time period? 

            Even though I have failed to locate any actual record, to date, which would prove my contention, it is, again, my belief that this Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) inherited property and wealth through the very wealthy and socially prominent Randolph family, but perhaps not directly.  One has to understand that no member of the Browder family of that time period possessed that kind of wealth other than this Isham Browder.  And we know where Jones Browder got his wealth, which was fairly considerable and came close to rivaling it.  So, from where else could Isham have obtained this wealth if not from some inheritance? 

            In addition, one must remember that Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739) did have at least one infant heir.  Arthur did possess property to be passed on.  In whatever way it was actually carried out, his property must have been passed down to this Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) by inheritance, perhaps by way of the Randolph family, who might have held it in trust.  And for this to have taken place, there must have been some familial relationship between them.  And the Browder and Randolph families of Old Virginia did often perpetuate at least a friendly relationship, evidently even after the death of Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739). 

            In any case, it is here that I will emphasize that the preponderance of the evidence (which is still extant) proves beyond a reasonable doubt that Isham Browder (c1732-c1784) was indeed the son of Arthur Browder and Mary Isham Randolph, and that, in fact, his name was actually Thomas Isham Browder, he, being named, in part, after his grandfather, Thomas Randolph.  I will present evidence for his name at a later point. 

            Still, the question remains as to exactly why so many Browders during this time period were given the name Isham.  They could not have all been directly descended from Mary Isham Randolph!  Indeed, as we shall see, they were not.  But they were all closely related, and all descend from John (c1690-p. 1760) and Elizabeth (1686/87-p. 1750) Browder, for sure. 

            The very first reason why any Browder would have been given the name “Isham” would certainly be due to descent from the Isham, or a related, family.  But that would not have to apply to all Browders named Isham.  Children were often named after family friends.  Direct descent would apply only to the prominent planter and politician, and his descendants, unless others were also descended somehow from the Isham or a related family. 

As for the rest, the primary reason could well have been a means by which the Browder family could collectively recall some major event in their own family history without actually talking about it.  In fact, this could have been a concerted effort by family members on their own to help protect the identity of the child of Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739) and Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772).  They may well have feared further violence or retribution.  They may well have believed, unfounded as that belief may have been, that the anger of the Randolph family would not subside, and that the child might still be in danger even into young adulthood.  But with so many Browder children suddenly possessing the given name Isham, it would have been difficult for anyone to focus on any particular Isham Browder.  However, once the child grew to adulthood the danger would have been essentially over and the naming of Browder children with the given name Isham would simply have become traditional.  Thus, it continued to be common for almost 100 years. 

            Of course, this is all speculation on my part because no one knows the answer.  But it is a question that researchers have attempted to solve, obviously with little to no success.  It is just plain unusual for a given name to crop up in a family line like this and be perpetuated so prolifically.  For it to be perpetuated on one line – the line of direct descent – is not unusual.  But, for it to be perpetuated on several related lines, with no known direct link to the origin of the name is very unusual.  But, I submit, that is exactly what happened because there is no good reason to posit that either John Browder (c1690-p. 1760) or his father, Edmund, married anyone from the Isham family, although some have tried to posit exactly this, with no evidence to support their contention.  I have to state that at least I have presented evidence to support my contention, even if it cannot be classed as absolute proof. 

            In any case, it is time to move on to a delineation of all of the known earliest persons named Isham Browder.  Among the other known early Isham Browders are the following: 

Isham Browder, Sr. (c1766, Dinwiddie County, Virginia-1852), who was the brother of George, Jr. (b. c1771) and Uriah “Urias” Browder, and who married Susanna (d. after 1852), and who also had a son named Isham (d. bef. 1852), is the first to be mentioned here.  Isham, Sr., George, and Uriah were the sons of George Browder, Sr. (son of John and Elizabeth Browder), who gave aid to the American Revolution on April 23, 1782 (Brunswick County Persons, p. ?).  This Isham Browder, Sr. resided in Dinwiddie County, Virginia all of his life and owned lands (252 acres) on Gravelly Run (at old Petersburg) close to the lands of Jones Browder, which were also located on Gravelly Run. 

            The land tax records of Dinwiddie County, Virginia in the early 1800’s show that Richard Browder, Isham Browder, Sr., Isham Browder, Jr., Jeremiah Browder, Jones Browder, and Littleberry Browder, as well as other family members, resided within just a few miles of one another. 

            The three brothers, Isham, Sr., George, and Uriah Browder, are also all mentioned in the parish register of the old Blanford Church, Bristol Parish, Church of England.  So, these brothers also continued to reside in the same area, and attend church in the very same building, that the Browder family had worshiped in during the previous generations.  However, the three brothers together converted to the Methodist faith once that denomination was organized by John Wesley.  They likely converted as early as 1776, because it is known that,

“the first important acquisitions to the Methodist [Societies] ranks came following a quarterly meeting in May, 1776, at Boisseau’s Chapel, in Dinwiddie County. . . .  [S]everal hundred were converted, and hardly anything was talked of but the wonderful work of God.  The counties of Dinwiddie, Amelia, Brunswick, Sussex, Prince George, Lunenburg and Mecklenburg, all shared in the revival” (The Old Free State, pp. 388-89/Memorials of Methodism, p. 88).

            The home and farm where these three brothers were brought up was located about 23 miles south of Richmond, Virginia.  This was close to the place called Browder Crossing (where Browder’s Bridge was located) and was also located about ten miles west of present day Petersburg, Virginia.

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder, Sr. (45 and over), along with his wife (45 and over), and slaves; four males and two females.  He and his family are listed directly above the family of Jones Browder, showing that they were close neighbors.

            The 1830 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder [Sr.] (60-69), along with his wife (50-59), and slaves; four males and eight females.

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County lists Isham Browder, Snr. (70-79), along with his wife (60-69).  Also listed within this household is one free colored male (24-35), as well as seven male slaves and twelve female slaves. 

            And the 1850 census of the Northern District of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder, Sr. (83), farmer, along with his wife, Susanna (75).  Interestingly, also residing within this household were Sam Jones (40), and Ben Jones (41).  All household members are listed as having been born in Virginia.  The value of Isham’s real estate that year was $1,740.

            He is most probably the Isham Browder who paid taxes in Dinwiddie County in 1800 (the 1800 Dinwiddie County, Virginia, Tax List).  He and Isham Jr. are both listed in the 1820 Dinwiddie County Tax List, with Isham Sr. having three slaves, and Isham Jr. having five slaves.

            In his will, Isham Browder, Sr. mentions his brothers, George and Urias, and his father, George (DCWB. 5, 1852, p. 176).  In the same county, by the way, other Browders also left wills including Susanna (1818), Caleb P. (1826), and Uriah “Urias” (1849). 

            Isham Browder, Sr., of Dinwiddie County left all of his property to the children of George Browder, Jr., upon his death in 1852 (Penland Browder, p. 40).  Obviously Isham, Jr. (as he was called), son of George, Jr. and Elizabeth, was named after this Isham, Sr.  Isham, Jr. also administered the estate of his uncle, Isham, Sr. 

It also becomes obvious that Isham, Jr., son of Isham, Sr., was deceased by 1852 (and had no descendants), and this is why Isham, Sr. left his property to the children of George Browder, Jr. 

            The will of Isham Browder, Sr. contains the following stipulations referring, in part, to the children of Isham Browder, Jr:

Legatees– Wife Susannah; Isham Jr. son of George; Nancy Stell (formerly Browder), daughter of George, deceased; Edna E. E. Browder, dau. of Isham Jr; Martha A. M. Browder, dau. of Isham Jr.; Uriah Browder “my only [living] brother”, (of Brunswick County, Virginia); Charles L. Browder, son of George, deceased; Mary P. Chandler, dau of George; balance of estate to George Browder’s children, Uriah’s children, Nancy Young’s children and Edna E. E. and Mary (Martha) A. M. Browder.  Dated December 8, 1852.

Executor: Isham Browder, Jr., son of George.

            George Browder (c1771-c1820) may be the George Browder who married Martha Hawks in July of 1813 in Amelia County, Virginia, although he is also known to have married Elizabeth.  George Browder, Jr. (b. c1771), brother of the afore mentioned Isham, Sr. and Uriah, along with his wife, Elizabeth (b. c1775), are known to have resided with the family of David Browder (son of Richard and Mary Browder) in Dinwiddie County from 1787 to 1794.

            George Browder patented 299 acres of land in Dinwiddie County, Virginia on the south side of Hatcher’s Run on December 15, 1758.  John Roberts purchased a neighboring tract of land to this George Browder in 1769.  He also acquired 105 acres of land, some of which was in Brunswick County, probably through inheritance, which he paid taxes on even while residing with the David Browder family (Penland Browder, p. 40).

            The children of George, Jr. and Elizabeth Browder were Isham, Jr. (b. c1799), Charles L. (b. c1801), Nancy (m. Robert? Stell), Mary P. (Chandler), and Elizabeth (m. George Landifer).  George Browder died about 1820. 

            The 1820 census of St. Andrew’s Parish, Brunswick County, Virginia lists Mrs. Elisa Browder (45 and over), along with three females [presumably, her daughters] (16-25), as well as eight male slaves and eight female slaves.  On the same page is listed Uriah Browder. 

            Isham Browder, Jr., son of George, Jr. and Elizabeth Browder, had at least two daughters, Edna E. E. and Martha A. M.  Isham, Jr. inherited 62 acres of land in Dinwiddie County on August 26, 1821, after the death of his father, George, Jr. 

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder Junior (16-25), as well as another male (16-25), and two females (10-15).  Who all of these persons were, is unknown but, presumably, one of the females may have been Isham’s wife.  Also listed are four male slaves and one female slave. 

            The 1830 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder [Jr.] (30-39), along with two females (30-39), and two females (under 5).  Also listed are five male slaves and ten female slaves. 

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder, Jr. (40-49), along with another male (25-30), and two females (10-15).  Obviously, his wife had died by this time. 

            Interestingly, it seems that another Isham Browder (40-49) is also listed in the 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, along with two females (10-14), and five male slaves, as well as one female slave. 

            The 1850 census of the Northern District of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Isham Browder, Jr. (51), brick layer, as a single head of household.  That year the value of his real estate was $1,200.  This census shows that Isham was born in Virginia.

            In 1830 this family is listed directly above Charles L. Browder (30-39) and his presumed wife (20-29).  Also listed on the same page is Robert Stell (20-29), along with his wife (15-19), and one other female (50-59).

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia also, again, lists Chas. L. Browder (30-39), along with his presumed wife (20-29), and presumed children; one male (under 5), two females (5-9), and one female (under 5).  Also listed are two male slaves and one female slave. 

            The 1850 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia (Northern District) once again lists Charles L. Browder (49), along with his wife, Sally A. (39), and children; Roberta F. (15), Henrietta S. (13), John A. (2), Elvira (9), and Laura A. R. (7), as well as a boarder, Joseph Lock (12).  This census confirms that Charles was born in Virginia.

            Uriah “Urias” Browder, Sr. brother of George and Isham, Sr., married Mary Quarles (daughter of John Quarles?) on May 9, 1793 in Brunswick County, Virginia.  They resided in Brunswick County, Virginia from 1795 to 1848/49.

            The 1810 census of St. Andrews Parish, Brunswick County, Virginia lists Urias Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (26-44), and presumed children; one male (10-15), four males (under 10), and one female (under 10).  It also lists eleven slaves.

            The 1820 census of St. Andrews Parish, Brunswick County, Virginia lists Uriah Browder (45 and over), along with his presumed wife (45 and over), and presumed children; two males (16-25), one male (16-18), two males (10-15), one female (16-25), and two females (under 10).  Also listed are three male slaves and five female slaves.

            The 1830 census of St. Andrews Parish, Brunswick County, Virginia also lists Urias Browder (60-69), and presumed children; one male (20-29), one male (under 5), one female (30-39), two females (10-14), one female (5-9), and two females (under 5).  Also listed are five male slaves and eight female slaves.

            The 1840 census of Brunswick County, Virginia lists Urias Browder (60-69), along with two females (20-29), and one male (10-14).  Also listed are five male slaves and seven female slaves.

            The will of Uriah Browder is dated January 10, 1849, and is recorded in Brunswick County, Virginia records (BCWB, Vol. 15, 1849, p. 232). 

            The children of Uriah Browder and Mary Quarles were John (b. c1801), Catherine (m. Hartwell Ogburn, January 25, 1825), Isham (b. c1805), Uriah, Jr. (b. c1812), Ann R. (Nolley), Susanna, Mary Jane (m. Lewis Barrow, June 24, 1839), and George E. (b. c1823/m. Judy R. Hawthorne) (Penland-Browder, p. 71). 

            The 1810 census of St. Andrews Parish, Brunswick County, Virginia lists John Browder [son of Uriah Browder and Mary Quarles] (16-25) as a single head of household residing alone.

            John Browder, son of Uriah Browder and Mary Quarles, is probably the one who married Angelia F. Dameron on March 8, 1834 (Penland Browder, p. 71).

            The 1840 census of Brunswick County, Virginia lists John Browder (30-39), along with his wife (30-39), and one daughter (under 5).  Also listed are four male slaves and three female slaves.

            It is of interest to note that on June 28, 1762 John Quarles, Nathaniel Tatum, Jr. and Hugh Williams witnessed a deed between Robert Sayer and his wife, Mary on the one part and James Howeton of Culpepper County, Virginia of the other part (BCDB 7, p. 134).  

            Uriah Browder, Jr. married Susan “Sally” Elmore on April 10, 1817 in Brunswick County, Virginia. 

            The 1840 census of Lunenburg County, Virginia lists Urias Browder [Jr.] (30-39), along with his wife (20-29), and two female slaves.

            The 1850 census of the Northern Division of Brunswick County, Virginia lists Uriah Browder [Jr.] (38), along with his wife, Susan (30), and children; Mary (8), Sarah (7), John (6), William (5), and Harriet (2).  This census shows that Uriah was born in Virginia.

            Isham Browder (b. c1805), son of Uriah Browder and Mary Quarles, married Martha W. Burnett on December 8, 1836 in Lunenburg County, Virginia. 

            The 1840 census of Brunswick County, Virginia lists Isham Browder (30-39), along with his wife (20-29,) and children, one male (10-14), one male (under 5), and one female (under 5).  Also listed is one female slave.  This family is listed directly under the family of John Browder. 

            The 1850 census of the Northern Division of Brunswick County, Virginia lists Isham Browder (45), planter, along with his wife, Martha (35), and children, Allen (12), Lucy (11), Philip (10), Lois (9), George (8), Martha (7), Thomas (6), Miles (5), Marcus (4), and Marietta (3).  The value of Isham’s real estate that year was $94,500.  All family members are listed as having been born in Virginia.

            On April 15, 1851 (BCDB 35, pp 390-91), Freeman J. Gunn sold Thomas Lambert 200 acres of land, known as the Jordan Tract, which was partly in Lunenburg County and partly in Brunswick County.  This land is said to have been bounded by the lands of Robert B. Jones, Samuel Mattox, and Isham Browder.  This is also within the same area of residence as previous Browder generations. 

            Another Isham Browder married Frances Daniel on December 2, 1782 in Pittsylvania County, Virginia.  I actually believe that this Isham Browder is one and the same as the Isham Browder (June 12, 1762-Feb. 19, 1830) of the Revolutionary War, and that he first married this Frances Daniel, but that there were no living children of this union, and Frances soon died.

            William Browder, son of Richard and Mary Browder, married and had children; Jeremiah (b. c1774), Isham (b. c1775), Thomas (b. c1776), William (b. c1777), and James (b. c1780).  This is probably the William Browder whose wife was named Elizabeth.  This Elizabeth relinquished her dower rights in a certain tract of land about 1790 (Penland Browder, p. 189/DCOB 1, p. 27).

            The brothers, Jeremiah, Isham, and Thomas, all resided in Mecklenburg and Lunenburg Counties, Virginia prior to moving to Iredell County, North Carolina.

            This is the sum total of what is known about the known Isham Browders of this period, with the exception of those who were descended from either Thomas Isham Browder or the Isham Browder of the revolutionary War. 

            But, in addition to these Isham Browders, there were other, unaccounted for, members of the Browder family, as well as an Arthur Browder (b. c1771) who married Charlotte (b. 1771) about 1790.  He is known to have relocated from Bladen County, North Carolina to Williamsburg District, South Carolina. 

            The 1800 census of Williamsburg, Marion District, South Carolina lists this Arthur Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (26-44), and presumed children, two males (10-15), one male (under 5), one female (10-15), and one female (under 10).  No slaves are listed.  Interestingly, on the same page is listed the Jacob Coleman family. 

            The 1810 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina again lists Arthur Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (26-44), and presumed children, one male (16-25), one male (10-15), three males (under 10), one female (10-15), and two females (under 10).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1820 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina also lists Arthur Browder (45 and over), as well as his presumed wife (26-44), and presumed children; two males (16-18), one male (10-15), one male (under 10), two females (10-15), and one female (under 10).  No slaves are listed.  Interestingly, the families of H. Henry Bostwick and Jonathan Bostwick are also listed. 

            The 1830 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina lists Charlotte Browder (40-49), along with presumed children; one male (20-29), one male (10-14), one female (20-29), and one female (10-14).  No slaves are listed.  So, it is obvious that this Arthur Browder was deceased by this time. 

            Also listed in the same 1830 census is the family of John Browder (50-59), along with one male (20-29), one male (5-9), two males (under 5), one female (20-29), and one female (5-9).  Presumably, he is one and the same as John Browder who was earlier listed in Chatham County, North Carolina. 

            The 1790 census of Chatham County, North Carolina lists John Browder (16 and over), along with five females (no age range specified), as well as three slaves, all within the same household.  He is probably the John Browder who was born about 1755 in North Carolina, and who married Frances Richardson of Tennessee.  He does show up in the Bladen County, North Carolina tax list of 1763. 

            Also, on the same page is listed Derias “Browdey” (16 and over), along with two males (under 16), and two females.  No slaves are listed for this family. This was, obviously, Darius Browder. Darius Browder enlisted in Company F. of the 62nd Infantry Regiment of Tennessee, CSA as a Second Lieutenant during the Civil War. 

            A John Browder is later named in the 1805 Roane County, Tennessee tax list, but it is unclear to me which John Browder this may be.

            Also, a Thomas Browder married Jane B. Montague on July 17, 1811 in Powhatan County, Virginia.

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Thomas Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (16-25), and presumed children; two males (under 10), and three females (under 10).  Also listed are two free colored persons (m. under 14), as well as five male slaves and six female slaves. 

            The 1830 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina also lists Thomas Browder (50-59), along with his presumed wife (30-39), as well as one male (20-29), one male (5-9), three females (5-9), and one female (under 5).  He is presumably one and the same as Thomas Browder from Bladen County, North Carolina. 

            The 1840 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina again lists Thomas Browder (60-69), along with his presumed wife (40-49), and presumed children; one male (5-9), one female (15-19), and one female (5-9).  No slaves are listed. 

            The 1830 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina also lists Joshua Browder (15-19), along with his presumed wife (20-29), and one male (under 5), one female (5-9), and one female (under 5).  This would have to be a son of Arthur and Charlotte Browder. 

            The 1840 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina again lists Joshua Browder (40-49), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; one male (10-14), two males (5-9), one male (under 5), and two females (10-14).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1850 census of Williamsburg district, South Carolina lists Charlott Browder (79), as residing within the household of Moses Brayboy (45), farmer.  This household also included Moses’ wife, Jane (35), and children, Martha (8), Maria (2), and H. V. (m. 2 months).  One would have to assume that Jane was Charlotte’s daughter, although this is not specified.

            Also listed on the same page in this census is the family of Sarah C. Browder (38), which included her children, Mary E. (16), H. E. (f. 15), L. J. (f. 10), B. R. (m. 3), and P. P. (m. 1). 

            Also, on the same page, is listed Joshua Browder (45 or 48), farmer, along with his presumed wife, Ann (35), and Mary A. (30).  Others, presumably children, listed were James W. (17), G. W. (15), Edwin (13), and Amarintha C. (1).  Also listed within this household are Gadson (23), Emma (18), and Laura S. (1).

            Joshua Browder (b. Williamsburg District, South Carolina), son of Arthur and Charlotte Browder, married Nancy Brayboy (born in 1802) in Williamsburg District, South Carolina in 1820.  Nancy is believed to have been a Native American.

            Other known children of Arthur and Charlotte Browder were Martin (b. 1807), and Isaac J. (b. 1816) and, presumably, Jane.  All children were born in Williamsburg District, South Carolina.

            The 1840 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina lists Martin Browder (30-39), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; two males (5-9), one male (under 5), one female (5-9), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina also lists Isaac Browder (20-29), along with his presumed wife (20-29), and presumed children; one male (under 5), two females (5-9), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            Presumably, another unknown South Carolina Browder had the following children; Arthur (b. 1784), Jeremiah (b. 1789), Charlotte (b. 1771), unknown, and Bessie (b. after 1775).  However, it seems obvious that this family likely also originated in Dinwiddie County, Virginia because a Jeremiah Browder is listed in the 1810 Tax List for that county as owning three slaves. 

            He is also listed in the 1810 census there.  He resided close to Jones, Littleberry, and the two Isham Browders, as well as others. 

            Jeremiah Browder, along with others, is again listed in the 1830 tax list.  Whether this is the same Jeremiah Browder as the one who resided in South Carolina is unclear.  But Jeremiah Browder, like a few others, does seem to have come from nowhere. 

            Another Jeremiah Browder married Susan Clements on January 28, 1790 in Dinwiddie County, Virginia.  I believe that this is one and the same as the following Jeremiah Browder.

            The 1810 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Jereh Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (26-44).  No slaves are listed.  It seems logical to assume that this Jereh Browder is a son of Jeremiah Browder, but this is not a certainty.  Interestingly, on the same page is listed the family of Littleberry Browder, John Bolling, and Lucy Booth.

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Jeremiah Browder (45 and over), along with his presumed children; one male (26-44), one male (under 10), one female (26-44), one female (10-15), and one female (under 10).  No slaves are listed. 

            The 1830 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Jeremiah Browder (50-59), along with one male (under 5) and one female (20-29).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Jeremiah Browder (60-69) along with two males (5-9), one female (30-39), and one female (under 5).  Neither Jeremiah nor the older female could read or write.  No slaves are listed. 

            Still other early members of the Browder family who remain unaccounted for include, but are not limited to, the following:

            John Browder (45 and over) is listed in the 1810 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, along with his wife (45 and over), and one male (under 10).  Eight slaves are listed for this family that year.

            The 1820 census of Sussex County, Virginia lists Joshua Browder (45 and over), along with his presumed wife (26-44), and presumed children; two males (under 10), two females (16-25), two females (10-15), and two females (under 10).  Also listed is one male slave and five female slaves. 

Most likely this is the Joshua Browder who was the son of a John Browder (d. c1814), who resided in Dinwiddie County, Virginia, and who also had another son named John, according to the 1788 tax records.  The John Browder in the above 1810 census is one and the same as the one who died about 1814 (Penland Browder, p. 54 & 56).  His son, John, is probably the John Browder who married Elizabeth Dyson on September 29, 1796 in Chesterfield County, Virginia.  John, Jr. died about 1817/18 (Penland Browder, p. 57).  So, he is not listed in the 1820 census.  His second son, John, Jr. was born about 1806 (ibid, p. 58). 

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Williamson Browder (26-44), along with his presumed wife (16-25), and one female (under 10).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia (Petersburg, West Ward) lists Wmson Browder (40-49), along with his presumed wife (40-49), and presumed children; one male (20-29), two females (15-19), and two females (10-14).  No slaves are listed.

            Williamson Browder is also found in the property tax records of Brunswick County, Virginia in 1814 and the Dinwiddie County tax records from 1815-1825 (Penland Browder, p. 57). 

            The 1820 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Dianna Browder [widow of John Browder, Sr.] (45 and over), along with her presumed children; one male (16-25), and one male (10-15).  Also listed is one male slave and two female slaves. 

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, North Carolina (Petersburg West Ward) lists John Browder (30-39), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; one male (10-14), two males (5-9), one male (under 5), and one female (under 5).  One female slave is also listed.

            The 1840 census of Caswell County, North Carolina lists Jno. Browder (40-49), along with his presumed wife (40-49), and presumed children; two males (15-19), one male (5-9), one female (20-29), one female (15-19), two females (10-14), one female (5-9), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Williamsburg District, South Carolina lists John Browder (50-59), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; three males (10-14), one male (5-9), one male (under 5), one female (15-19), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1850 census of McMinn County, Tennessee (Subdivision 23) lists, possibly, the same John Browder (60), along with his wife, Susan (55), and family members; Sarah (22), Mary (19), Craton (16), Artissa (14), Dorcas (12), Robt. (10), William (8), Stephen (6), and Nancy (4).  This census shows that John was born in North Carolina. 

            The 1850 census of McMinn County, Tennessee (Subdivision 23) also lists John’s likely brother, Edmond Browder (66), along with his wife, Lucinda (64).  This census states that Edmond was also born in North Carolina.  Both John and Edmund are likely sons of Edmund Browder of Chatham County, North Carolina. 

            The 1840 census of Sumter, South Carolina lists Jno. J. Browder (30-39), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; one male (10-14), two males (under 5), and two females (10-14).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Thos. Browder (60-69), along with his presumed wife (40-49), as well as one male (15-19), one female (10-14), and two females (5-9).  Also listed are two free colored males as well as one male slave and three female slaves.

            The 1850 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia (Northern District) lists Thomas Browder (72), along with his presumed wife, Amy G. (50), as well as household members; Adelina (17), and Martha E. (13).  This census states that Thomas was born in Virginia. 

            The 1840 census of Kanawha County, Virginia lists Thos. Browder (20-29), along with one female (70-79), one female (40-49), two females (15-19), two females (10-14), and two females (5-9).  No slaves are listed.

            There was also a Thomas Browder who is found in the land records of Bladen County, North Carolina in 1770 (Penland Browder, p. xi).  I have been unable to place him properly within any family grouping.  However, since he is listed in 1770, he would have had to have been born as early as 1749.  Since there is no indication that John and Elizabeth Browder had any children after Joseph (although this is not impossible), I believe he is most likely to have been an unknown child of one of their children (most likely Richard Browder), since he is obviously an early Browder.

            The 1840 census of Dinwiddie County, Virginia lists Jeremiah Browder (60-69), along with two males (5-9), one female (30-39), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Halifax County, Virginia (South District) lists Jessee Browder (40-49), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and presumed children; two males (5-9), one female (10-14), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1850 census of Halifax County, Virginia (South District) lists Jessee Browder (55), Planter, along with his wife, Susan (48), and children; Nancy (19), John N. (18), David A. (16), Susan A. (11), and Martha J. (9).  All family members are listed as having been born in Virginia.

            The 1860 census of Stokes County, North Carolina lists Jesse Browder (63), rock and brick mason, along with his wife, Susan (56), and children; Susan Ann (22), and Nancy (28).  All family members are listed as having been born in Virginia.

            The 1840 census of Halifax County, Virginia (South District) lists Ann Browder (30-39), along with her presumed children; one male (10-14), three females (5-9), and one female (under 5).  No slaves are listed.

            The 1840 census of Sussex County, Virginia lists Jos. D. Browder (20-29), along with his presumed wife (30-39), and slaves; three males and two females.

            The 1850 census of Sussex County, Virginia lists Joshua D. Browder (31), along with his wife, Martha C. (28), and children; Junius C. (7), William T. (6), and Sarah E. (2).  This census shows that Joshua was born in Virginia.

            The 1840 census of Sussex county, Virginia lists Elizabeth Browder [also listed in the 1820 census/widow of John Browder, Jr.] (60-69), along with one male (20-29), and one male (5-9).  Also listed are five male slaves and four female slaves.

            Finally, one thing is also virtually certain; our Arthur Browder (c1715-c1739) was also the very first Browder to be given the name Arthur, as far as is known.  The ancestry of these others named Arthur Browder is unknown, as would be their potential relationship with this Arthur Browder.

            Still, to end the story of Jones Browder, I must reiterate the following, which will help to decisively tie up the previously detailed family connections.  The Dinwiddie County, Virginia Order Book of the Circuit Superior Court (FHL Film#7406) details the settlement of Jones Browder’s estate on May 3, 1836.  It was finally settled on that date by John T. Sydnor, Executor of David Thacker, Administrator of Jones Browder’s estate. 

            On that date, his estate was ordered divided among his first cousins on the paternal side of his family (interesting that no one on his maternal side sued for any of it and also received nothing), and it was divided among them on September 21, 1836.  The details are not necessary to delineate here since I will do so elsewhere.  That which, from his record, does add to our knowledge of this family at the time in question is a fact mentioned in the order, which could easily be overlooked by researchers who may not realize its significance. 

It is noted that David Thacker had also been administrator of the estates of Isham Browder, Augustine Browder, Nancy Fears (formerly Browder), Tabitha Clarke (formerly Browder), Martha Whitmore (formerly Browder), all children of Thomas Isham Browder and Mary Cox, as well as Mary Meador [I corrected this] (formerly Browder) and Richard Browder, heirs of Martha Browder (who married Isham C. Browder), deceased. 

            Wow!!!  After all of the speculation by others, and also by me, over the years past, the very first thing that MUST be acknowledged here, based on the above, is that this one notation proves, with absolutely NO doubt whatsoever, that Martha Browder, who married my Isham C. Browder, WAS a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder!  It names her two certainly still living children, Richard and Mary.  This connection is NO LONGER subject to speculation by anyone!  My Isham C. Browder DID marry his second cousin, Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder. 

            And the very fact that David Thacker administered not only the estate of Jones Browder, but also the estates of Isham Browder (obviously Thomas Isham Browder, since the estate of Isham C. Browder was administered by Clement Whittmore) and his son, Augustine [C.] Browder, as well as all of Augustine’s sisters (except for Mary, who was deceased and had no heirs, for they had also passed away by this date), shows a link between Arthur Browder and Thomas Isham Browder that is shown in no other extant record that I am aware of.  The very fact that they are linked at all, in any way, is significant!  If their relationship had not been proven before, I submit that it has been with this document! (thanks to Judy Bean for posting this document on-line.  I did not find this document until December of 2012!).
