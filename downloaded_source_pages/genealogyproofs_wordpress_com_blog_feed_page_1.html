Origins of the South Carolina Browder Family

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div.

Feb. 23, 2025

Based on what records that we do have, it is clear that the South Carolina Browder line starts with a John Browder (c1731-c1769) who married someone named Amelia (d. c1785). They are found in Bladen County, North Carolina first. In 1763, the Bladen County, North Carolina tax list names Henry “Bosswill” (Boswell) together with “Jno.” Browder (John Browder who married Amelia). It also names David “Braveboy” (Brayboy?). So, John Browder is placed there are early as 1763. What John’s relationship was with Henry Boswell is not known, but the fact that they are listed together in the same tax list demonstrates that they held property together. Perhaps it was a business.

I do not know yet who Henry Boswell actually was. But he is a clue in that he could well have been a relative of Amelia.

Another clue comes in the form of the Nov. 16, 1776 will of Thomas Sanders (Bladen Co., NC Deed Book 1738-1779, p. 5):

Thomas Sanders of Bladen County, NC states that he was very weak & sick, and bequeaths to his “bloved wife, Emilea…my children viz: Elizabeth Boswell, Elisabeth Ellis, Amelie Morley, Thomas Browder & Elay Sanders, my daughter in law.”

Wit: Duncan Morris, Senvill Hardwick.

One has to understand (as if this would be a real question) that Thomas Browder was a son of John Browder, not Thomas Sanders, even though Sanders calls him his “son” in his will. So, why did Sanders call Thomas his son? Because Amelia married Thomas Sanders after the death of John Browder. Thomas Browder was already an adult by that time since he is found in the land records, so he was born about 1749.

One has to presume that the above Elizabeth Boswell either married a Boswell or had the second name of Boswell but was not yet married. But an Elisabeth Ellis is also named in Sander’s will so that it becomes obvious that one was his daughter and one was his step-daughter. Since Elisabeth Ellis is named second, it seems that we can conclude that Elizabeth “Boswell” was a sister of Thomas Browder and, therefore, a daughter of John Browder and Amelia.

It is known that Henry Boswell (b. c1740), married an Elizabeth and some state that she was Elizabeth Sanders, based on this will. I am going to suggest that it is the other way around and that Thomas Sander’s daughter, Elisabeth, married an Ellis and that John Browder’s daughter, Elizabeth, married Henry Boswell because we see a direct relationship between John Browder and Henry Boswell early on, but do not see the same thing concerning Thomas Sanders. It seems obvious, thus far, that Henry Boswell was a son-in-law to John Browder, Boswell having married John’s daughter, Elizabeth.

Now, by 1770 Thomas Browder, son of John Browder and Amelia, is found in the land records for Bladen County, North Carolina. He purchased some land that year from Dennis Lennon.

We don’t know who Thomas Browder (b. c1749) married. But we can continue to trace him because in 1771 Thomas Browder sold some land in Bladen County, North Carolina to his half-brother, Christopher Sanders, son of the above Thomas Sanders.

On Apr. 27, 1778, Christopher Sanders, son of Thomas Sanders, of Bladen County, North Carolina, wrote his will. In it he mentions his daughter, Amelia Sanders, among others.

On Nov. 11, 1779, Thomas Browder, son of John Browder and Amelia, received a land grant of 200 acres in Bladen County, North Carolina. So, he was clearly still alive.

Amelia died about 1785 as shown by the fact that she deeded to her son, Thomas Browder, her property in Bladen County, North Carolina.

“AMELIA SANDERS TO THOS BROWDER NO 472

Bladen County, NC

Deed Book 1, page 391

To all to whom these presents Shall come I Amelia Sanders do send greetings Know ye that I the said Amelia Sanders of Bladen County and providence of No Carolina being very sick and weak of body but of perfect mind Memory do hereby for and in consideration of love and affection I have and do bear my beloved Son Thomas Browder have given and granted and do hereby give grant and bequeath unto my said beloved Son all my real personal Estate that is to say all my Money goods Bonds accompts my Cattle Hogs and Sheep Poultry and Negroes and all this property to me belonging not here mentioned with the condition of his letting my beloved daughter Lucy Ellis have one half of the pewter and with allowing me a sufficient Mentenance as long as please God let me live I hereby make over to my Son Thomas Browder as my free will and Gift as above Mentioned together with a full power to dispose of as he thinks proper given under my hand this twenty fourth day of Octr 1785

Signed Sealed & delivered       Amelia her ++++ mark Sanders

in presents of us

Wm Brown

Nancy Brogdon

Bladen August 1785 This Deed was proved by William Brown and ordered to be Registered

John White C. C.”

Further, the 1790 census of Bladen County, North Carolina lists Thomas Browder.

On June 22, 1795, Thomas Browder deeded his property to Johathan Meares (Deed Book 7, page 266). This deed was proven in September of 1799.

It becomes obvious that Thomas Browder and his family moved to Williamsburg, South Carolina about 1799 both because the above deed was proven in that year, and because of the fact that on December 11, 1807, Thomas Browder, Jr. petitioned the court in Williamsburg to be allowed to sell whatever property his father had to pay off debts.

Now, everyone understands that Arthur Browder (c1770-c1825) came from Bladen County, North Carolina (yet, he shows up in none of the records there, indicating that he was probably a minor at least almost until he moved to South Carolina). Since the only line shown in the records there is the one detailed above, it becomes obvious that Arthur Browder was a son of Thomas Browder, Sr. and a brother of Thomas Browder, Jr. So, the earliest Browders in the area of Williamsburg, SC would have been descended from either Arthur Browder or Thomas Browder, Jr. Sadly, I have yet to be able to detail the family of Thomas Browder, Jr. But that of Arthur Browder has been done.

The one remaining question is exactly where John Browder (b. c1731) who married Amelia came from. Previously I had connected him to John Browder II of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, who married Mary. I now have to accept that as a mistake because the local tax records are just too clear that John had an entirely different son named John who married Dianna and remained in Dinwiddie County until his death. Without going into extensive detail, the only real possibility is that John’s father was Edmund Browder (b. c1716), son of John Browder and Elizabeth Jones. This Edmund Browder (I have tried to point out, to little avail) was actually the father of the Edmund Browder who later moved, with his family, to Chatham County, North Carolina and who wrote his will there in 1785. People have, over the years, conflated two Edmund Browders into only one. Edmund Browder, Jr. is the one who married Ann Deloney. His father, Edmund, also married someone named Ann, whose maiden name is not known. In effect, the two brothers, Edmund Browder, Jr. and John Browder (both sons of Edmund Browder, Sr. and Ann) both moved at some point to North Carolina, being the first of the Browder family to do so. They moved to different counties, but that was by no means unusual.

Regardless, Arthur Browder was apparently still a minor in 1790 since he is not listed in that census of Bladen County, North Carolina as Thomas was. Thus, his birth date is estimated as about 1770. But he would have married Charlotte about 1792 in any case because I have been able to determine that their first son, Hardy Browder, was born in Bladen County, North Carolina about 1794. For me, this clearly indicates the possibility that Charlotte’s maiden name may well have been Hardy.

Hardy Browder did have a daughter named Charlotte (b. c1829), who married Henry Parker, and a son named Arthur (c1835-1860). Hardy Browder died in Pike County, Alabama.

This is not all that could be done here, but is all that I am going to do at this time.

Clearing Up Some Early Browder Family Research:

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (Dec. 19, 2023, updated Jan. 1, 2025)

First of all, let’s be clear about one thing. The earliest researchers into the Browder family line were direct descendants of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Richard was born about 1719 and died about 1807. His birth is not actually recorded anywhere, but we can ascertain it based on his connections to his siblings as well as one record which is suggestive, but not to be considered as proof. That record is the following:

Aug. 11, 1719 – JOHN BROWDER along with several others, was summoned to answer a petition and complaint of William Davis, Overseer of Highways, for their contempt in refusing to assist in clearing the highway, and these persons (except JOHN BROWDER), failing to show just cause, it was ordered that each of them, except BROWDER, pay Davis 5 shillings. Present: Maj. Robert Munford (Va. Gen. Soc. Qtly., Nov. 1986, p. 58).

The timing of this alleged incident is interesting to me as it seems to have been around the time of Elizabeth’s pregnancy with their son, Richard Browder, and this birth may have had something to do with John’s refusal to appear in this situation, as he probably needed to be home. Obviously, John Browder had already made his reasoning clear, and it was accepted, whatever it was.

The earliest researchers into the family didn’t know the name of Richard’s father anymore. It had already been lost to time by the time research was being conducted. This fact is first demonstrated by a passage in the addendum to the booklet, Genealogy of David Browder, of Virginia and His Descendants, in which David P. Browder refers to research he had recently obtained through his nephew, the Rev. Robert W. Browder, which Robert Browder, in turn, had obtained from his father, the Rev. George R. Browder, who had, in turn, obtained the information from his father, Robert Browder (b. 1804), son of David Browder and Mary Cousins. In the addendum to this book by Thomas E. Browder, David states this fact in the following manner:

“One —– Browder, an Englishman, married a Welsh woman, and emigrated to America early in the 18th century, and settled in Virginia, where he lived and died. His name has not been retained in the family. The children of these progenitors of the Browder family were Richard, Joseph, Arthur, George and Nancy as far as known. . . .

Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Thompson Browder, married a cousin Isham Browder. Their children were Mary and Richard so far as is known (Browder, Thomas, pp. 37-38).”

David P. Browder also reiterated this fact in a letter written on Nov. 27, 1901. Even so, no one had any direct proof of the union of Isham Browder and Martha Browder until I uncovered it from court records.

Now, there are some errors here, but the information did lead later researchers in the right direction. The “Englishman” in question, we now know, was John Browder who married Elizabeth. I’m not going to get into what her maiden name was here because it is not necessary to the narrative. Suffice it to be said that she is here referred to as a “Welsh woman”. The text also states that the “Englishman” emigrated to Virginia. However, we now know that he was actually born here.

The rest of the book deals with the earliest known descendants of some of the children of Richard and Mary Browder. None of them were named Isham, By the way.

We actually know at least the names of three sons of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, including John, because of one particular land deed. On July 20, 1736, John Browder was granted 300 acres of land, located on Gravelly Run, by King George II. According to the document, this land joined that which he had previously been granted. It also bordered on the land of his brothers (as stated in the document) George Andrew Browder and Edmund W. Browder (Prince George County Land Grant Records Book [PGCLGRB] 17, p. 125).

So, we know that Edmund and Elizabeth Browder had at least three sons, John, George Andrew, and Edmund W. If they had any daughters who lived to maturity, it is not known.

Four of the children of John and Elizabeth Browder are known because their births are listed in the Bristol Parish Register. These are William, George, Anne, and Joseph. They are the youngest four, the others having been born before the extant Registry was started. The ones born prior to the start of said Registry were Arthur, Edmund, John, and Richard. So, we have exact birth dates for the last four, but have to estimate the others. Notably, none of them are named Isham either. But the two to be focused on here were the brothers, Arthur (b. 1715) and Richard (b. c1719).

Again, those who did the earliest research into the Browder family were descended from Richard Browder. The open question was who the parents of Isham Browder were. They did not try to answer that in the booklet. They only stated that he was a cousin of Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder. And, in fact, he was her cousin as court records demonstrate (we will get to them), but early researchers were looking at the wrong Isham Browder as the cousin of Martha Browder. They THOUGHT that he was the Rev. War soldier who had died in Kentucky in 1830. But they were wrong. Since that time, others have attached the Rev. War soldier to Arthur Browder as Arthur’s son. They are also mistaken.

Now, Arthur Browder did indeed have a son. But that son is not named in any extant record. There is only one record that demonstrates that Arthur had a son. From the Bristol Parish Vestry Book, the notation in question is presented here, verbatim:

“Concerning the Returns of Processioning for the Year 1739 (pp. 98-99) the notation reads as follows:

“Miles Thweatt and William Batt[e] the Same (Processioned all the Lands in their Precinct) excepting two lines of Batte & Jordan they disagreeing & another piece belonging to the HEIR of ARTHER BROWDER the HEIR being an Infant.”

This short notation demonstrates that (1) Arthur Browder was already deceased and that (2) he had only one heir, who was an “infant” in 1739. Unlike today, a person was considered an “infant” until the age of 21, at which time one could inherit land and perform other legal actions. So, the term was used differently back then than it is today. It did not mean he had to still be a baby. Sadly, the notation neither names the child’s mother, nor the child. But there can be no doubt that the members of the Vestry knew exactly who the child was and who his mother was.

Regardless, the same sources from whom these earliest researchers obtained their information also stated that Richard Browder had taken in the son of his deceased brother, Arthur, and had brought him up within his own household. And those earliest sources named him Isham. So, it is actually clear that Isham Browder was THE son of Arthur Browder and that he was brought up by Arthur’s brother, Richard after Arthur died. What happened to the child’s mother is never told.

Now, anyone can see that Isham Browder had to have been born prior to 1739. We know that this Isham Browder first married Agnes Tatum in Surry County, Virginia. We also know that they were married about 1758 because they had a son, Isham Browder, Jr., who was born on March 17, 1759. He was christened on June 3, 1759, in Albemarle Parish, Sussex County, Virginia (Albemarle Parish Register [Colonial Dames] 44). Isham, Jr.’s godparents were Nathaniel Beddingfield, William Weathers and Phoebe Pettway, all named in said document.

PLEASE NOTE that this Isham Browder (1) did not marry anyone named Martha and (2) that he had a son named Isham by his wife, Agnes Tatum. That son was born in 1759. There is no way that Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Rev. War could be descended from this Isham Browder, period. So, the Rev. War Isham Browder is not descended from Arthur Browder and is not descended from his son, Isham, either.

If that isn’t proof enough, then here’s more.

There is no further record of either Agnes Tatum-Browder or her son, Isham Browder, Jr. Agnes isn’t even mentioned in the will of her father, Christopher Tatum, who died in 1769. Isham Browder, Jr. isn’t either. Whatever tragedy happened, these two were clearly deceased by 1769. In fact, they were deceased earlier than that because Isham Browder, Sr. married again on Feb. 3, 1767 to Talitha Cox in Lunenburg County, Virginia. Documentation states that Talitha Cox was of Mecklenburg County, Virginia (Mecklenburg County Will Book and Marriage Records 3, p. 125), but she was actually born in Brunswick County – the part later made into Mecklenburg County. Isham and Talitha were married within Cumberland Parish, Lunenburg County, Virginia. The documentation also states that Isham Browder was of Halifax County, Virginia. So, that is where he resided at that time. 

PLEASE NOTE that Halifax County, Virginia absorbed parts of Surry and Isle of Wight Counties in 1732. It has become clear that Isham Browder, Sr. resided in Halifax County, but, for whatever reason, attended church within Albemarle Parish which was partly in Surry County. Perhaps that part of Surry County was absorbed by Halifax County in 1732. The lines were rather fluid during the earliest years in any case.

There is more than ample evidence and proof that Isham Browder, Sr. and Talitha Cox had another son named Isham. I am certainly not going to present all of it here. But here is the basic framework.

Isham and Talitha Browder are found in Rowan County, North Carolina within a year of their marriage. So, they had moved from Virginia to North Carolina, settling on Wolf Island Creek. That part of Rowan County was formed into Guilford County within a few years. Isham Browder, Sr. died in Guilford County in 1784, according to ample documentation. After his death, that part of Guilford County was made into Rockingham County. Talitha Browder died in Rockingham County in 1828.

Now, their son, Isham, was born about 1774. This is proven by census records. Said records also show that his wife’s name was Martha. They also show that she was also born about 1774.

By 1810, according to the Rockingham County, NC census of that year, Isham Browder and Martha Browder had two sons, as well as one daughter. Their sons are listed as being between the ages of 10-15 and under 10, respectively. The daughter is listed in the next to youngest age range (10-15). We know that one son, the older one, was Richard and the daughter was Mary, but the name of the other son is a mystery. He is never again directly referenced in any known document except the 1820 census. He obviously reached adulthood, but appears to have soon died.

This census shows that the Isham Browder family resided close to his mother, Talitha Browder, and close to the Augustine Claiborne Browder family (his deceased brother’s family).

However, backing up just a  bit, there is a document that establishes their relationship.

Isham Browder and Martha Browder, his wife, gave Power of Attorney to Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia to sell Martha’s interest in about 200 acres of land, located on the Gravelly Run (RCDB N, p. 339). While all of this is not directly specified, Richard Browder, Martha’s father, was not a large land owner and probably only owned about 200 acres at the time of his death. So, it is certain that he did not bequeath it all to Martha, but bequeathed each of his living children a certain percentage of divided interest in said land. But sadly, we don’t have a Will to consult.

NOTE: It is likely that the Richard Browder mentioned in the Power of Attorney was the son of Martha’s brother, David Browder and his wife, Mary Cousins, since Martha’s father had already died about 1807, and that the land in question must have been inherited from her father, Richard Browder.

It should be understood that there is no reason for Martha to have been named in such a document other than the fact that she had inherited a percentage in said land, and that she was actually the one giving Power of Attorney to sell land that she had inherited. Otherwise, it would have only been necessary for Isham to have been named.

So, this document shows a direct relationship between Richard Browder and his daughter, Martha, and shows that she married her cousin, Isham Browder. It clearly shows a connection between Martha Browder and Dinwiddie County, Virginia. The fact that Power of Attorney was given to a member of the Browder family also shows conclusively that Martha was a member of the Browder family. Otherwise, why would she not have elected a member of some other family? This is the only known document that conclusively names Martha Browder as the wife of Isham Browder, although the division of Jones Browder’s property (we will get to that) also indicates it directly.

From RCDB N, p. 339 (from a copy by genealogist, Maude Reynolds):

“Browder, Richard from BROWDER, ISHAM & WIFE, Power of Attorney, 200 A of Land: Know all men by these presents that we ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER his WIFE, both of the county of Rockingham and state of N. C., have for good cause to us moving, have nominated, authorized, ordained and appointed, and do by these presents authorize, ordain and appoint our friend Richard Browder of the county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth of Va., to act, make over convey and confirm a certain piece or parcel of land situate, lying and being in the said county of Dinwiddia and commonwealth aforesaid and the waters of the Gravelly Run, and contains about 200 acres of land: And we, the said ISHAM BROWDER and MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves and heirs, creditors and administrators do make or convey and confirm unto the said Richard Browder full power absolute authority to sell and dispose, make over, convey and confirm at the will and pleasure, a sure and good right and title to the aforesaid land and premises as above described, to whomsoever he may think proper, as full and ample in every respect, as we could do were we personally present, to all which matter and things – we the said ISHAM BROWDER and the said MARTHA BROWDER for ourselves our heirs creditors and Administrators firmly by these presents, sealed with our seals and dated this day of ___ in the year 1808. Signed, sealed and acknowledged in the presence of ISHAM BROWDER (seal) Augustine C. Browder MARTHA BROWDER (seal) Thos. Winston Joseph Mclain St. of N. C. Rockingham Co. May Session 1810: The written power of attorney was duly proved in open court by the oath of Thomas Winston & on motion ordered to be recorded. R. Galloway, C.”

OK, so in late 1799 or very early 1800, Isham Browder, son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox, and Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder, were married, probably in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Court records also prove this union. There is no known marriage certificate, so no other details are known. 

And, so, on to the court documents. It is only necessary to present one document since they are all very repetitive. These court documents have to do with the estate of Jones Browder.

Now, for clarification, Jones Browder (c1764-1826) is known by documentation to have been the son of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786)who had other sons also – Samuel, Frederick, and Harrison. These last three sons were killed during the Rev. War and, so, Jones, the youngest (too young to serve during the war) inherited their legacies. After Jones Browder died, family members sued for portions of his property since he had no legal descendants for it to go to. The court determined that shares of this property would be divided between the FIRST COUSINS OF JONES BROWDER ON THE PATERNAL SIDE OF HIS FAMILY. Only a few others were allowed to take part, the other parties agreeing to this.

PLEASE NOTE that the Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) who was the father of Jones Browder was NOT the son of John Browder and Elizabeth. He was a different Arthur Browder. In fact, because of the stipulation that the estate be divided among the first cousins of Jones Browder, it becomes clear that this Arthur Browder was a son of Richard Browder and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. The reason we can conclude that this is true is because every legatee was (1) a descendant of John and Elizabeth Browder, but (2) only the ones that were allowed to join by permission were NOT descendants of Richard Browder and Mary. In other words, the actual legatees were ALL descendants of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia except those allowed by the court to also participate. Since Jones Browder was a grandson, therefore, of Richard and Mary Browder, all of the legatees were also grandchildren of Richard and Mary Browder except those allowed by permission to take part.

PLEASE ALSO NOTE that Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) did NOT have a son named Isham. All of his sons are known. They were Frederick, Harrison, Samuel, and Jones. There are two reason se KNOW that he had no other sons, least of all one named Isham. (1) Jones Browder inherited everything, period. He obtained the estate of his father and the Rev. War pensions of his deceased brothers as the only heir.

Sept. 6, 1777 – The three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel Browder, sons of Arthur Browder (b. c1742), enlisted together into the Continental Army in Capt. Peter Jones’ Company of Continental Regulars, 14th Virginia Regiment, commanded by Col. Charles Lewis. They are known to have been at Valley Forge during the bitter winter of 1777. They are also said (perhaps incorrectly) to have been at White Plains, and West Point, but obviously not at any battles at these places because they took place prior to their enlistments.

Winter 1777-1778 – During the bitter winter Continental soldiers massed at Valley Forge. Pvt Isham Browder was among them. This is when the Continental Army was finally made into an effective fighting force by the efforts of Gen. George Washington and Gen. Baron von Steuben. Again, the three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel, were also there. Interestingly, so was John Marshall (grandson of Rev. James Keith and Mary Isham Randolph), who would later become Chief Justice of the US Supreme Court. Whether they all were acquainted with each other or not is unknown, but it seems possible.

Feb. 28, 1778 – Frederick Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at Lancaster, Pennsylvania.

1778 – Samuel Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at Lancaster Pennsylvania.

June 28, 1778 – The Battle of Monmouth Courthouse takes place. Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830) is wounded in the arm during this battle. This battle was a stalemate between the forces. Several mutinies took place afterward, including that of Maj. Gen. Benedict Arnold.

Nov. 26, 1778 – Harrison Browder, son of Arthur Browder, dies while serving as a soldier during the Revolutionary War, probably at York, Pennsylvania.

June 15, 1786 – Jones Browder, son of Arthur Browder and Rebecca Jones (the other three brothers may have had a different mother), who was too young to enlist in the Revolutionary War along with his brothers, inherits his deceased brother’s Revolutionary War service monies as well as 300 acres of land from their war bounty. John Lewis and Littleberry Browder appeared earlier on April 19th before James Greenway and Edward Pegram, Jr, Justices in Dinwiddie County, Virginia, stating that Jones Browder was the only surviving heir. The document not only shows that the three brothers were deceased, but that their father, Arthur, was also.

NOTE: Both Arthur Browder and Littleberry Browder were brothers.

Oct. 2, 1826 – Jones Browder, son of Arthur Browder and Rebecca Jones, and grandson of Richard Browder and Mary, dies at Blanford in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. At his death he was one of the few Browders to still own land at Gravelly Run in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. It totaled just over 345 acres. He also owned two slaves and six horses. But he had no descendants to leave his property to.

1832 – Some family members initiated a lawsuit over the property of Jones Browder since he left no will and had no descendants.

Apr. 2, 1834 – From the document dated April 2, 1834 (Dinwiddie County, Virginia Chancery Order Book 1, 1832-1852, pp. 46-47), concerning the division of Jones Browder’s estate, the following is copied verbatim (but structurally modified for readability, also adding information in brackets as a help to identify persons named):

“Mary Pentecost, formerly [Mary Thompson] Browder [daughter of Richard and Mary Browder], John W. P. Pool [Pettypool] and Mary [actually, Nancy A. Sydnor exor of David Thacker decd do render an amount of his Testators administrations of the estate of Jones Browder decd before a commissioner . . . is directed to . . . and settle the same and make report thereof to the Court with any matters specially stated deemed pertinent by himself, or which may be required by the parties to be so stated, and that the commissioner advertize once a week for two months in some newspaper printed in the city of Richmond and in the Cty of Washington for all persons who may claim to be distributes of Jones Browder decd in the maternal line to appear before him and prove their relationship to the decedent on or before the first day of September next or they will be excluded from all share of the estate.”

NOTE: No members of the maternal line ever responded, so none ever became part of the suit.

Now, to be clear, Martha Browder (c1774-c1811) is mentioned multiple times in these records from the Dinwiddie County, Virginia Order Book of the Circuit Superior Court, which details the settlement of Jones Browder’s estate, although she was already deceased by the time the case went to court and, thus, her children, Richard and Mary, are also named in her stead. Martha is mentioned because she was a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, and Jones Browder (c1764-c1826) was her nephew, making her children cousins of Jones Browder. This becomes absolutely clear from the text. So, if her children were first cousins to Jones Browder (and they were), then the father of Jones Browder had to have been her brother. There is no way around this. And it has been established by documentation that Jones Browder had three brothers, Harrison, Frederick, and Samuel, and that all of them were sons of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786). That makes Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) Martha’s brother.

THIS Arthur Browder, clearly a son of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, is NOT the one who may have married Mary Isham Randolph (c1716-Dec. 1772). He is, instead, believed to have first married a daughter of Gabriel and Grace Harrison, although this has not been proven. He is known to have married Rebecca Jones, perhaps his second wife.

The case was finally settled on April 4, 1837 by John T. Sydnor, Executor of David Thacker, who had been Administrator of Jones Browder’s estate. On that date, Jones’ estate was ordered divided among his FIRST COUSINS on the PATERNAL side of his family. It was divided among some of them on September 21, 1836, and also on April 4, 1837. Martha Browder and her heirs (children), Richard and Mary, are named in these documents. Martha Browder, who married Isham Browder, Jr. (son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox), therefore, again, WAS a daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.

For the children of Martha Browder (daughter of Richard and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia) to have been first cousins on the paternal side of Jones Browder’s family, Martha had to be a sister to Arthur Browder, father of Jones Browder, period. But they didn’t have to be in order to be named because Martha was a first cousin on the paternal side of the family in question herself. So, SHE is the one actually named as an inheritor. But, since she was already deceased when everything was decided, her children inherited in her stead.

NOTE: Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Rev. War, who later moved to Kentucky, was deceased by the time this lawsuit was initiated. However, that would not have prevented his still-living wife and children from participating IF they had had a claim to the estate. But none of them ever participated. This leads to the FACT that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was NOT a first cousin on the paternal side of this family, nor were his children. He was simply NOT descended from any of these people, except for John and Elizabeth Browder. Remember, only those who were not descended from Richard and Mary Browder, but WERE descended from John and Elizabeth Browder, were allowed to participate if they petitioned the court and it was accepted by the other parties. But none of Isham’s descendants did that either. In fact, several lines descended from John and Elizabeth did not take part. Perhaps they did not feel that they deserved a part of Jones Browder’s estate. Others, on the other hand, did.

I submit that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was indeed a descendant of John and Elizabeth Browder. But his parents were NOT any combination of Arthur Browder and wife, or of any Isham and Martha Browder union. Proof has been provided that the Isham Browder who married his cousin, Martha (purported to have been the parents of Isham Browder (1762-1830) could not have been his parents, not least of which because they were born AFTER he was. Also, he was NOT a son of Arthur Browder (c1742-c1786) because ALL of his sons are known from court documentation and other records and none of them were named Isham. Finally, Isham Browder (1762-1830) could not have been a son of Isham Browder, Sr. (who married Agnes Tatum and later Talitha Cox), because Isham Browder, Sr. had sons name Isham by both marriages (yes, that sometimes happened), and neither one was born in 1762. And we know that Pvt. Isham Browder was born in 1762 because of his military pension records and his tombstone.

ADDENDUM:

Genealogy research being relatively new even during my early years (I started in 1976), and somewhat prior to that, some people began asking questions about their heritage quite early, and for that, I am grateful. The first to be mentioned in this regard is David Parrish Browder, Sr. of Logan County, Kentucky (b. 1882), great-grandson of Richard Browder (c1719-c1807) and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. He is known to have conducted extensive interviews with living family members. Part of his questioning focused on “Isham Browder”. David thought that the Isham Browder he was receiving information about was the Isham Browder (1762-1830) who had moved to Fulton County, Kentucky. He basically received accurate information, but had it confused with the wrong Isham Browder.

This is shown in a letter he wrote on March 6, 1937 to Gussie Browder, who resided in Fulton, Kentucky. In this letter David stated that he felt “certain” that Richard Browder’s brother, Arthur, had had a son named Isham. He was the first known to have connected Isham Browder to Arthur Browder as his infant heir mentioned in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book. Obviously, David had also read the excerpt in the Vestry Book which, again, did not actually name Arthur’s heir. The letter also establishes David’s understanding that Richard Browder had taken Arthur’s infant heir into his own household to be brought up following Arthur’s death. And this information, taken by itself, is correct, as records show.

However – and this is where the inaccuracy comes in – David also believed that the Isham Browder brought up by Richard Browder was a father of Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Revolutionary War. He also believed that the elder Isham Browder had married his cousin, Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.

Thus, this latter part is completely incorrect, as records show. The Isham Browder who was the son of Arthur Browder was brought up by Richard and Mary Browder and did have a son named Isham. But it was Isham, Jr. who married his cousin, Martha. In so many words, David had obtained basically the correct information, but none of it had to do with Isham Browder (1762-1830) of Kentucky. We now know these things because of extant court documentation which David apparently did not uncover, but which is brought out here.

Now, a cousin of David P. Browder, Thomas E. Browder of Olmstead, Kentucky (great-grandson of Richard and Mary Browder), had also done a great deal of research. Sadly, most of his research was destroyed after his death. However, he wrote a book entitled, “The Genealogy of David Browder of Virginia and His descendants”. The book is dated Nov. 27, 1901.

It is interesting to me that the two earliest known researchers into the Browder family were descendants of Richard (c1719-c1807) and Mary Browder. It seems that their interest was initially sparked by their hope to be connected to Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Revolutionary War. They were in error. Regardless, they did come up with some valuable initial research even if their efforts to connect to that particular Isham Browder were a bit misguided since he was not a descendant of Richard and Mary Browder. It was actually a good guess on their part, though, since it becomes obvious by way of their research that at least one Isham Browder was brought up by Richard and Mary Browder – just not that one. If they had not asked the questions concerning Isham Browder we might not know the relationships today except for what can be gleaned from court records.

Anyone should be proud to be descended from Pvt. Isham Browder (1762-1830). But they owe it not only to their descendants, but also to him, to get his genealogy right. His life has been misinterpreted for far too long. It takes strength to let go of cherished notions even when faced with evidence that they are incorrect. But I submit, his descendants, and others, have that strength within them and will now strive to do right by him and his descendants.

Browder as Opposed to Broderbund

OK, I suppose it’s about time I posted this. I have been doing genealogy research since I was in high school way back before the internet was even thought of. I started in 1976, so I do know a thing or two about my own ancestry and have read, probably, everything that is out there, at least about my direct line. I have even written a couple of books. I am descended from several James Forte colonists. My direct line begins with Edmund Browder. He was born about 1651 and died sometime before 1739. He probably did not reside in James Forte itself for very long, but he definitely arrived there, probably from Ireland. That in and of itself has led some people to assume that the Browder surname must be derived from some Irish surname, and many attach our name to the Broderick family name. That is the first thing that I would like to point out here that is actually false. That said, let me state a few things for the record, please. (1) The ancestors of anyone and everyone who goes by the Browder surname came originally from Flanders and were weavers/embroiderers by trade. They entered England and soon began to use a term derived from their Flemish language, which became an Old English term, as a surname. The word was browderer”. It literally meant a weaver or embroiderer of clothing. So, like many other families, including, as an example, the Weavers, they derived their surname from their trade. There were two main variants – Browderer and Broderer. Both of these were eventually shortened, leaving off the “er” on the ends, to Browder and Broder. It’s that simple. So, (2) Browder is NOT an “Americanized” name, as some state. It was not made up after we came over here because it existed in England and Ireland beforehand. Our families traveled from England, to Ireland, and then to Virginia. (3) Therefore, there is no link to the Broderick surname, or to their Coat of Arms. And there is no link to any Viking forename, s some posit. These latter claims come directly from speculation done by earlier researchers, which they stated was speculation. (4) While the Flemish immigrants into England (during the 1400s) may well have had Germanic or even Viking ancestry, this cannot be proven because there are no records to consult. (5) Edmund was NOT the son of any Johannes Broder and Mary Adams, as some continuously perpetuate with no documentation. This appears to have come directly from ancestry algorithms. (6) Someone, somewhere along the line grasped onto the made-up (for a business) word “Broderbund” and linked it to the Browder name. This has no basis in reality at all. Broderbund isn’t even a surname, although some websites have lately begun to pick it up as if it is. (7) Not all Browders in America today are from Edmund Browder. But all of the earliest ones appear to have been. It also appears from records that his father’s name was probably George Browder. Regardless, there is no need to posit some variant spelling for Edmund’s father’s name because the name Browder already existed and even George Browder can be found in Irish records at a very early date – early enough to have been Edmund’s father. There are several other possible candidates too, all with the exact surname spelling “Browder”. So, there literally is no reason for anyone to mess up the Browder line by positing anything else. We may eventually figure out which Browder was Edmund’s father and take the line back by using actual records. Until then, I hope this statement will help to keep some others from perpetuating incorrect information. Thank you.

Thrasher, John, Sen to John Thrasher Jr., 17 Feb. 1788

OK, having found this deed on the internet yesterday, I quickly came to realize that I needed to make corrections and comments about it and post it so that others may have corrected information. Here is the text of said deed from Rockingham County, North Carolina Deed Book B, pp. 24-25.

“Thrasher, John, Sen to John Thrasher Jr.:

This Indentured made this Seventeenth Day of February in the Year of our Lord Christ Seventeen hundred and Eighty eight and in the Year of American Independence Between John Thrasher Sr. of Rockingham County and State of North Carolina of the One part and Joh. Thrasher Junr of the other part. Witnesseth that the Said John Thrasher Senr for and in consideration of the Sum of Two hundred pounds lawful money of state the Receipt where of both acknowledge, Granted sold confirmed unto John Thrasher Jun his Heirs and assigns forever a certain Tract or Parcel of Land Situate lying and being in Said County and State on the Waters of Lickfork, Hamblin Branch of Hogan’s Creek Being Part of a survey of Land 693 Acres, Granted by Lord Earl Granville to Said John Thrasher Senr and the upper end of Said Tract. Thence West one hundred and thirty two poles to a post oak. Thence South Crossing Said Creek, two hundred and Seventy [could be twenty] two poles to the first Station containing Two hundred and Twenty Acres of the same More or Less. To Have & To Hold and peaceably Enjoy the Said Two Hundred and Twenty Acres of Land, Together with all houses, orchards, woods, waters, mines and meadows and everything that appertains to said Lands and promises above mentioned and the Said John Thrasher Senr and Ruth his wife for Themselves their Heirs do Warrant and forever defend the Right title, claim and Interest of Said Lands ?? to him the Said John Thrasher Jun. his Heirs and assigns forever, And the Said John Thrasher Senr and Ruth Thrasher his wife do covenant promises and Agree to make the said John Thrasher Junr His Heirs or Assigns any further Deed Right or title that he or his Heirs assigns may Lawfully require.

In Presence of Us John Thrasher (Seal)

                                Her Mark

Jos C. Thrasher    Ruth Thrasher

     His Mark

Isaac Thrasher

William Bethell X

John Browder”

Now, the main correction is at the very end. The online source, which comes from a transcription done by someone at the LDS Church, shows Isham Browder as the signatory. However, Isham Browder, Sr. was deceased, having died in 1784, and Isham, Jr. was too young to have witnessed said deed. However, their relative, John Browder, still resided in the county and, therefore, was obviously the witness rather than Isham Browder. I have seen the writing on these deeds, and I can attest that it is difficult to tell an “I” from a “J”. So, this is an easy mistake to make. The names Isham and John look very similar in these documents. I can’t fault the transcriber for this, or for likely not knowing that Isham, Sr. was already deceased.

However, frankly, for an organization that takes such a store in genealogy, one might think that the LDS could hire, or have volunteers, who knew how to read the old writing better. I state this because of the other mistakes I had to correct in this deed. Several times the transcriber placed a “z” where an “s” should have been and made the mistake that only an amateur could make when they wrote “fs” when the correct spelling is actually “ss”. Yes, I am well aware of the fact that the way they wrote back then looks like “fs”. But that is exactly my point. I know it, so why don’t they?

There were also a few other minor errors which have been corrected here.

I will continue that, aside from mistakes that have been corrected, this corrected document also demonstrates that John Browder, whoever he really was (his relationship with Isham Browder is uncertain, but he was likely the son of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia even though family tradition states that he died as a boy) still resided in Rockingham County in 1788. He resided close to Talitha Browder, widow of Isham Browder, Sr. In fact, a document in which he witnessed another deed in 1795 shows that he continued to reside there at least that long. Whether he died (most likely) or moved on is not certainly known. He simply does not correlate with any known John Browder who lived during that time-period except for the son of Richard and Mary Browder.

All that being said, the main point here is that Isham Browder was deceased in 1788, so he did NOT witness this deed. John Browder did.

Browder Family Traced to Dublin, Ireland

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., MTS, M.Div.

In her book “Notes on the Browder Family of Tidewater, Virginia”, Blanche Penland Browder (Penland Browder, p. 3; Nugent, in “Cavaliers & Pioneers”, Vol. 2, p. 152) stated that, 

“The first occurrence of the name [in the American colonies] was in September of 1674 when a JOHN BROADAR was brought to Virginia by William Havercomb. Havercomb held a grant for 950 acres on the east side of the Elizabeth River in Lower Norfolk County, acquired for the transportation of eighteen persons (including the said JOHN BROADAR and self at fifty acres each – an allowance regardless of the relationship to the transporter, or even the number of times the same person was brought in).”

However, Blanche was mistaken. She did a great job, but simply did not have enough information. This is indeed the first extant record of anyone in the family having been brought over if we accept that he was a Browder and his name was misspelled (and I do). However, Helen Sides-Dye in her book “The Browder Connections” referred to records of a “Geo. Browder” who resided in Charles City County, Virginia as early as 1665 – possibly as early as 1655. Since Edmund Browder I was not born until about 1651 and since this George Browder had to have been an adult by 1665, the strong likelihood is that George Browder was the father of Edmund Browder I. In addition, records from Dublin, Ireland show a George Browder listed on Dec. 15, 1642 in the Court of Chancery Bill Books, 1627-1884 (vol. 2, p. 113). 

The fact that family tradition has always held that three Browder brothers sailed from Ireland to the colonies coupled with the fact that we find a George Browder in Dublin Ireland legal books who could easily be one and the same as the George Browder who is found in early Virginia records demonstrates the likelihood that George Browder was, in fact, at least among the first Browders to settle in Virginia and that he is the likely father of Edmund Browder I. There are other possible indications that I do not yet have enough evidence for. Suffice it to say for now that even though he was not a first son, Edmund Browder I did name a son George Andrew Browder. First sons were not ALWAYS named after the paternal grandfather, after all.

Parthenia “Theney” Browder-Bailey Never Existed, and Here’s Proof

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Jan. 1, 2025)

You know, one has to see it as really something when it becomes necessary to actually prove that someone never even existed. Frankly, to do so is more of a daunting task than one might imagine because one has to find a way to decipher relevant facts and figure out how anyone came up with this person in the first place. Then one has to take the evidence and present it properly, piece by piece. At least, that’s how I proceeded.

Now, the reason I felt it necessary to do this in the first place was not just because someone made a simple mistake. No, it is more than that. See, not only does this mistake continue to be perpetuated all over the internet even though I have spent the balance of three years trying to eliminate it, but in some instances, it is perpetuated to such a degree that it literally cries out for correction in that at least one person literally made up several siblings to go along with this non-existent person! Worse than that, this person connected “Theney” to my direct ancestral line. In effect, he created an entirely mythical family for one of my ancestral couples. I simply could not let that go. Of course, when contacted he denied that he had ever put said information up, which made it even worse. Even so, it took me a while to get into fully investigating it. But such is life.

So, let me break everything down so that it can be fully understood that Parthenia Browder never existed at all, period. Please keep in mind that, if she never existed, then she cannot be connected with any of the supposed family members that others have connected her with, including the up to ten siblings that one or two persons connected her with. And let me categorically state that no matter who she was – or wasn’t – she had absolutely NO relation whatsoever to any Isham and Martha Browder at all. There is, in fact, a connection to the Browder family here, but none of the above are that connection.

Let us begin with James Bailey. I have not been able to ascertain his birth or death dates, mainly because there is no tombstone for him anywhere. Absent any other reliable information to provide these dates, I have chosen not to make a guess. Regardless, James Bailey is the person that Parthenia “Theney” Browder is supposed to have married.

Now, James Bailey is supposed to have been buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery in Hopkins County, Kentucky. And I concur with that belief even though he does not have a tombstone to mark his grave. The reason being, first, that a James Bailey (Nov. 2, 1830-Sept. 20, 1835) is, in fact, buried there. Second, the tombstone of the person obviously mistaken for “Parthenia Browder” is also buried there. Thus, we do have a family unit here of three persons, James Bailey, Jr. along with his mother and father.

Now, the tombstone which is obviously attributed to “Parthenia Browder-Bailey” simply does not have that name on it. That tombstone actually reads “Permelia Bailey Born Jun. 18, 1806 Died Mar. 2, 1850 Aged 44 Years….” Now, several obvious things have happened here. First, her name was misspelled anyway on the tombstone and should have been “Parmelia” (but not “Parthenia”). One can ascertain this fact simply by noting that several other women buried in the same cemetery have the given name “Parmelia”. Regardless, again, her name was NOT “Parthenia”. Second, someone, it becomes obvious, had gone to this cemetery and tried to read this tombstone, coming up with the name “Parthenia”. Beyond that, this person also evidently got the dates on the tombstone wrong too, coming up with (May 13, 1827-Oct. 12, 1854). As can be seen by this picture, these dates are wrong.

You know, I have been doing this kind of research for a LONG time, and early on I understood how difficult it could be to read these old tombstones. It always pays to carry some chalk with you so that you can chalk over the face of the tombstone, blow the excess off, and then read it (no matter how many people say it’s bad for the tombstone, it isn’t). I haven’t visited any old cemeteries in a long time, but I still have chalk in my vehicle so that if I ever do, I can do that. It’s literally the best way to prevent such errors.

In any case, this supposed person, “Parthenia ‘Theney’ Browder-Bailey, May 13, 1827-Oct. 12, 1854” never even existed. Please, I implore anyone who has un-wittingly perpetrated the spread of this person, especially on the internet, to correct the information with what I will provide here. James Bailey married someone named Parmelia. And her dates were June 18, 1806-Mar. 2, 1850. That’s the first thing. OK?

The second thing is that she was NOT a Browder. How do I know this? Well, let’s parse it all out.

Yes, one would reasonably think that she might have been a Browder since she was so early buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery. So, I, in fact, looked into this possibility in a number of ways, including the truly far-fetched notion that some other Browder family might have arrived along with that of Isham and Rachel Browder. But, let me back up just a bit here for clarification. Browder’s Chapel and Cemetery were established by the Isham Browder who had been a Private during the Revolutionary War, in 1803. He, along with his wife, Rachel Slayden, and their family, had arrived in that area about that time. Rachel later died there and was buried in that cemetery. Many others over time have been too. So, remember that I mentioned that there was a Browder connection, but that it was not what others had drawn out. The connection is with this family even though Parmelia was not a Browder herself, at least by descent.

Here’s what happened. Isham and Rachel were married and first resided in Goochland County, Virginia. This, by the way, has caused some researchers to assume that Isham had been born in Goochland County, Virginia. I believe that this is an error and that he was instead born in Dinwiddie County, Virginia, but resided in Goochland County after the war. Regardless, this is where he married Rachel and they had their first four children. In 1786 Isham was awarded lands in Kentucky for his military service. But it wasn’t until about 1793 that he and his family migrated, first, to Lincoln County, Kentucky to settle on some of these land grants. Later, the family moved on to Christian County, Kentucky, to Henderson County, Kentucky, and then to Hopkins County, Kentucky. They were in Henderson County by 1799. By 1803 they were in Hopkins County.

Now, again, Parmelia was born on June 18, 1806. At first glance one might think that she could be a daughter of Isham and Rachel Browder and that other researchers had simply made a mistake, assuming that her mother was Martha. The problem with this is that the last child of Isham and Rachel Browder was David, who was born on June 6, 1806. I hope I don’t have to spell out for anyone why it is impossible for Parmelia to have been their daughter.

Well, then what about the first two sons of Isham and Rachel? Their first son, Thomas, was born in 1786 and their second son, Harbert Claiborne, was born in 1788. Obviously, both were old enough to have been the father of Parmelia. And, in fact, Thomas married Rebecca J. Harvey in 1806! Seems like an open and shut case at first glance, right? Problem is, the 1810 census of Hopkins County, Kentucky lists Thomas Browder, along with his wife and two male children. NO daughters. In fact, their sons were Thomas, Jr. and John. Thomas died in 1812 serving in the War of 1812. He had no further children.

OK, so what about Harbert Claiborne Browder? Here is where the rubber meets the road, as they say. I have known for years that he had married Henrietta G. Her maiden name was unknown to me. Neither was their marriage date (and I still don’t have that, but in the end, it doesn’t really matter when they were married as long as it was after a certain date).

Henrietta, as it turns out from further research, was Henrietta G. Slayden, daughter of John Slayden and Judith Garland. How they may have been related to Rachel I do not know. Regardless, she was born about 1792. On Jan. 30, 1806 John and Judith Slayden signed a document allowing their daughter, Henrietta, to marry James J. “Dozer”. There is a record that James Dozier and “Henry Slaton” were, in fact, married by Andrew Tribe in Madison County, Kentucky. On Sept. 20, 1833 John Slayden made his will in Henderson County, Kentucky. In it he refers to Henrietta as “my oldest daughter, Henrietta G. Browder, late Slaton”.

From the above several things become obvious. First, Henrietta G. Slayden married James J. Dozier in 1806. That is the same year that Parmelia was born. But then, why did her father call her Henrietta G. Browder? Well, as already shown, Harbert Claiborne Browder married someone named Henrietta G. Again, it doesn’t matter when they were married as long as it was after sometime in 1806 when Henrietta married James Dozier.I think I just have to say that we can see exactly why Henrietta was getting married to James Dozier at such a young age – about fourteen years old. Whatever then happened to him afterward, Henrietta later married Harbert Claiborne Browder. Therefore, Parmelia was a Dozier, not a Browder. She obviously became adopted by Harbert Claiborne Browder and was probably referred to as a Browder because of that, but she was not a Browder by descent.

Now, obtaining this information from whatever source it came from, I have always known that Harbert Claiborne Browder and Henrietta G. had one daughter, Judith G. Browder. However, I now have to modify that because her tombstone is, in fact, in the same cemetery. It becomes clear that her name instead was Judith Ann Browder. Her tombstone shows that she died on Oct. 8, 1847 at 33 years of age, making her birth year about 1814. And, also as the tombstone shows, she, in fact, had married a Slaton.

Previous to that, however, she is known to have married Beverly Daniel Key (June 6, 1805-Apr. 28, 1843).

Harbert Claiborne Browder himself died in 1815. One reasonably has to assume that he and Henrietta had been married about 1813/14 and then had Judith about 1814. Then Harbert died a year afterward. They had no further children. Daniel Slaton, presumably Henrietta’s brother, was administrator of Harbert’s estate.

Now, even though we have cleared all of this up, I want to go a little further. There was, in fact, a Parthenia Browder and she is buried in Browder’s Chapel Cemetery. I must stress here that she is the ONLY Parthenia Browder I have ever found anywhere, period. See, Isham and Rachel Browder had more children than just Thomas and Harbert. Their third son was John. John Browder married Nancy H. Allen. One of their sons was James Monroe Browder (b. c1823). James married Parthenia McIntire. I have not been able to ascertain where her birth date of May 13, 1827 came from, but at least it is plausible. However, her death date of Oct. 12, 1854 is accurate according to Hopkins County, Kentucky death notices. Sadly, she, along with her daughter, Rachel, both died of the flux (dysentery) on that date.  James Monroe Browder moved on to Texas after that and remarried. 

Finally, Parthenia Browder with dates 1760-Mar. 2, 1850 is also completely mythical. Someone else made this up.

So, in the end we can see that the only “Parthenia Browder” was Parthenia McIntire, married to James Monroe Browder. We also see that James Bailey, Sr. married Parmelia Dozier, daughter of James J. Dozier and Henrietta G. Slayden, who had been adopted by Harbert Claiborne Browder when he married her mother. Finally, it is obvious both by her tombstone and by her father’s will that Henrietta remarried to a Slaton sometime after the death of Harbert Claiborne Browder. Whether she had any further children, I do not know.

The Ancestry of Isham Browder (1762-1830) Possibly Solved At Last

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div.

Most, if not all, researchers into the Browder family ancestry know that the mystery of the parentage of Isham Browder (1762-1830) who fought during the Revolutionary War and later moved to Kentucky has been a mystery for generations. Anyone who ever knew who his parents were has long since passed on. In fact, the parentage of my own Isham Browder (c1732-1784) was also a mystery for generations until I solved it back in the 1980s.

I, in fact, have made diligent efforts over the years to solve this one remaining mystery and have put forth educated guesses on the matter. My best guess, until now, was that Isham’s father was Frederick Browder. Today I wholeheartedly rescind that guess because it has become obvious to me after looking at things in a different way that Frederick was not his father. That, although it is still possible that Frederick did have children that are unknown to us today.

I have always maintained that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was a son of one of the sons of John and Elizabeth Browder because that has to be the case. The only question was which one. Frankly, with the proliferation of Browders with the given name of Thomas combined with the fact that Isham named a son Thomas as did all of his male children, one would want to conclude that Isham’s father had to have been named Thomas. But I have done genealogy long enough to know that to employ logic when trying to understand how children were named is futile. As much as we would like to think that logic might have been utilized, it wasn’t. People often named their children after ancestors or even themselves, but not always.

All that said, there is, again, a tendency to name children after ancestors of other close relatives. So, as in the case of my ancestor, Isham Browder (c1732-1784), his name (as I found through documentation) was actually Thomas Isham Browder, he being named after his grandfather, Lt. Col. Thomas Randolph, and his mother, Mary Isham Randolph. But neither he, not any of his close progeny, named any children Thomas.

So, which son of John and Elizabeth Browder is Isham Browder (1762-1830) likely to have come from. We can eliminate Arthur altogether since he died about 1738, and we now know who his descendants were. He simply wasn’t one of them. Even though both Edmund (b. c1716) and John (b. c1717) are slight possibilities, they both seem unlikely after all. Again, he certainly wasn’t a descendant of Richard (c1719-c1807) and Mary. The known children of Joseph (1741-1818) does not show an Isham. The known children of George (1731-c1819) DOES list an Isham, but he is not the same person. He was born about 1766 and died in 1852 and this is documented. That simply leaves William (1723-c1763) as the most likely possibility.

William, son of John and Elizabeth Browder, married Margaret about 1742. Margaret’s maiden name is unknown. But, their known children were Margaret (b. c1742), Susannah (b. c1745), William (c1755-c1820), and Mary (b. c1760). Mary, as I discovered, married someone from the Lucy family. I have actually not investigated that much further but do think that it is just possible that she married Isham Lucy. William Browder (c1755-c1820) had the sons Jeremiah, Isham, Thomas, William, and James.

Now, how does all of this have anything to do with Isham Browder (1762-1830)?

Excerpt from Brunswick County Deed Book 6, p. 323: “Indenture made the 28th day of November, 1758, between William Browder and William House, for 26 [pounds], conveying 100 acres, adjoining lands of Isham Lucy, Robert Gee, and Benjamin Wheler. Witnesses: Robert Gee, James Procter, and Henry Gee. Acknowledged on February 27, 1759 by William Browder.”

Excerpt from Brunswick Bounty Deed Book 6, p. 439: “Indenture made the 8th day of August, 1759, between Randle Daniel and William House, for 50 [pounds], conveying 538 acres, adjoining lands of Peter Daniel, Crablouse Creek, Francis Lett, and Kimble. Witnesses: William Browder, Joseph Cannon, and Hugh Edwards. Acknowledged: March 24, 1760 by Randle Daniel.”

If you didn’t catch it yet, don’t feel badly because I didn’t for a long time either. But once you see it, it will be so obvious to you that you won’t be able to deny it, I think.

The first thing to ask here is, if George, whose wife was not of the Isham family, would name a son Isham and all three of his sons also had sons named Isham, why would William not have a son named Isham if he was in any way related to or closely acquainted with Isham Lucy, as one may presume he might have been. After all, he had a daughter who appears to have married into that family. Again, logic doesn’t really get us there. But circumstantial evidence does. The proliferation of the Isham name among descendants of George Browder PLUS their strong ties to Methodism, when it is also obvious that William and George were close brothers, leads us in the direction of at least assuming William might have had a son named Isham too. And if he did, it would undoubtedly be the Isham Browder (1762-1830) of the Revolution. Why? Exactly because of what I had been missing in reading these documents all this time, until now.

I have maintained for years that Isham Browder (1762-1830) FIRST married Frances Daniel. There is a record of an Isham Browder marrying Frances Daniel on December 2, 1782 in Pittsylvania County, Virginia. I have maintained that it is this Isham Browder, who is the only one who fits, to have been the one found in this record. Obviously, then, Frances died sometime after their marriage and the union produced no living children. She must have been a daughter or close relative of one of the Daniel men mentioned in the above deed excerpts. Knowing that, it is practically inconceivable that William Browder did not have a son named Isham who married Frances Daniel. That it was THIS Isham Browder (1762-1830) is demonstrated, of not completely proven, by the obvious connection to Methodism because this Isham Browder became a Methodist lay minister and even founded a Methodist church.

So, in my mind it just becomes obvious that Isham Browder (1762-1830) was a son of William Browder (July 7, 1723-c1763) and Margaret. The whole story becomes bittersweet when one realizes that William died about a year or less after Isham was born. On top of this, Isham’s first wife also died soon after their marriage. What may have happened to Margaret, his mother, is unknown but later court testimony would show that she lived until at least 1780. Whether she remarried or not is unknown.

It now seems obvious why early researchers mixed up the information about Isham Browder (c1732-1784) and Isham Browder (1762-1830). It has long been clear to me that, when Arthur Browder died about 1738, his brother, Richard, took his son, Isham (c1732-1784) in and brought him up. I have detailed the reasons why this would have been the case previously and won’t go into that detail here. Suffice it to be said that a very close family relationship continued – so much so that Richard’s son, John, went to Guilford County, North Carolina, along with Isham and his wife, Talitha and so much so that Isham and Talitha’s son, Isham C. Browder, married Martha, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder.

There is no real record of Isham Browder (1762-1830) prior to his enlistment during the Revolutionary War as a teenager. And there isn’t much after his discharge prior to his move to Kentucky. Both Ishams, interestingly, had children with similar names, but that’s where that correlation ends because the name similarities do not stem from common sources. It’s just interesting. The name “Isham” came into each family line from different sources too, obviously. But it was there, nevertheless. Both served during the Revolution but held different ranks and had different outcomes. Memories of both, again, became conflated with time. I feel that Isham Browder (1762-1830) struck out and joined the Revolution at such an early age and he was determined to make something of himself as early as he could.

It is interesting to note that Mary Lucy was allowed a share in the division of Jones Browder’s property even though, as the documentation states, she was not technically due anything. This was done by agreement of all parties, but no reason is given. Apparently neither Isham, nor William, Jr. asked to be included and, frankly, William was deceased by that time anyway.

Isham Browder (1762-1830), it now seems clear, was a son of William, Sr. (1723-c1763) and Margaret Browder. I will venture to say that it is also likely that Jesse Browder (1758-1783), who married his cousin, Mary Thompson Browder (c1762-p, 1840), daughter of Richard and Mary Browder, was also a son of William and Margaret Browder, he perhaps also having been brought up by Richard and Mary Browder. It is possible.

And I will add Charles Browder to this list even though it has long been held that he was a son of Richard and Mary Browder.

What is NOT true or accurate is the following, found on some other genealogy sites – “Isham had 7 siblings: Mary Thompson Browder, Parthenia Theny Bailey (born Browder) and 5 other siblings” and/or that this Isham Browder was a son of an Isham and Martha Browder. Both are completely incorrect, started by someone on ancestry.com and copied onto some other genealogy sites.

False Browder Ancestry

I am SO SICK OF seeing incorrect information about my family surname on ancestry!!! It’s like trying to kill the Hydra! The first thing that I have tried so hard to get corrected is the following completely false and fictitious definition/explanation for our surname: “Americanized form of Dutch Brouder ‘brother’ or of German Broder”. THERE IS NOTHING AMERICANIZED ABOUT OUR SURNAME BECAUSE IT EXISTED BEFORE ANY MEMBER OF THE FAMILY CAME TO AMERICA!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!! Ancestry even has a good bit of information on people who went by our surname back in Britain and they STILL have this false definition up! In addition, as I have shown, our surname originates from the term “Browderer”, which was also used briefly as a surname until it was shortened to Browder. It’s really too easy! And getting that fictitious “Broderbund” linkage off of ancestry and the rest of the internet is just impossible I guess. I have had people take it down from their trees only to still see it spread elsewhere. Whoever came up with this has done a great disservice to our family name.

Finally, every time I look I find practically every ancestor married to yet someone else, with nothing to base it on. Right now, someone even has our Edmund Browder married to a Richard Jones, who is somehow a female. Not quite sure how that works out. Another one has him married to an Elizabeth Jones, which is also false. Still another one has him married to a Mary Jones and to an Elizabeth Jordan, also false. I would ask where people come up with this crap, but I already know. Ancestry’s algorithms cause this kind of mess with their “hints”. Correcting Hopeless Errors Concerning Browder Family Genealogy:

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Jan. 1, 2025)

Sadly, I have never seen more errors – often very easily corrected ones – perpetuated with reference to any family more so than my own Browder family. Why this is is, in part, a mystery. It does seem that some researchers are not really interested in accuracy. But the main culprit that I have found is the website, ancestry.com, itself. Their hints regarding any family are almost always wrong. Yet many accept them without looking further, even as they gather documentation that actually refutes the link they have made. In so many words, they never read it. I have decided here to point out a few of these errors in the hope that other researchers will take this information and not perpetuate earlier mistakes in the future.

1. The very first and foremost mistake that has been perpetuated and shared endlessly for many, many years – even well before the internet – is that Isham Browder (1762-1830) who moved to Kentucky from Virginia was either the son of Isham and Martha Browder or the son of Isham and Talitha Browder. Both are false. Isham Browder (c1732-1784) and Talitha Cox (c1745-1828) did have a son named Isham. However, he was NOT the guy who went to Kentucky. He lived all of his life in (except for a brief period in Virginia) and died in, Rockingham County, North Carolina. He never even went to Kentucky, period. However, he did indeed marry a lady named Martha and some try to posit that they are the parents of the Kentucky Isham Browder. Too bad Isham Browder, who married his cousin, Martha Browder, wasn’t born until about 1774. That would make it pretty difficult for him to have a son born in 1762, right? For those who wonder – there were no other Isham and Martha Browder combinations, period. These generations are extremely well-documented. So, contrary to earlier times when such mistakes could be accepted, there really is no excuse for making this one anymore. Sadly, no one is certain who Isham Browder’s (1762-1830) parents were although I believe I have that figured out and have posted it.

2. Another error that continues to be perpetuated from earlier research is the idea that the Browder surname is in any way Irish or in any way related to the Broderick family (along with the Broderick family Coat of Arms). There is absolutely no connection between these families and the Browder surname did not start in Ireland. Research that I have been able to conduct in recent years demonstrates that (1) the Browder surname originated in England among Flemish immigrants and (2) that it derives from the term browderer. Sometimes the term was shortened into the form “broderer”. Thus, those who today have the surname Browder descend from those who used the full term “browderer” and those with the surname Broder descend from those who used the shortened term “broderer”. Both simply dropped the extra “er” from the name’s ending. It’s that simple. Our family (the ones who first came to the American colonies during the 1600s) probably did sail from Ireland because they evidently resided there for a generation or two. But that did not make them Irish.

3. Here’s a relatively recent error. How this actually came about, I am unsure. But, frankly, it originated with shoddy research, however it was done. The idea that a James Bailey married a Parthenia Browder. Birth dates for Mr. Bailey run the gamut, as one might expect, on the internet. As for the supposed Parthenia Browder, her birth date generally lines up with that of Isham Browder (1762-1830). In fact, many connect them both as brother and sister. Sadly, what I have been able to find out is that they also assume that the Parthenia Browder who is buried in Browder cemetery in Hopkins County, Kentucky is the supposed Parthenia Browder who supposedly married James Bailey. Problem is, that is simply not the case, and it is not possible. One might think that a least someone would have noticed that she is not referred to as Parthenia Bailey. In any case, Parthenia Browder, again, is buried in Browder Cemetery. Her dates are 13 May 1827-12 Oct 1854. 1827 is a LONG way from 1762! In addition, the fact of the matter is that Parthania was married to James Monroe Browder, who was a GRANDSON of Isham Browder (1762-1830). Furthermore, (1) There is no record of a James Bailey marrying a Parthenia Browder, or any Browder, for that matter. In fact, there is no marriage record for him at all. (2) No, she is NOT the daughter of Isham and Martha Browder either, as some posit. SHE IS NOT! (3) No, she was NOT the daughter of Isham Browder (b. 1732) either! All of this is complete fantasy, probably stemming from a misunderstanding of who Parthenia Browder, who married James Monroe Browder, really was. It’s still unfathomable, though.

4. The idea that Edmund Browder (c1651-c1739) married an Elizabeth “Lysbeth” Simpson is another new and perplexing error, which the documentation actually shows cannot be correct. Some  even have a Lysbeth Simpson  as marrying an “unknown”, but having Edmund’s children, except for John. Here are the problems with the documentation provided for it. (1) There is no necessary connection between the Lysbeth Simpson they cite to any of the materials they provide. If she was even real, they provide no documentation for this. (2) The Elizabeth Wales document and the Elizabeth Browder document they sometimes provide together do not go together at all. First, Billingshurst, shown in one document as a birthplace, is not the same as London, shown in the other document. Second, one document is from 1668 while the other is from 1804. Further (3) they sometimes have the parents of Lysbeth Simpson in yet another document as being Edward and Hannah Wales, not Simpson. None of this has any connection to Edmund Browder in any record shown, nor any record I have ever seen. The Andalucia Star-News article entitled, “Brower family descendants were avid supporters of education”, by Curtis Thomasson (April 6, 2019), is full of fatal errors on this matter. The author, when queried about it, stated that he had just got his information from ancestry and really had not done any research himself.

And, by the way, some of these researchers claim that one of their children was named Catherine. Sadly, there is no historical record for this. They also posit that Catherine was born in New York. Our Edmund Browder never lived in New York. 

5. It has long been understood that John Browder (b. c1686), son of Edmund Browder and Elizabeth, married a lady named Elizabeth, whom it was somehow remembered had been Welsh. She was termed a “Welsh lady”. That much, according to long-held family tradition, is considered true and is held as such by researchers. I also hold this as true. The problem, however, is that, apparently, someone along the line got the bright idea that, because she was Welsh, then her maiden name must have been Wales. I’m not even kidding here! Look, if someone’s “research” is this shoddy, then they don’t need to be doing research. They just straight-up made this up, and other people have accepted it so much that it is almost impossible to eradicate as an idea today. Even worse than this; those who have accepted correction here have sometimes moved to, instead, make the wife of Edmund Browder to have been Elizabeth Wales! Now, I know exactly how the latter happen because I have seen such happen with hints directed toward me. What happened here is that, once someone made the correction that John’s wife was, in fact, Elizabeth Jones (this is what my careful research has shown to be the case), a “hint” came up indicating that Edmund’s wife was Elizabeth Wales. I kid you not – that is exactly the type of thing that happens on ancestry because of the way their system functions. And, naturally, some people will simply accept these hints. Of course, there is absolutely nothing in any record or even in family tradition that Edmund married a Welsh lady, so the connection is tenuous from the start just because of that. Again, Edmund did indeed marry Elizabeth, but her maiden name has been unknown for generations. I had believed for some years that it was likely Bolling, but could have been another name instead. I never said it absolutely was Bolling and, thankfully, not many people followed me in this belief. The point being that even the careful researcher can sometimes be wrong. The reason I can state this is that, more careful recent research has revealed to me that she was, instead, Elizabeth Parsons, daughter of Joseph Parsons. I’m not going to explain why here since I have done so in detail elsewhere. Regardless, neither John, nor Edmund, married anyone with the maiden name of Wales, period. That is completely made-up and, therefore, incorrect. Let me just add here; please don’t just blindly accept any hints from ancestry! Please!

6. When on ancestry, one will find the parentage of Edmund Browder (b. c1651) as being various persons born in Germany. By whatever name they are given, this is completely false. Once again, (1) the Browder surname originated in England and (2) these connections also stem from ancestry “hints”. Any connection to Germany, if there is one, would have to be much further back than Edmund’s parents. Trust me. The surname Browder can be traced as far back as at least the 1400s in England alone, with Browderer being used before that as a surname. 

7. There is NO surname “Broderbund! It doesn’t exist! Neither Edmund, nor John, nor Arthur Browder ever went by “Broderbund”, period. So, where we find others using this term as some alternate surname for any early Browder family members, they have made an error. Please do NOT accept this word as any alternate or variant of our surname! Please! And, by the way, John Browder (b. c1686) did not have any known daughter named Jane.

8. Let’s get this straight before someone messes this up. Edmund Browder (c1651-c1739) was often mistakenly referred to as Edward in records of the time. So, if any researcher sees these records and concludes that they were two different people, they will be in error. The records are clearly referring to one and the same person. 

9. Isham Browder (c1732-1784) was a Captain in the NC militia during the Revolutionary War. Isham Browder (1762-1830) served in the war as a Private and never rose in rank. He was wounded in battle and discharged as a Private. I have actually seen a newspaper article about the latter Isham Browder referring to him as a Captain. This is false. The two were being mixed up again, somehow.

10. Just for emphasis, I will repeat what I have stated elsewhere. I am the one who came up with the idea that Arthur Browder (1715-c1738) eloped with and married Mary Isham Randolph. I came up with this connection way back in 1983/84 by doing research using BOOKS and other materials. There was no such thing as the internet way back then. I know that is hard for some people to fathom these days – a world without the internet. How in the world would one even do research?! But this is exactly part of the problem – the idea that people think they can just get on ancestry or some other genealogy website and be fed their family information. It just doesn’t work that way.

11. That said, many people have accepted my connection here, some, I think, without even checking it. Be that as it may, I still hold to that connection because there is absolutely no evidence that I have been able to find for anything else. But I do NOT claim to have proven it, only that I have all-but proven it. 

12. That said, I have seen other so-called “researchers” posit as many as five or six or even more children emanating from this union. The problem with that is simple, for those who are even vaguely familiar with the story. Mary Isham Randolph eloped with an unnamed plantation overseer. Her family were opposed to this union. Evidently, some kind of altercation took place. Afterward, Mary was led to believe that her husband had been killed, along with her infant child. Her family told her that they were dead. She only had ONE child with this man, not five or ten! Not many years later, she remarried to the Rev. James Keith and had several children with him. These children’s birth dates, by the way, overlap the birth dates of the supposed other children that Arthur and Mary might have had, if they had remained together. Anyone who knows anything about the story would see the error here. Sadly, most don’t know the story. In any case, it is strictly impossible that Arthur and Mary could have had more than one child, period. They were separated while their first child was still an infant, and they didn’t have any more. This all happened in 1732, according to documentation. Conversely, Arthur Browder died about 1738, leaving an unnamed infant heir, according to the Bristol Parish Vestry Book. Afterward, it is known that Arthur’s brother, Richard of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, brought up the child within his own household. The first record that still exists shows Isham Browder as being of adult age during a year which would make his birth date no later than 1735. But, as any researcher knows, records are sparse because of wars and the deterioration of time. So, this may well have not been the first record tha mentioned Isham. It’s just the first one that still exists today. Various other records do prove that Isham Browder was, in fact, the son of Arthur Browder. What they don’t show is who his mother was. But, frankly, there is no other scenario that fits. This was Capt. Isham Browder who married Talitha Cox.

13. The first son of Isham Browder and Talitha Cox was Isham C. Browder (c1774-1832). Records prove that his middle initial was “C”. It probably stood for Coleman, his grandmother’s (on his mother’s side) maiden name. But, nothing proves that. He is the one who married his cousin, Martha Browder, daughter of Richard and Mary Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Isham’s second name was NOT John, as some people keep perpetuating. This was a mistake I made early on in my research because I read a document incorrectly. Telling maybe 2-3 people has led to it being perpetuated all over the internet!!! Some apparently are unwilling to change this error to this day, even in the face of proof of his middle initial! All I can say is that if I make an error, I am willing to correct it.

14. Isham and Martha Browder had three children who lived, at the most. Censuses show that they had two boys and one girl. Neither of the boys, by the way, was Isham Browder (1762-1830). Did I really need to say that again? We know who two of the children were. The girl was named Mary “Polly” Thompson Browder (1800-1844), who later married William J. Meador (often mistakenly referred to as “Meadows” in various records). The known boy was my ancestor, Isham Richard Browder (c1801-1885) who later moved to McNairy County, Tennessee. The other boy, whose name is not known, lived to maturity but apparently died soon thereafter with no descendants.

This is as far as I want to go with it for now.
