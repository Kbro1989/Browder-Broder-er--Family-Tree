Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth, and Their Earliest Descendants

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 5, 2019December 28, 2025Posted inUncategorized

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Dec. 28, 2025)

Edmund Browder I (c1652-c1739) is believed to have possibly been born in Wales or Ireland.  However, some other family researchers state that he was born in 1670 in Charles City (later Prince George) County, Virginia.  I do not know what the source of this latter information may be and cannot completely confirm it.  However, I have come to favor the probability, based on extant evidence, that he was, in fact, born at or close to Jamestown in James City County, Virginia.  His father was, most likely, George Browder (c1624), who can be found in Dublin, Ireland records and (Helen Sides-Dye states) some early Virginia records.  Edmund was a very common given name in Ireland, so descent from George Browder of Ireland is at least possible, though unproven. 

Edmund Browder I is known to have died about 1738/39, probably at home in Prince George County, Virginia, although Jamestown, Virginia (the town no longer existed for all practical purposes by that time) has been suggested, at about 70 years of age. 

During his lifetime, he came to be called Edmund Browder, Sr. because he had a son named Edmund W. Browder, who was often referred to as Edmund Browder, Jr.  Edmund’s Will was probated in 1739, but this Will has apparently not survived.  The Will of Edmund Browder is known to have been proven in court at Fitzgerald’s on February 12, 1739/40 by Theophilus Field, one of the witnesses to Edmund’s Will, according to a minute book fragment of a court record from Prince George County, which has, fortunately, survived (Prince George County Minute Book [PGCMB], 1737-1792, p. 387). And since it is obvious that Edmund Browder knew Theophilus Field well, and was also a merchant himself, it is interesting to note the following excerpt of a Bond.  Witnesses to this Bond were Ninian Boog, Robert Miller, William Hodsden, and Robert Jones, Jr.

Robert Donald of Hanover County, Merchant, indebted to Theophilus Field of Prince George County, Merchant, for £43, that whereas Robert Donald by virtue of a power given him by James Donald of North Britain, Merchant, agreed to sell to Theophilus Field of a lot containing half an acre in the town of Petersburg in Dinwiddie County, and  numbered  in  the  plan  of the said Town #33, and have received the sum of £ 21, 10 shillings, Robert Donald shall cause and procure James Donald to convey to Theophilus Field the lot and half acre within 18 months, then obligation to be void (Brunswick County Deed Book [BCDB] 7, p. 114).

Another indenture made concerning Theophilus Field reads as follows, the witnesses being Gray Briggs, John Thornton, James Walker and Theophilus Field, Jr. and proven in court on June 28, 1762:

Indenture made the 25th day of January, 1762, between Theophilus Field of Prince George County, and John Sims, for £200, conveying 275 acres on North side of the Little Creek, adjoining land of Ralph Jackson, and purchased of Thomas Jones and Anne, his wife, by two deeds, the first dated the 3rd day of March, 1748, and the other dated the 6th day of October, 1748 (BCDB 7, p. 122).

Theophilus Field was not only a merchant, but was also a Justice, a Vestryman, and one of the founders of the original Blanford Church in Bristol Parish, Virginia.  And he was a tobacco planter, having his own fleet of ships, which delivered tobacco to England.  He is interred in the chancel floor of Blanford Church in Present-day Petersburg. 

Here the reader should also begin to see that there is already a connection with the Jones families of Virginia, something that will be brought out in more detail in other posts. 

Elizabeth, wife of Edmund Browder I, was born, about 1653, probably in Virginia.  Records indicate that she died about 1721/22 in Prince George (later Dinwiddie) County, Virginia.  It cannot be determined at this time where they were married as no marriage record has, to date, been found.  But if Elizabeth was born in Virginia, then they were likely married in Charles City County, Virginia where they afterward resided. It is my firm belief that Edmund Browder I married Elizabeth Parsons, a probable daughter of Joseph Parsons. 

In any case, as the records indicate, Edmund Browder I resided in Surry County, Virginia at least by 1682, since he paid taxes in Surry County, Virginia for the years 1682 and 1683 (Surry County Tithables, 1680, 1681, 1682, p. 57; Surry County Tithables 1683, 1684, 1685, pp. 39 & 43). 

The Edmund Browder I family later resided on the waters of Gravelly Run (at old Petersburg, now within modern-day Petersburg) about ten miles west of Blanford (which was later absorbed by Petersburg).  They resided near the villages of Sutherland and Church Road.  This Tidewater region of Virginia is the same area over which Chief Powhatan, father of Pocahontas, had ruled prior to, and well into, the time of the first English colonists, who first resided at what became Jamestown. 

Also, Petersburg was established from what had originally been Peter Jones’ Trading Station, located along the Appomattox River.  This trading station was established prior to 1675.  It was the foremost part of Southern Virginia for trade with the Native Americans during that time-period.  It later became known as Peter’s Point.  From this, the original settlement of Petersburg began in 1738. 

The following indenture concerning Peter Jones, Jr., son of Maj. Peter Jones and Dorothy Chamberlayne, is of interest here, the significance of which will be brought out later.  Witnesses were Benjamin Jones, Richard Jones and David Walker.  It was proven in open court on January 25, 1762. 

Indenture made the 14th day of November, 1761, between William King of Elizabeth City County, and Peter Jones, for £200, conveying 630 acres, 230 acres of which being part of a greater tract formerly granted to Peter Jones by Patent dated November 2, 1726 (MDCCXXVI) and conveyed by deed from the said Jones to Wm. Wynne, and the other 400 acres was granted to the said William Wynne by Patent dated February 1, 1738 (MDCCXXXVIII), and conveyed from the said Wynne by deed to Fredrick Jones and by Fredrick Jones conveyed to William King (BCDB 7/Brunswick County Patent Book [BCPB] 13, p. 38). 

As will be brought out later, of interest is the fact that the area that became known as “Peter’s Point” had at first been the southernmost point of English settlement. There, Ft. Henry had been established upon an island below the falls of the Appomattox River. Ft. Henry was first commanded by Maj. Gen. Abraham Wood, whose daughter, Margaret, married Maj. Peter Jones. Following the death of Abraham Wood, his son-in-law, Peter Jones, took command of Ft. Henry. That is the area that later became known as “Peter’s Point”.

Now, according to court documents, there can be no doubt that Edmund was sometimes mistakenly referred to as “Edward”, as all documents are obviously referring to the same person.  “Edmo. Browder” appears on a list of Tithables in Surry County, Virginia, taken on June 10, 1682 (Va. Gen, XXII, Vol. 4).  “Edwd. Browder” was listed on a list of Tithables for the same county, Lawne’s Creek Parish, on June 12, 1683 (ibid, XXIII, Vol. 1).  And “Edmond Browder” was listed in the same county and parish on another list of Tithables (ibid, XXIV, Vol. 1).  Again, the first entry is for 1682. 

In addition, a list of persons owing debts to the estate of Thomas Jordan, deceased, made by his administrators, Lt. Col. William Browne and James Jordan, on March 1, 1686, lists “Edward Browder”, as well as Thomas Binns and others (Hopkins, p. 10).  And in 1693, judgment was granted by Surry County Court to Christopher Bly against Joseph Rogers for certain articles of clothing which, apparently, Rogers was unwilling to hand over to Bly.  Bly states that he had one pair of the French fall shoes on the account of Edmund Browder (Haun, p. 33).  The judgment reads as follows:

Judgmt. is granted Christopher Bly agt. Joseph Rogers for a serge suite of Cloaths, a sett of Tanners working Geere & three pr. of mens French fall shoes (one pr. onely in difference being by the said Rogers put to the oath of the said Bly who declared that he had them on the accot. of EDMUND BROWDER) with Costs als Excn. (Haun, p. 33). 

And, a lawsuit David Crawley Vs Edward Browder was dismissed in 1719 (Va. Gen. Soc. Qtly., Nov. 1986, p. 58).  It is unknown to me what this lawsuit was about.  Again, it can’t be proven only from this, but it is entirely possible that Edmund Browder I himself was a merchant, perhaps in association with Theophilus Field, who witnessed his Will.  Or he may have been in business even with Peter Jones himself. 

Edmund Browder I is also mentioned in a court order, dated February 6, 1693, as a defendant in a lawsuit in which the Sherr [Sheriff] was ordered to pay a fine of 630 pounds of tobacco because Edmund failed to appear in court on a prior date.  The suit had been one brought against Edmund Browder by Charles Goodrich.  [Col.] Robert Bolling is referred to as the “High Sherr” in the court document, dated April 5, 1693, in which judgment was granted to Charles Goodrich against Col. Robert Bolling because “Edward” Browder failed to appear in said court.  Edmund Browder had apparently, then, been ordered arrested, and this arrest was to be carried out by Col. Robert Bolling, at which time this judgment was to be voided and the payment to be made out of Mr. Browder’s estate.  However, Bolling failed to arrest Browder, so an attachment against Bolling for 630 pounds of tobacco was therefore levied (Charles City County Orders 1687-1695, p. 478).  

The real question here becomes, why did Col. Robert Bolling refuse or neglect to arrest Edmund Browder?  I submit that it is likely because they were relatives.  As far as records are concerned, Edmund Browder was never actually arrested at all.  His presumed father-in-law, for whatever reason, had tactfully neglected to arrest him. 

In any case, by June 3, 1695, payment had been made by Edmund Browder of twenty-four shillings to Charles Goodrich so that the previous judgment was vacated on said date.  It probably took Edmund that long to be able to pay this debt, which he could not have done at all if he had been languishing in jail.  The court later dismissed the suit altogether, and placed judgment against Charles Culliford, whom Goodrich had represented and who also failed to appear in court, for twenty-four shillings (ibid 1687-1695, pp. 488 & 574).  So, Edmund Browder effectively won this lawsuit. 

The one remaining question is just exactly what prompted this court proceeding in the first place.  It certainly does not seem to be an auspicious way to begin a new life in a new world!  But it does seem to have set an unfortunate precedent for later generations.  The answer to this question is, again, unknown.  

Edmund Browder I is also mentioned in the quit rent rolls of Prince George (previously Charles City) County, Virginia in 1704, at which time he rented 100 acres of land (des Cognets, p. 222).  This is the only complete colonial tax ledger which is still extant, and it is the earliest record of Edmund Browder which some previous researchers had obtained access to.  Payments of rents were made by the renter to the King of England since the land was granted by the King.  At that time, the later to be formed county of Dinwiddie existed within the boundaries of Prince George County.  Thus, the Browder family did not move during later generations, but instead remained within the same boundaries, which originally were in Charles City County and later in Prince George County and, finally, formed into Dinwiddie County.  Richard Bland (1,000 acres) and Col. Robert Bolling (3,402 acres) are also mentioned as renters for 1704 in Prince George County (Virginia Historical Magazine, pp. 328-29). 

Also in 1704, the county of Prince George compensated Edmund Browder for killing a wolf, by awarding him 100 pounds of tobacco (Prince George County Court Orders [PGCCO] 1714-1720). 

Now, the circumstances under which Edmund obtained some of his first, non-rented, land holdings are detailed in the following scenarios.  This information is very important, as will be seen. 

May 6, 1711 – Edmund Browder of Bristol Parish, Prince George County, Virginia purchases 129 acres of the land that had been previously patented to Henry Randolph II, James Cocke, John Golightly, and Solomon Crook for 647 acres of land at a place called in the patent, “ye Second Swamp” This property bounded lands still owned by James Cocke, William Mayes, John Golightly, and Solomon Crook. Witnesses were William Anderson (son of Reynard Anderson) and Edmund Chamberlayne. Deed was recorded on May 8, 1711 (PGC Wills & Deeds Book, 1710-1713, p. 53).

“Henry Randolph of Bristol Parish, Henrico Co, to Edm’d Browder of Bristol Parish, Prince George Co, for £5, a tract, part of a greater one granted to Mr Henry Randolph, dec’d, James Cocke, William Mayes, John Golightly & Solomon Crooke by patent 19 April 1690, being in Bristol Parish and bounded by lands of William Mayes and Solomon Crooke, 129 acres.

Signed: Henry Randolph

Wit: Wm. Anderson, Edm. Chamberlayne

Recorded 8 May 1711”

This appears, according to the records, to be the first land purchase made by Edmund Browder. Previously he rented 100 acres, but there is no indication that he purchased that land. It is telling that his very first known land purchase was from Henry Randolph III, whose mother was Sarah Swann. Henry Randolph III was selling his part of the original patent to Edmund Browder.

Also, an early record of interest having to do with Edmund Browder I is to be found Boddie, Historical Southern Families, Vol. 1, “Prince George County Orders and Returns of Executions, 1714-1720”, pp. 166-67 (p. 197, pt. 2): 

On petition of Hugh Lee, it is ordered that James Baugh be overseer of the Nottoway River Road from John Coleman’s on Appomattox River, to the South end of Blackwater Bridge, and that the male labouring titheable persons of Capt. James Thweatt, Henry Thweatt, John Thweatt, David Crawley, Thomas Gregory, Edward Burchett, James Anderson, EDMOND BROWDER, David Vaughn & Thomas Spain assist in clearing the said road, 8/12/1718. 

On September 7, 1719, James Anderson of Prince George County, Virginia (son of James Anderson and grandson of Reynard Anderson) deeded 200 acres of land to Richard Cureton, located in Bristol Parish, which land was bounded by that of “Ed. Browder” and “ed. Burchett”.  Witnesses were James Thweatt and James Thweatt, Jr. (PGCDB 1713-1728 Part II, p. 349).  Then, on February 13, 1720/21 (PGCDB III, 1711-1724, p. 446), Edmund Browder I sold the land he purchased from Henry Randolph III to John Philips (PGCW&D 1710-1713, p. 446). 

Edmund Browder I also purchased land, located on Gravelly Run in Prince George County, which was surveyed on November 6, 1719 (PGCDB III, p. 750).  Later, Elizabeth Browder, his wife, signed a paper relinquishing her dower rights in a certain parcel of land which was also located in Prince George County, in the year 1720/21 (PGCW&D 1713-1728, p. 482).  This was the land that Edmund had surveyed in 1719.  Edmund W. Browder, their son, was a witness to this transaction, along with Robert Mumford, Buller Herbert and Col. John Bolling I (descendant of Pocahontas).  Elizabeth’s attorney for this transaction was Lewis Green, presumed half-brother of Burwell Green.  Edmund Browder I sold this land within the same year to Thomas Gregory on February 13, 1720. 

Two of the witnesses, Robert Mumford and Col. John Bolling I, again, frequently visited William Byrd of Westover over a period of several years.  The interesting thing here is that one of the witnesses to Elizabeth’s relinquishment of her dower rights was none other than Col. John Bolling I, son of Col. Robert Bolling (who married Anne Stith) and Jane Rolfe.  

Edmund Browder I also leased 50 acres of land on the south side of Blackwater Swamp to Thomas Gregory on August 7, 1721 (PGCDB III, p. 482).  And Edmund served on a jury in 1718.  Finally, Edmund Browder was paid 75 pounds of tobacco for serving three days as a witness in Prince George County in 1738.  This is the last record that shows that Edmund still lived.  So, we know by this final record that Edmund Browder I lived at least to 1738.  Again, he is known to have been deceased by sometime in 1738/39, according to records previously mentioned. 

Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth Browder were both members of the Bristol Parish, Church of England and attended the Old Blanford (St. Paul’s) Church, located in present day Petersburg, Virginia.  The Randolph, Epes/Eppes and Herbert families were also members of Bristol Parish, and members of these families also attended Blanford church along with the Browders.  We know this not just from records, but also because some members of these families are buried in the cemetery surrounding the church building.  In addition, Edmund Browder was a tobacco planter, as the others also were, so they held a similar status in society, although wealth between these families varied greatly. 

Early Browder family members are likely buried in the church cemetery, but their gravesites cannot be located today because no readable headstones exist for them, if any ever did at all.  They probably never had any headstones, but likely only had wooden crosses marking their graves, which deteriorated over time.  Wooden crosses placed at the head of a grave was a standard practice for that time period, to be replaced some of the time with a more permanent monument of stone.  The crosses marking these Browder graves probably never were replaced by stone monuments.  But Blanford Church was obviously the closest church in Bristol Parish to the early Browder homesteads, so they almost certainly were buried there, if not on their own lands somewhere. 

It is of interest to note that during the American Revolutionary War, the Battle of Petersburg was fought very close to the church building.  As the British forces landed at City Point (modern day Hopewell) the Prussian Maj. Gen. Baron Friedrich Wilhelm Augustin von Steuben, who had taken command of the American forces around Petersburg, devised a plan to make the British fight and use up resources and time, after which he and the Continental Army would withdraw to fight another day.  The plan worked.  After this battle, British Maj. Gen. William Phillips contracted a fever and died on May 13, 1781.  He was buried in Blanford Church Cemetery in a secret location by none other than his deputy commander, Brig. Gen. Benedict Arnold.  During the church’s heyday, memorial services were held there for Presidents George Washington, John Adams, and Thomas Jefferson, following their deaths. 

During the Siege of Petersburg in the American Civil War, 1864-1865, the church building was used as a Confederate field hospital.  In June of 1866, the first Memorial Day observation was celebrated at this location in honor of Confederate soldiers.  In 1901, the Ladies Memorial Association of Petersburg began to transform the old building into a Confederate memorial chapel.  Each Confederate state contributed to the addition of one Louis Comfort Tiffany stained glass window (15 in all). 

Having been inside, I can attest to the fact that the church building is actually quite small, so those who attended Blanford Church would have been well acquainted with one another.  And it is well known that during colonial times, persons would be heavily fined for failure to attend church, which was a capital offense, in addition to the already high church support taxes levied by the King of England.  So, attendance was mandatory. 

From the Bristol Parish Register, begun on October 30, 1720 (which was lost for several years but, miraculously, later found again), we can glean the names of most of the known children of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, as well as for several other early Browder families.  However, all of their children are not listed because the vestry was not initiated prior to several of the children’s births, and also after about 1742, no further Browder entries were made in this register at all.

In the early 1720s, only the brothers John, George Andrew, and Edmund, W., sons of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, were having children christened within Bristol Parish.  In 1727 William Browder also began having children christened within this parish (Penland Browder, p. 5).  The known children of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder were John (c1680-p. 1762), Edmund W. (c1699-1771), George Andrew (c1701-p. 1741), and, probably, William (c1702-c1788).  It is very likely that they also had some daughters.

What is certain is that, with the exception of a few Browders who arrived in the Americas at some later dates, all persons in the United States of America who possess the surname Browder are likely descended from Edmund and Elizabeth Browder.  This is also well stated by Helen Sides-Dye in her book The Browder Connections. 

John Browder, son of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, married Elizabeth Jones in 1715.  Edmund W. Browder, son of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, married Martha about 1720.  George Andrew Browder, son of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, married Elizabeth about 1724.  And William, probable son of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, married Dorothy about 1727.  The maiden names of the wives of all of these Browder brothers are unknown or uncertain at this time except for Elizabeth, who married John.  John Browder married Elizabeth Jones, daughter of Lt. Arthur Jones and Susanna King. 

Edmund, John, Edmund W., and George Andrew Browder all owned land in the same area, which connected.  William Browder owned land close to these four.  George Andrew Browder, for example, purchased 264 acres of land on June 22, 1722, situated on both sides of the Gravelly Run. 

Also, on December 1, 1740, Francis and Isham Epes (these appear to be sons of William Epes, who married a Worsham, and grandsons of Francis Epes III) purchased 694 acres of land in Prince George County, Virginia, located on the south side of Gravelly Run, which adjoined their own lands and those of Richard Nance and William Browder (Prince George County Land Patent Book [PGCLPB] 19, pp. 881-82).  On the same date, Francis Epes purchased 400 acres of land, located on the lower side of the main Sellar Fork of Deep Creek, which was bounded by lands of Isham Epes and William Browder (ibid, p. 883). 

The only other reference that appears to have been about the above Isham Epes I, that have found, is the following:

Bond of Isham Epes, Thomas Scott, and John Dabney of Dinwiddie Co. to Richard Epes and Augustine Claiborne, for £1000; Isham being appointed Adm’r of William Worsham, decd. with will annexed on 4 May past, with Richard Epes and Augustine Claiborne his security, Claiborne and Epes felt they were in possible jeopardy by possible maladministration of estate. witnesses: James Boisseau and John Jones. Recorded: 1 Mar 1754 (Arlandson, Epps of Early Virginia; Chesterfield County Deed Book 2, p. 97). 

Edmund W. Browder, son of Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth, patented 100 acres next to that of his father’s, on Gravelly Run on June 16, 1727.  Edmund Browder “of the island” (as he was referred to) is listed in the 1747 Henrico County tithables list.  Edmund W. Browder was referred to mistakenly as Edward Browder, Jr. in a 1751 deed in which he acquired 113 acres of land on Gravelly Run (Old Petersburg).  Edmund W. Browder held 394 acres of land in Chesterfield County on Skinquarter Road as early as 1756.  He sold this tract to George Robertson on October 21, 1765.  He then acquired 2 acres, located in Chesterfield County, in 1756, which was part of this island, located in the Appomattox River.  He added three acres in 1758 to this tract at a place called “Long Island” in the document.  In June of 1761, Edmund W. Browder purchased 125 acres of land from William Dyson.  He then sold this tract of land to Joseph Rowlett in April of 1767.  Edmund W. Browder is also known to have served in the Chesterfield County militia by 1760. 

To be a bit more clear, there existed an island, then in Chesterfield County, on the Appomattox River at the time of the deeds called “Browder’s Island” (also called “Flee Island” and “Long Island” in the documents). Obviously, it was then called “Browder’s Island” because the Edmund W. Browder family resided on it and owned most, if not all, of it.  Edmund W. Browder certainly resided there as late as 1767 since he made a deed in Chesterfield County that year in which he was referred to as “Edmund Browder of the Island” (Charles City County Deed Book [CCCDB] 5, p. 501).  A survey for Robert Bolling, son of Col. John Bolling and Mary V. Kennon, was made of it in 1761 (Dinwiddie County Platt Book [DCPB], p. 16), which shows the location of a bridge to this island with reference to the town of Blanford, a swamp, and a stream called “Bolling’s Run”, which looks like a stream called “Lieutenant’s Run” on present day maps (Penland Browder, p. 87). 

This bridge was called “Browder’s” (located at Browder Crossing); constructed to allow access to this island.  This bridge is also said to have been close to “Jones’ Line” (ibid, p. 89).  An early record from the year 1759 stated that it was of no use to the general public.  It is also stated that this bridge already existed, and had been erected “during the twenty-fifth year of the reign of his Present Majesty” and, obviously, was in need of repair at that time.  So, according to this record, this bridge was erected in 1752, during the reign of King George II.  The document, gleaned from Hening, Chapter XXIV, page 308, reads as follows:

AN ACT TO PREVENT THE JUSTICES OF DINWIDDIE FROM OBLIGING THE JUSTICES OF CHESTERFIELD TO JOIN WITH THEM IN BUILDING A BRIDGE, OVER APPOMATTOX RIVER, AT A PLACE CALLED BROWDER’S ISLAND.

I.  WHEREAS a bridge hath lately been erected by one Browder, over Appomattox river, just below the falls thereof, between the counties of Chesterfield and Dinwiddie, and the said bridge is of little or no advantage to the inhabitants of the said county of Chesterfield.

II And whereas by a clause of an act of assembly, made on the twenty-fifth year of the reign of his present majesty, intituled, An Act for building a bridge over Appomattox river by subscription, the justices of both the said counties were prohibited from levying any money or tobacco on the inhabitants of the said counties for re-building the bridge in the said act mentioned:  And the said bridge is now out of repair, and from its great conveniency to the public will be rebuilt by subscription.

III        And it will be very burthensome to the inhabitants of said county of Chesterfield to be obliged to join the inhabitants of the said county of Dinwiddie in the charges of re-building or keeping in repair the bridge known by the name of Browder’s:  Be it enacted, by the Lieutenant-Governor, Council, and Burgesses, of this present General Assembly, and it is hereby enacted, by the authority of the same, That the justices of the county of Chesterfield shall not be obliged at any time hereafter to join with the justices of the county of Dinwiddie to build a bridge over the said river, at the place aforesaid, at the charge of the said county of Chesterfield, any law, usage or custom to the contrary thereof in any wise notwithstanding.

Thus, the bridge was ordered to be repaired by subscription, but the residents of Chesterfield County were not made to contribute.

Further research on my part has allowed me to conclude (and I wasn’t even looking for this at all) that the island in question was one and the same island that Ft. Henry had been established on. Of course, by this time the fort had surely deteriorated and was no longer in use. And it wasn’t quite the outpost that it had once been. But the progression is that Ft. Henry was established upon Flee Island below the falls of the Appomattox River and was first commanded by Maj. Gen. Abraham Wood. It then fell to Maj. Peter Jones to command. Eventually, that location, which was also a major trading area, became known as “Peter’s Point”, from which Petersburg eventually grew. Edmund W. Browder eventually purchased part, or most, of that island and resided there.

Finally, William Gates sold 100 acres of land located on the branches of Skinquarter Creek to George Robertson on May 7, 1762.  Later, Edmund W. Browder sold to him 394 acres on Skinquarter Road on October 21, 1765; a tract which Edmund had held since March 10, 1756.

In any case, it seems that the Edmund W. Browder family did not fare particularly well, as noted in the following from the Bristol Parish Vestry Book, entry dated 1769:

To Paid Patt Bird [Byrd] for attending Edmond Browder & his wife, Poor people of this Parish, on Sufferance & ordered by Robert Bolling, Esqr late Church Warden & Doctor James Field who attended the Sd Browder was Called upon by Robt Bolling Esqr & my Self, who said, they Deserved 40/, . . . (p. 226).

The Bristol Parish Vestry Book (Blanford and Petersburg) also notes the following for the July 30, 1771:

To a Grave Digger for Edmond Browder 2/6 . . .  . . (p. 227)

Nov. 1771 – To Cash Paid Digging Edmond Browder’s Grave, . . . .2   6 (p. 244).

To 2 Quarts of Rum 2/6 ¾ Lb. Tea 1/6 Edmond Browders Widow, . . . 4 (p. 245).

Sadly, the grave of Edmund W. Browder can no longer be located.  Whether Edmund Browder I may also have been buried in the church cemetery, along with his wife, Elizabeth,  also cannot be ascertained today as no extant records are available.

The known children of Edmund W. Browder and Martha (listed in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book) were Handstress (b. November 30, 1721), James (b. August 24, 1725), and William (October 31, 1727-c1802).  It is believed by some that Handstress may have married a member of the Isham family, but this has not been proven, to my knowledge. 

William Browder was taxed on 55 acres of land in Dinwiddie County, Virginia from 1787 to 1795.  From 1796 to 1802 he was taxed on 40 acres of land there.  Then from 1803 to 1804 his estate was taxed on 40 acres of land (Hughes, p. 52). 

In 1739, George Andrew Browder, son of Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth, was appointed by the Vestry of Bristol Parish as Sexton of Hatcher’s Run Chapel, appointed to ensure that a new chapel be built on property owned by Thomas Bonner, Sr.  George A. Browder was paid 75 pounds of tobacco for this. 

George A. Browder owned 264 acres of land on Gravelly Run in Prince George County, Virginia, next to his brother, John Browder.  And he sold 100 acres to his brother, Edmund W. Browder, on September 9, 1723. 

The known children of George Andrew and Elizabeth Browder, all daughters, according to the Bristol Parish Vestry Book, were Mary (b. August 20, 1725), Jean (b. December 29, 1729), Priscilla (b. June 2, 1735), and Winifred “Winny” (b. January 7, 1741). It is my belief, although unproven, that Priscilla married Thomas Bonner, Jr.  He is known to have married someone named Priscilla, and they had at least one known daughter, Elizabeth, who was christened in North Farnham Parish, Richmond, Virginia on Sept. 20, 1751. 

Thomas Bonner, Sr. was a brother of John Bonner who married Mary Cooke, daughter of Thomas Cooke I (son of William Cooke II and Jane Roper). Thomas Cooke I married Mary Jones, daughter of Lt. Arthur Jones and Susanna King. 

William Browder (c1702-c1788), son of Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth, married Dorothy.  William Browder was taxed on 100 acres of land, located in Dinwiddie County, Virginia, from 1787 to 1788 (Hughes, 52).  It should be noted that William Browder is presumed to be the son of Edmund and Elizabeth Browder because he resided in the same area.  But there is no actual record proving this. 

The known children of William and Dorothy Browder were Sarah (b. December 22, 1727), and Amy (b. July 11, 1733). 

It is of interest to note here that there are some pretty obvious gaps between the births of some of the children mentioned here.  To begin with, for Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth, there is a substantial gap between the years 1680-1699.  This could signify any number of things, including that this couple may have had children who are unknown to us. 

The same discrepancy holds true with the sons, Edmund, George Andrew, and William.  For Edmund and his wife, a discrepancy between the years 1721 to 1725 (a four-year gap) is found, which is really not that big, but noticeable in any case.  For George and his wife, the gaps between each child are very noticeable, indicating the possibility that they could have had more children between each known child.  Finally, for William and his wife, a gap for the years between 1727 to 1733 (a six-year gap) becomes obvious. 

The fact that the children of these families, up to about the year 1742, are listed in the Bristol Parish Vestry Book would seem to indicate that they had no other children, unless some were had after 1742, but that is not a given.  It is fairly likely, however.  It is unlikely, but not quite impossible, that other children were born to these couples.

It is a virtual certainty that Edmund and Elizabeth Browder would have had some daughters as well as the sons that are known to us.  And some surely reached adulthood, married, and had children of their own.  Wilmuth and similar names being quite common in the Browder family early on, I suggest that they also did have a daughter named Wilmuth and that this daughter was the Wilmuth/Wilma who married Capt. John Banister, son of Rev. John Banister and Martha Batte. Capt. John Banister was brought up by the Byrd family of Westover, and later became a business associate to William Byrd II. Banister became a Justice for Prince George County as well as a Vestryman for Bristol Parish. Lt. Col. John Banister (b. Dec. 26, 1734, Prince George County, Virginia), son of Capt. John Banister and Wilmuth, is buried in a family cemetery on Hatcher’s Run in Dinwiddie County, Virginia which appears to be located close to the location of the chapel that George Andrew Browder was tasked to have erected in 1739. He became a Continental Congressman and was a signatory to the Articles of Confederation. And he served in other governmental and military positions. He married (1) Elizabeth Mumford of Amelia County, Virginia, (2) Elizabeth Bland, and (3) Ann “Nancy” Blair. Part of his state did go to members of the Browder family in 1788, but details are unknown. 

Capt. John Banister and Wilmuth also had a daughter, Martha, born on Dec. 21, 1732 in Prince George County, Virginia. 

John Browder I, son of Edmund Browder I and Elizabeth Parsons, is dealt with in another posting.
