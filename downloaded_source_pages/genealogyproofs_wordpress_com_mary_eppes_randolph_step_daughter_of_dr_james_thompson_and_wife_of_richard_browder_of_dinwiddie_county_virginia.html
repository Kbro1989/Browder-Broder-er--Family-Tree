Mary Epes Randolph, Step-Daughter of Dr. James Thompson and wife of Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia

Posted byjmarkbrowderSeptember 4, 2019December 28, 2025Posted inUncategorized

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Dec. 28, 2025)

Mary Epes (1700-c1753), daughter of Col. Francis Epes III (1657/59-Jan. 1718) and Anne Elizabeth Isham (1665-1718), married (1) William Randolph, Gent. (c1690-c1726), son of Capt. Henry Randolph II (Jan. 16, 1665-Feb. 26, 1693) and Sarah Swann (c1672-1714) about 1718, and later, (2) Dr. James Thompson.  She married James on February 8, 1724/25.  These are facts, as can be found in the records. 

Mary Epes was born about 1700 at Bermuda Hundred, Henrico County, Virginia, and was deceased by June 1, 1753.  According to the “Epes Book”, Mary Epes may have been “Mistress Mary Epes a pretty girl” mentioned in the diary of William Byrd of Westover on February 17, 1709. 

Dr. James Thompson was a physician.  He was born about 1696, also at Bermuda Hundred in Henrico County, Virginia.  James died in 1746/47. 

Mary is believed by some to have remarried after his death to (3) George Robertson, but I have been unable to find evidence to support this. 

The children of William Randolph and Mary Epes were Isham (before August, 1720-August 1751), Mary Epes (b. c1720), and an unknown female (b. c1722).  The daughter of Dr. James Thompson and Mary Epes was Anne (c1730-c1793).  James did not have a daughter named Mary.

Anne Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson and Mary Epes (1700-c1753), married John Sturdivant (c1719-c1793).  The known children of John and Ann Sturdivant were; Mary Epes (b. Jan. 18, 1750), Thompson, a son (b. Sept. 11, 1752), Ann Isham (b. Oct. 10, 1754), John (b. May 20, 1756), Sally (b. July 20, 1758), and Joel (b. Jan. 15, 1764).  These are taken from the Bristol Parish Register. 

Isham Randolph, son of William Randolph and Mary Epes, never married.  He died in Henrico (now Chesterfield) County, Virginia.  On October 25, 1751, the inventory of Isham Randolph’s estate was presented by Burwell Green (Chesterfield County Will Book [CCWB]1, 1749-1753, p. 207).  The account of Isham’s estate was sworn to in court by Burwell Green, administrator, in May of 1756 (Chesterfield County Will Book [CCWB] 1, 1749-1753, p. 206). 

It is of interest to note that, on May 1, 1748, “Henry Randolph, Jr. of Dale Parish”, deeded to George Cousins, for £40, 447 acres of land located in Dale Parish, next to the properties of Field Archer, George Cousins, Gills, Widow Franklin, Col. Poythress, and Isham Randolph, this land being part of 1,790 acres which was patented to Col. Francis Epes, grandfather of Isham Randolph, but which had lapsed (because Isham died), and was then granted to Henry Randolph, Jr.  There were no witnesses, but it was signed by Henry Randolph and recorded on May 1, 1749 (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Chesterfield County Deed Book, 1748-1750, p. 99). 

This was Henry Randolph IV, son of Henry Randolph III and Elizabeth Eppes, sister of Mary Eppes. This deed alone proves that Mary Epes married William Randolph because their son, Isham, was patented these lands by his own grandfather, Francis Epes. But after the death of Isham Randolph, this property was transferred to Henry Randolph IV, son of Henry Randolph III and Elizabeth Epes.

Mary Epes Randolph (b. c1720), daughter of William Randolph (d. c1723) and Mary Epes (1700-c1753), married Richard Browder (c1719-c1807), about 1741.

The unknown female (b. c1722) married (1) Burwell Green (d. aft. 1751).  They had at least two daughters, Mary Isham Green and Betty Green, who are named in their grandmother’s Will. 

It is important here to provide some detail about William Randolph, Gent. The documentation is as follows: First, on December 1, 1707, Giles Webb and his wife, Sarah, brought suit against John Stuart due to a deed, dated Oct. 29, 1695, in which Giles and his wife, Sarah, allowed for fifteen years Stuart to dwell upon said plantation which was on Swift Creek where Henry Randolph, deceased, formerly lived, and Stuart was to keep the dwelling house there in good repair. However, a fire burnt said house on Dec. 30, 1705, and Stuart refused to repair it. Therefore, Giles and his wife, Sarah, were asking for 10,000 lbs. of tobacco, which was the bond.

Please understand that there is no indication that the Col. William Randolph I side of the family ever owned any lands at Swift Creek. It was the Henry Randolph line who owned lands there.

On August 5, 1709, Giles Webb of Henrico County, Virginia wrote his will. To his loving wife, Sarah, he bequeathed all of his lands during her lifetime, etc. To “son-in-law” (step-son), Henry Randolph (III), the other half of his goods, etc. After wife’s death, his lands were to go to his brother, Thomas Webb, of Gloucester, England. Will was proven, June 1, 1713.

In the meantime, on February 5, 1710, “Henry Randolph, Jun’r., son and heir at law of Henry Randolph of the parish of Bristol, dec’d” deeded to John and Thomas Pleasants, Gents., 175 acres of land in Henrico County, Virginia upon which two mills and a dam stood, on Swift Creek, “which land and mills descended to me as heir of my father, who was son and heir of Henry Randolph Sen’r the patentee and builder of said Mills, Land, &c. . . . Beginning at a tree on the North side of Swift Creek, parting this land and the land now of John Archer, being part of the Saw Mill divident, (so called). . . .”

The above is Henry Randolph III. It is not certain which John Pleasants is being referred to here, but he was a relative in any case. Thomas Pleasants was, presumably, a brother of John.

A distinct pattern begins to emerge here. Careful attention to detail suggests that Henry Randolph III “inherited” directly from his father, Henry Randolph II, even though Henry Randolph II did not leave a will. There is no indication of William “inheriting” anything from him directly. But apparently, there was a plan for him in any case.

Note that John Archer I above married Martha Feild, daughter of Maj. Peter Feild and Judith Soane, widow of Henry Randolph I. John Archer II married Sarah Randolph, daughter of the above Henry Randolph III and Elizabeth Eppes.

That Henry Randolph III calls himself, “son and heir at law of Henry Randolph of the parish of Bristol, dec’d” does not mean that he was the only son and heir. But he may have been the only direct heir, for whatever reason.

Later, on March 7, 1716, Thomas Webb of Gloucester, England, deeded to William Randolph, all lands in Virginia left him by his brother, Giles Webb.

There can be no real mistake here. Thomas Webb is deeding to William Randolph lands that had been passed to him by his brother through Sarah Swann, who married his brother, Giles, to a son of Henry Randolph II, first husband of Sarah Swann. There is NO reason why this would be any other William Randolph than the son of Henry Randolph II. If William were not a son of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann, then these lands would surely have been deeded to Henry Randolph III. If Henry Randolph II had left William Randolph out of his inheritance, his mother, Sarah, cleverly, did not.

Aside from this, William Randolph first appears in the extant records in August of 1711 in a deed in which John Archer and his wife, Martha, deed to William Randolph, 961 acres of land left by Peter Feild to said Martha, his daughter. This John Archer was John Archer I who married Martha Feild, daughter of Peter Feild and Judith Soane, widow of Capt. Henry Randolph I.

Again, the above William Randolph, it seems obvious, is a son of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann and he had just turned 21 years of age. This William Randolph would be the brother of Henry Randolph III, who married Mary Eppes, daughter of Col. Francis Eppes III and Anne Elizabeth Isham. Henry Randolph III married Elizabeth Eppes, sister of Mary Eppes.

Again, there can be no real mistake here. Thomas Webb is deeding to William Randolph lands that had been passed to him by his brother through Sarah Swann, who married his brother, Giles, to a son of Henry Randolph II, first husband of Sarah Swann.

Further, on September 28, 1716, Thomas Webb, Gent. of the city of Gloucester, England deeded to William Randolph, Gent., of Virginia, several other tracts of land in Henrico County, Virginia “which Giles Webb late of the said Colony deceased devised to the said Thomas Webb in fee simple. . . .” John Bolling was attorney for Thomas Webb for the Prince George County section. Other attorneys appointed in his stead by Thomas Webb for Henrico County property were Joseph Royall, Jr., Francis Epes, Jr., Thomas Randolph, and Richard Randolph, all of Henrico County, Virginia. Signed: Thomas Randolph, Isham Randolph, John Randolph, Edward Hill, E. Randolph, Thomas Posford, etc. (Henrico County, Virginia Wills and Deeds Book, 1714-1718, pp. 157-58).

Again, the above William Randolph is obviously a son of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann. On first reading of this document, one might think that the William Randolph in question must have been William Randolph II, son of Col. William Randolph I and Mary Isham, who married Elizabeth Beverly since almost all of his brothers are named or signed said document. However, one must remember that all of these persons were Justices and commonly selected as attorneys by many people. Plus, they were relatives.

Further, on June 28, 1717, William Randolph of Henrico County, Virginia deeded to William Byrd of Charles City County, Virginia, 418 acres of land on the south side of the James River near Falling Creek, “lately in possession of Giles Webb, deced”, bought by Webb from Andrew Martin, and 132 acres of land on the north side of the James River, bounded by the lands of the said William Byrd. Witnesses: Charles Griffin and Richard Randolph (Henrico County, Virginia Wills and Deeds Book, 1714-1718, pp. 181-82).

Then, on July 1, 1717, William Randolph of Henrico County, Virginia deeds to Thomas Wood, Jr., of same, 277 acres of land on the north side of the James River in Henrico Parish and County, sold by Joshua Stepp, Jr. to Giles Webb, Gent., and from him to his brother, Thomas Webb, and by Thomas Webb to William Randolph. Witnesses: Joseph Pleasants and John Redford (Henrico County, Virginia Wills and Deeds Book, 1714-1718, p. 183).

And on July 1, 1717, William Randolph of Henrico County, Virginia deeds to John Bolling, Gent., of same, 150 acres of land in Henrico Parish and County on the south side of the James River, bounded by the land formerly owned by Henry Lound and of John Bowman, Jr., land which Henry Gee conveyed to Giles Webb and by Giles Webb to his brother, Thomas Webb, and by Thomas Webb to William Randolph, Witnesses: William Kennon and Joseph Royall, Jr.

“At a Court held for Henrico County the first day of July 1717 William Randolph acknowledged this Deed with the Livery and Seizen acknowledged to be his Act and Ded, and the Land with the … admitted to Record. Then Elizabeth his wife being first personally examined … her right of Dower in the land hereby conveyed. . . .” (Henrico County, Virginia Wills and Deeds Book, 1714-1718, pp. 186-87).

This is William Randolph, Gent., son of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann, who married Mary Eppes, in both of the above deeds. It is apparent from this document, however, it appears obvious now, that William Randolph had first been married to someone named Elizabeth. It is easy here to jump to the conclusion that these deeds are really referring to William Randolph II who married Elizabeth Beverly. But that simply would not make sense given the preponderance of the evidence.

Finally, on June 3, 1726 (dated 1727, but should be 1726), John Stewart, planter, of Henrico County, Virginia deeded to “William Randolph of the same county, Gent.”, 400 acres of land in Henrico County, Virginia near Fighting Creek lately surveyed by Francis Eppes, County Surveyor, part of a patent lately granted to Stewart on Nov. 24, 1725. Witnesses: John Redford, John Corn and Thomas Short, Jr. (Henrico County, Virginia Wills and Deeds Book, No. 1, Part 1, 1725-1737, pp. 103-04).

Again, the reason we know that this has to do with William Randolph, son of Henry Randolph II and Sarah Swann, is that there was a lawsuit against John Stuart, dated Dec. 1, 1707, in which Giles Webb and his wife, Sarah [Swann], brought suit due to a deed dated Oct. 29, 1695 in which Giles and his wife, Sarah, allowed for fifteen years Stuart to dwell upon their plantation on Swift Creek. Stuart must have purchased this land in the meantime and then sold it to William Randolph. But William, sadly, died soon thereafter and his wife, Mary Eppes, married Dr. James Thomoson the next year.

To conclude the proof documentation concerning William Randolph, it must be noted that in 1753, during the May session of the Chesterfield County, Virginia Court, the following order was made. “Ordered that the petition of Samuel Gordon admer de canis non of James Thompson decd against Elizabeth Randolph decd. be dismissed.” Following this entry on the same page is the same order concerning Benjamin Ratcliff (Chesterfield County, Virginia County Court Order Book 1, 1749-1754, p. 341).

The questions here are, (1) who was Samuel Gordon, (2) who was Elizabeth Randolph, and (3) who was Benjamin Ratcliff?

First, John Pleasants, son of John Pleasants and Dorothea Cary, purchased land from Samuel Gordon according to his 1771 will. The elder John Pleasant’s first wife was Mary Woodson. This doesn’t mean much in and of itself except that the Woodsons were related to the Randolphs and the Gordons appear to have been related to the Pleasants’ and the Parsons’. But how did he become administrator of the estate of Dr. James Thompson? Since he was a Justice in Henrico County, it is possible that he was simply appointed to said task by the Court, although he was a relative too, as was usually the case in such matters. That John Pleasants had a son named Samuel may be a further clue. Of course, the records show that John Pleasants sued Samuel Gordon too, but the cause is not known. I have actually found very little else to go on concerning Samuel Gordon.

As for the Ratcliffs, the family seems mainly to have been Quakers, as the Woodsons also were. So, this may be the main connection between them. I found absolutely nothing else about Benjamin Ratcliff.

Now to Elizabeth Randolph. Elizabeth Beverly-Randolph, wife of William Randolph II, was deceased by this time. But why would her estate be sued? It is understood that she died about 1723 (although this is a guess since the death year on her tombstone is unreadable), several years prior to her husband’s death. So, if an estate were to be sued between the two, it would have been his, not hers.

James Thompson was connected to the Henry Randolph line, not that of Col. William Randolph I. The preponderance of the evidence clearly shows this. That there are connections between the two Randolph families, however, is natural, especially since both lines married into the Eppes family. But if it is about the estate of Dr. James Thompson, and the suits are against (1) Elizabeth Randolph and (2) Benjamin Ratcliff, it would seem most likely that we are talking about a first wife of William Randolph, Gent., as the records seem to suggest. If indeed he had a first wife named Elizabeth, as the records appear to show, this is the Elizabeth Randolph (d. c1717) who is mentioned here. It is highly likely, then, that Benjamin Ratcliff was the executor of her estate and that it had not all been finalized even though she also died several years prior to this point. I suggest, but cannot prove, that, perhaps, she was an Elizabeth Ratcliffe. But there are several other names that could be proposed, including Woodson, for example.

Let’s be clear here. Siblings and other relatives don’t always get along. There is no indication that William Randolph, Gent. and Henry Randolph III had any real interaction with one another. But that does not prove that they were not siblings. That they married sisters indicates that they may have gotten along up to the point of these marriages, but that something might have transpired that changed their relationship. Perhaps we will never really know.

Regardless, it seems obvious that Henry was the older of the two, but only by about a year or so. One might like to think that it might have instead been Elizabeth who was the mother of William Randolph. That would make sense except that then one would have to answer why Sarah Swann was evidently so intent upon ensuring that William receive some sort of inheritance. It would make more sense to suggest that Henry was a son of the previously unknown Elizabeth. I am not stating this is a fact, of course, just a suggestion.

Anyway, for those who insist that Henry and William were not related due to supposed DNA evidence, I suggest that some further checking is in order now.

As for Dr. James Thompson, he was a witness to the Will of Peter Jones of Bristol Parish, Prince George County, Virginia, in 1721.  He later purchased land in Prince George County, Virginia on February 8, 1724/25.  He was a resident of Henrico County, Virginia by September 12, 1726, when he sold this land and his wife, Mary, relinquished her dower right to said land. 

In an indenture dated April 1, 1728, land was bought from Joseph Willson of Bristol Parish, County of Prince George, and sold to Theophilus Field of Elizabeth City County, of 169 acres on the south side of Appomattox River called “Boyling Springs.”  This tract of land adjoined lands of Richard Vaughn, Daniel Vaughn, John Vaughn and William Vaughn, and was “all the land Joseph Willson bought of Dr. James Thompson on that side of the Appomattox River.” 

It seems obvious that the land bought by Joseph Wilson is the land sold by Dr. James Thompson in 1726.  This deed (dated 1728) was witnessed by J. A. Mumford, George Willson, and Thomas Hardaway, and was signed by Joseph Willson.  It was recorded on April 9, 1728 in PGCW&DB 1713-1728, p. 1098. 

On August 17, 1731, James Thompson of Henrico County, Virginia gave power of attorney to his wife, Mary, to conduct all necessary business.  This was done in preparation for a move to Edgecombe Precinct, North Carolina, where they settled by November of 1731.  Isham Randolph, Mary’s son, also made this move along with them.  It is interesting to note that there is no mention of Mary Epes Randolph as having made the move along with the others. But perhaps she did.

Once relocated, the couple were witnesses to a land deed with William Epes.  In 1734 a deed between James Thompson of Edgecombe Precinct and Henry Goff of same was witnessed by Isham Randolph, Alexander Wright and William Epes, Sr.  The James Thompson family all returned to Henrico County, Virginia sometime later. 

Significantly, in October of 1740, Mary Epes Randolph, orphan of William Randolph, Gent., chose Dr. James Thompson as her guardian.  John Robertson, Gent, held security (Arlandson, “Randolphs of Early Virginia”; Court Orders 1737-1746, p. 126).  This demonstrates that Mary was unmarried at the time.  Why so many years had transpired between the marriage of Dr. James Thompson and Mary Epes and this guardianship selection is not certain. 

This is a key document that not only shows that she had chosen Thompson as her guardian, but also that she was not yet married to Richard Browder. It also demonstrates how Mary became known as Mary Thompson rather than Mary Randolph. Family tradition had always stated that Richard Browder married Mary Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson. But, again, he had no daughter named Mary.

The documentation, verbatim, reads, “Thompson Guard – Mary Randolph one of the Orphants (sic) of William Randolph Gent Deced coms (sic) into Court and chooses Doctor James Thompson to be her Guardian who accept. Thereof and enters into Bond for the Same. John Robertson Gent [son of Rev. George Robertson, Sr. and Mary Worsham (daughter of Capt. John Worsham I)] Security. Present Richard Royall Gent.” (Henrico County, Virginia Court Orders 1737-1746, p. 126).

Later, on September 6, 1751, Mary Epes-Thompson, mother of Isham Randolph, dcsd., having relinquished her right of administration; Burwell Green, as the greatest creditor was appointed as Administrator of Isham’s estate.  William Pride and Edward Hill held security (Arlandson, Randolphs of Early Virginia; Chesterfield County Order Book [CCOB] 1, p. 138).  

Still later, on October 4, 1751, Mary Epes-Thompson, widow of James Thompson, was summoned to accept or refuse administration of her late husband’s estate (ibid, p. 147), an action which became necessary due to the death of her son, Isham Randolph, who had also previously been named as his Executor. She did not accept and administration of his estate fell to Samuel Gordon.

Isham Randolph, son of William Randolph and Mary Epes, had also been a witness to James Thompson’s (of Dale Parish, Chesterfield County, Virginia) Will, which was dated March 6, 1746, and proven and recorded on the first Monday in November,1747 in Henrico County, Virginia (Chesterfield County Deed Book 1744-1748, pp. 314-314a).  This Will names Thompson’s own daughter Anne, his wife, Mary, and his “son-in-law” (actually, step-son) Isham Randolph (again, Mary Epes-Thompson and Isham Randolph were both named as Executors).  Other witnesses were Field Archer (1711-1785), Elizabeth Archer, and Katherine Mail. 

The Will stipulated that half of James Thompson’s estate was to be given to his wife, Mary, and the other half to his daughter, Ann Sturdivant.  No mention is made of Mary Epes Randolph, daughter of his wife, Mary.  It is entirely possible that, since she was not actually his daughter, he did not wish to leave anything to her, or to her brother, Isham.  They might have, presumably, been expected to have inherited from their mother later, after all. 

As an aside, in 1749, after James Thompson died, James Randolph purchased land from (1) Joseph Kennedy of Mill Creek and, (2) 200 acres of land from Benjamin Bordon on the corner of Samuel McClure’s and James Thompson’s line, also on Mill Creek. 

Mary Thompson’s (of Dale Parish) Will was dated January 23, 1753 and probated in Chesterfield County, Virginia on June 1, 1753 (CCOB 1, 1749-1753, pp. 85-88/p. 353).  She had died by April 6, 1753 (ibid, p. 330). Mary left half of her property, as well as seven negroes, to her daughter, Anne Sturdivant (the daughter of James Thompson), and the other half, along with eight negroes, to her granddaughters, Mary Isham Green and Betty Green.  Mary also mentioned her grandchildren, Mary Epes Sturdivant and Thompson Sturdivant, in her Will.  Her daughter, Anne Sturdivant, and Ann’s husband, John Sturdivant, were named as Executors (Saunders, Early Settlers of North Alabama, p. 475).  Her own daughter, Mary, was not mentioned and received nothing. Perhaps, then, she had received her inheritance when she married Richard Browder. Things like this often happened.

The following is an excerpt of a judgment relating to the Will of Mary Epes-Randolph (note that she is referred to as Mary Randolph in said judgment, not Mary Thompson, even though she signed her will as “Mary Thompson”), from BCDB 7, 95:

Joseph King and William Thornton are held and firmly bound unto Burwell Green and Benjamin Hunt of Sussex County, in the sum of 500 pounds, that whereas the above Burwell Green and Benjamin Hunt became bound unto William Lightfoot, first Justice of the Commission of the Peace of the County of Sussex, for the due execution of the Last Will and Testament MARY RANDOLPH, deceased, to make or cause to be made a true and perfect inventory of all and singular the goods, chattel and credits of the said deceased which should come into the hands of Joseph King to fulfill his bond, dated the 22nd day of June, 1756. Signed by Joseph King (his mark) and William Thornton. Witnesses were Wm. Clack and James Maclin Junr. Bond proved by the oaths of William Clack and James Maclin Junr., in Court on April 26, 1762. 

It is clear that both Mary Isham Green and Betty Green were both minors, and still unmarried, when the Will was written.  And it is noted in said will that both were under 14 years of age at the time.  They also would have to have been born prior to October of 1740, due to the guardianship judgment.  So, they would both have been born between about 1738, at the earliest, and October of 1740.  Whatever happened to them, beyond this point in time, is unknown, however, because I have found no further record of either of them.  It is entirely possible that both reached adulthood and married.  But that is not a certainty. 

Again, one must keep in mind that family tradition has always held that Richard Browder of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, married “Mary Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson”.  So, unless one wishes to posit that this is an error and that he must have married some other Mary Thompson (or even some other Mary), then one has to accept that Richard actually married this Mary Epes Randolph, step-daughter of Dr. James Thompson.  There literally is no other conclusion to be drawn.  The facts are clear.  Dr. James Thompson did not have a daughter named Mary, and Mary Epes Randolph, his step-daughter, requested that he become her guardian in 1740, prior to her marriage to Richard Browder.  Whatever the circumstances really were, this has to be the lady referred to as “Mary Thompson, daughter of Dr. James Thompson” in family tradition.

Either way, there is also no evidence that I have been able to find that either of the granddaughters reached maturity.
