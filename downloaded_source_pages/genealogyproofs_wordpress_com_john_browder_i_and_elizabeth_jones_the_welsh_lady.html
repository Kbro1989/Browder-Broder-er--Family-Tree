John Browder I and Elizabeth Jones (the “Welsh Lady”)

By: John Mark Browder, B.A., M.S., M.T.S., M.Div. (updated and corrected, Dec. 27, 2024)

John Browder I (c1686-c1757), son of Edmund Browder I (c1651-c1739) and Elizabeth (c1653-1721/22), whom I believe to have been a Parsons, was born in Charles City (later Prince George) County, Virginia.  

The name of John’s wife was Elizabeth (c1685-p. 1741), and family tradition has always held that she was a “Welsh lady.”  The fact that she has been remembered as a “lady” is telling, since that would signify that she was, perhaps, descended from one of the Welsh noble families.  And it is known that Welsh noble families traced their ancestry to King Arthur, and tended to name their first sons Arthur. 

It is my belief that Elizabeth’s maiden name was Jones, both because this is a Welsh ancestral name of noble origins, and also because one of their descendants, a grandson of Richard Browder and Mary of Dinwiddie County, Virginia, was named Jones Browder.  Another of their descendants, son of their granddaughter Nancy Browder, who married Thomas Williams, was named Jones Williams. 

In further support of the belief that Elizabeth was of Welsh descent, one needs only to look at the name of her eldest son, Arthur (1715-c1738).  Again, during this time period, it was very common for the Welsh, Cornish, and those from Brittany in Northern France, to name their first sons after the mythical/ historical figure, King Arthur.  This appears to be the first time that the name Arthur can be found within the Browder family line anywhere.  And, no doubt due to his mother’s influence, the story of King Arthur had some impact upon young Arthur Browder himself as he was growing up.  It is possible that he even went so far as to try to emulate King Arthur to some extent, having been influenced by his mother and the story of King Arthur and the Knights of the Round Table.  It should not be considered at all far-fetched to suggest such a thing as popular entertainment having great influence upon people.  That is, possibly, an even greater common occurrence today.  There can be little doubt, Elizabeth instilled the tale of King Arthur and the Round Table into the minds of all of her children.

So, again, Elizabeth may perhaps also have been of noble descent herself, a true “Welsh lady”.  And, although little is actually known of John and Elizabeth Browder, their impact upon future generations of their descendants cannot be overestimated.  Indeed, Elizabeth must have made a very strong impression on at least the next two Browder generations descended from her in order for her to be remembered simply as a “Welsh lady” even while they, apparently, actually forgot her maiden name.  So, it is perfectly obvious that, whatever her maiden name might really have been, the influence of her Welsh heritage was remembered and passed down to later generations of her descendants, probably due, in part, to the imparting of the story of King Arthur. 

John Browder I and Elizabeth were married, almost certainly, in 1715, since that is the year Arthur is supposed to have been born and because Elizabeth is still referred to as “Eliz Jones” in her (presumed, but virtually certain) father’s will, which was written early in that year.  This date proves important in determining Elizabeth’s parentage, as will be shown. 

The idea that Elizabeth may have been a member of the Jones family is entirely my theory.  I had never encountered it elsewhere prior to coming to this conclusion, and had, in fact, encountered other theories, which I found to be without merit because they were based on little or no evidence.  Still, I myself did look into the possibility of her descent from other families, including the Scoggan and Mumford  families (since association with these families is obvious), for example, finding nothing of substance. 

It is said that three Jones brothers, Peter, William and Richard, emigrated from Wales to the American Colonies together.  Although it is more complicated than this, it is known that the first Peter Jones settled in Dinwiddie County, Virginia on or near the location of present day Petersburg, but he was not the founder of Petersburg.  Still, mainly due to his influence, the area was first called “Peter’s House”, then “Peter’s Town”, and finally “Petersburg.”  His brother, William Jones, died unmarried (Duda, p. 166).  She is, unfortunately, also not descended from this Jones line. 

But, before we solve this mystery, we must continue with John and Elizabeth themselves.  Extant records concerning or involving John Browder I, follow: 

John Browder I is first mentioned (as in, for the first time) in the official records of Prince George (later Dinwiddie) County, Virginia, in 1712, at which time he had some land surveyed by Robert Bolling, son of Col. John Bolling and Mary V. Kennon (PGCDB III, 1718-1728, p. 750).  This land was located on Gravelly Run near old Petersburg.  The fact that he first appears in such a record in 1712 shows that he had turned twenty-one years of age and was, therefore, considered to be an adult.  

The next record we have took place when, on August 11, 1719, John Browder, along with several others, was summoned to answer a petition and complaint of William Davis, Overseer of Highways,

For their contempt in refusing to assist in clearing the highway, and these persons (except JOHN BROWDER), failing to show just cause, it was ordered that each of them, except BROWDER, pay Davis 5 shillings.  Present: Maj. Robert Munford (Va. Gen. Soc. Qtly., Nov. 1986, p. 58). 

The timing of this alleged incident is interesting to me as it must have been around the time of Elizabeth’s pregnancy with their son, Richard Browder, and this birth may have had something to do with his refusal in this situation, as he probably needed to be home.  Obviously, John Browder had already made his reasoning clear, and it was accepted, whatever it was. 

Later, on June 22, 1722 (on the same day his brother, George Andrew Browder, was granted 264 acres of land in the same general area) John Browder I received a grant of 100 acres on both sides of Gravelly Run.  This land was probably that which had already been surveyed during 1712.  Also, on June 22, 1722, John Browder I had even more land surveyed (p. 482). 

Later, the Bristol Parish Vestry paid John Browder 100 pounds of tobacco per month for allowing Robert Glidewell to board with the family, since Glidewell was unable to work.  The Bristol Parish Vestry Book, in a notation dated April 14, 1728, (pp. 88-89) mentions this situation in the following manner:

Rob Glidewell Being afflicted With a Cancur in his face which hath Made him unable to labour for his livelihood it is ord’red that the Church Warthen find him necessary board and he to be allow’d one hundred pounds of tob* Pr month.

             And, on “Octbr 1728”:

1730 South Side Bristol Parish Dr. (pp. 52-53)

To JOHN BROWDER . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . . 1,000  80

This Robert Glidewell, by the way, is probably the one who married Elizabeth Nash, daughter of Robert Nash, about 1720 in Henrico County, Virginia.  Robert Glidewell, born about 1701 in London, England, son of William Gladwell and Alice Bryant, was transported to Virginia in 1716, and died of cancer in 1729. 

One of Robert Glidewell’s sons, Robert (October 23, 1722-c1809), married Elizabeth Byrd (c1732-c1782), daughter of William Byrd (c1704-bef. 1777) and Jean (Bell?/c1705-aft. 1765), about 1756.  This William Byrd was not the famous William Byrd of Westover, but was, instead, his uncle.  But the connection must not be lost to the reader. 

John and Elizabeth Browder were obviously, therefore, members of the Church of England, residing within the boundaries of Bristol Parish, Virginia, just as Edmund and Elizabeth Browder, John’s parents, had been.  They obviously attended services within the very brick church which still exists today.  However, during 1742, Bath Parish was formed from part of Bristol Parish.  It seems that this family resided within the boundaries of the new parish, as records of them in Bristol Parish cease about 1741/42.

Further, on October 15, 1741, John Browder I was granted 325 additional acres of land.  At that point, then, his property holdings totaled at least 725 acres.  On this deed, John signed only with a mark, “B”, so he was likely unable to write or sign his name (but that did not necessarily mean that he was illiterate, as some misunderstand).  Two of his closest neighbors were John Eppes and Isham Eppes, and some of John’s land bordered land owned by Isham Eppes. 

Also, on September 20, 1745, John Hailes sold 74 acres of land located “on the heads of the branches of Gravelly Run and Hatcher’s Run” to William Sanders.  This property was bordered by the properties of John Browder, William Sanders, and Jeffrey Mumford (PGCDB, p. ?). 

And, on September 3, 1750, John and Elizabeth Browder together sold some of the land they had acquired on June 2, 1737 from John Scoggan [(c1697-July 1760), son of George Scoggan (b. c1665)], to Joseph Crook [son of George Crook and his wife, Elizabeth] of St. Andrew’s Parish (Brunswick County Deed Book [BCDB] 5, 1751-1755, pp. 35-37).  This particular tract was located on the south side of Waqua Creek, which was located in Brunswick County at that time, because some of John Browder’s land was in that part of Prince George County which was later formed into Brunswick County just across the Nottoway River.  This deed transaction follows verbatim:

Here it must be emphasized that, contrary to the prevailing belief among some researchers, making a mark, rather than signing one’s own name, is NOT necessarily a sign of illiteracy.  There are any number of reasons why a person either may not have been able to write their own name (such as arthritis, etc.), or may not have wanted to do so.  Or a person may have only been used to printing, but unused to, or unable to, actually sign their name, having never been taught to write cursive.  In these early records, it is actually fairly common for persons that we know to have been literate to simply make their mark rather than signing their name.  I have seen many, many of them. 

But what is of even more interest here is the known fact that, most of the time, people who did not sign their own names simply made an “X” mark.  That was the usual way practically everyone did it.  However, in this document alone, we see that John Browder’s mark was a “B”, and that James Love’s mark was an “I”.  Some may state that I am taking this a little too far, but it seems to me that, since Elizabeth did make her mark with an “X”, that shows us something. 

It is my opinion that this indicates that Elizabeth had no middle name, or initial, while both John Browder and James Love DID.  Therefore, they both wrote their middle initials as their marks.  I won’t go so far as to guess the middle name of James Love, although one great candidate comes to mind (Isham).  But I will state here that it seems more than logical and reasonable to me that, if “B” truly did stand for John Browder’s middle initial, that his middle name was very likely BOLLING  and that, therefore, his full name would have been John Bolling Browder.  This is more than plausible, although it can’t be proven with this document alone.  So, I don’t call him John Bolling Browder within this work, although I am fairly certain that this should be his full name. 

I must emphasize here that, although John Browder wrote a “B” as his mark, which someone could say stands for the first letter in Browder, James Love wrote neither a “J” nor an “L”.  So, his mark stands for the first letter of neither James nor Love.  All of this, to me, clearly indicates that the marks made, except for Elizabeth’s “X”, stood for the first letters of their middle names.  So, there can be little doubt, at least, that John Browder’s middle initial was, in fact, “B”, whatever it may have stood for.  And the most likely, and logical, possibility, according to my own research, is that it stood for Bolling. 

It is also important to note the slim possibility here that Elizabeth might, instead, have been a Scoggan, rather than a Jones.  It is said that Scoggan is a Gaelic name, and can be Welsh in origin, as well as Scottish and Irish.  It is possible that Elizabeth could have been a sister of the John Scoggan, son of George Scoggan, and grandson of the George Scoggan (b. c1630, England), mentioned above.  However, it is unknown whether George Scoggan (b. c1665, England) had a daughter named Elizabeth or not.  I simply suggest it as another possibility while maintaining that, based on available evidence, Arthur Jones (as I will show) is much more likely to have been her father.  And, I must add, those who research this branch of the Scoggan family maintain that they were of Scottish origin, not Welsh, in any case.  Furthermore, the surname Scoggan is actually an old Scandinavian name (as also is “Crook”, by the way), meaning “the bearded one”.  So, Scoggan is actually a poor candidate for Elizabeth’s maiden name.  From BCDB 5, p. 42:

Indenture made 8 October 1750, between William Scogin Senr. of Brunswick County, St. Andrews Parish, and William Scogin Junr. of same, 50 Pounds, 300a, on North side of Sturgeon Run on the North side of the Reedy branch, it being part of a tract of Land containing 383a granted to the said William Scogin by Patent dated 28 September 1728.  Wit:  William Scogin, Martha Scogin.  Wit:  John Wall, James Love, Joseph Crook.  Court 26 March 1751, Indenture acknowledged by William Scogin Senr., and Martha the wife of the said William personally appeared and relinquished her right of Dower. 

It is also interesting to note the following deed transaction excerpt from BCDB 6, p. 463:

It is also interesting to note that the original home of John and Elizabeth Browder is said to have still been standing as late as 1963, at that time being owned by Mrs. George R. Slate.  They both died, and are buried, where they resided, in Dinwiddie (previously Prince George) County, Virginia.  They very well could have been buried in or near the old Blanford brick Church and Cemetery, but their gravesites, like those of the other early Browders, appear to have been lost to time. 

Now, getting to the possible ancestry of Elizabeth, the difficulty in finding her parentage should actually go without saying.  Which Welsh family should one look into?  Also, even in colonial times, the Jones family was already quite numerous, even within the borders of the American Colonies, from an early date.  But, based on available evidence, the Jones family is, and always has been, the best candidate for Elizabeth’s ancestry. 

So, with that as a working hypothesis, I engaged in a process of elimination in order to ascertain the most likely candidate(s).  It should be noted, however, that information on the early Jones families is incredibly mixed up on the internet, and full of obvious errors.  I did rely heavily on said internet information, but believe I have come to a good picture of the early Jones families in certain areas of Virginia overall by utilizing extant records, where available, that were useful for this purpose.  Thankfully, most Jones families are completely eliminated without difficulty. 

To begin with, the surname Jones is first found in Denbighshire, Wales, where Llanerchrugog served as their ancient family seat.  There are three main original sources for the surname Jones; from (1) Gwaithvoed, Lord Cardigan, Chief of one of the fifteen noble tribes of northern Wales in 921 CE; from (2) Bleddyn Ap Cynfyn, King of Powys; and from (3) Dyffryn Clwyd, a Chieftain of Denbighshire about the eleventh century.  Although originating in Wales, some members of this family early moved to Ireland (houseofnames.com/Jones Family Crest and History). 

Regardless, all of this elimination, both of Jones and other families that could have been candidates, leaves the very best possibility of Elizabeth’s parentage as being Lt. Arthur Jones of Isle of Wight County, Virginia and his wife, Susanna King.  They are known to have had a daughter named Elizabeth (b. c1685), but little more seems to be known of her.  Some posit that she died by 1716, but this is an obvious error, based on the fact that no record is found of her after her mention in her mother’s Will of 1691 and a misreading of her father’s Will.  It is nothing more than an assumption, based on lack of evidence for her existence past a certain date.  I happen to disagree with that assumption, for reasons I will show.  

Lt. Arthur Jones (c1663-1715), son of Edmund/Edward Jones, was born in Oldland, Hanham, County Gloucestershire, England and died in Surry County, Virginia, where he had been a miller.  He married (1) Susannah King (c1665-1691), daughter of Henry King (proven by the Will of John Newman, Isle of Wight County Will Book 1, p. 192, as well as the marriage record in IOWCW&DB 2, p. 192 & 565/Chapman, p. 26), in 1679 in Isle of Wight County, Virginia, and later, (2) Prudence Baldwin (c1660-aft. January 22, 1715), about 1695.  Susannah had previously been married to the said (1) John Newman (d. 1679), briefly, prior to her marriage to (2) Arthur Jones.  In his Will, Newman gave Arthur Jones the estate due his wife, Susanna. 

Susannah died in Isle of Wight County, Virginia, very likely due to complications from childbirth, since that was extremely common in those days and she was still of childbearing age.  Prudence died in Surry County, Virginia. 

Susannah wrote her own Will, dated December 19, 1691, so, sadly, even at her young age, she somehow knew she would soon die.  In her Will, she named her husband, Arthur Jones, her father, Henry King, and her daughters, Mary and Elizabeth, so they were still living at least as late as December of 1691.  This Will was witnessed by John Person, Richard Bennett, Jr., John Person, Jr., Richard Jones [brother of her husband, Arthur Jones], and Thomas Proud (IOWCWB I or II, p. 565). 

Still, one has to ask here why Susannah did not name her soon-to-be-born son, Arthur, in her Will, since he was obviously still living.  The reason for this is probably also obvious.  There was no way for Susanna to really know whether the child she carried would live or not and, frankly, she could not give him a name because she did not know that he would be a boy yet. 

This Will was not proven until May 26, 1713, which presents a problem.  If Elizabeth and Mary had been deceased by 1713 (as some researchers state), there would surely have been some notation of this in the record when said Will was proven.  But no such mention is made, so it becomes obvious that they were still alive.  In addition to this, Susanna’s son, Arthur, would have reached the age of twenty-one by this time.  So, although he did not stand to inherit anything, that is a reasonable explanation for why it was not until 1713 that her Will was finally probated.  All of her children had become adults by the standard of the time.  A copy of the actual Will of Susannah Jones (microfilm courtesy of the Virginia State Library), follows:

In any case, Arthur lived several years past Susanna’s death and, in the meantime, remarried.  One interesting incident which took place in the interim is mentioned thusly.  Among those who signed the following address were Lt. Arthur Jones and Cornet Thomas Clarke (Boddie, p. 169). 

In 1702 the Magistrates and militia of Isle of Wight county prepared an address of loyalty to Queen Anne. They stated that they “resented the attempt made upon our Religion, Laws and Liberty by the late unaccountable action of the French King in owning and declaring the pretended Prince of Wales to be the King of England.”

The Will of Arthur Jones was written on January 22, 1715, and probated on May 16, 1716 in Surry County, Virginia.  In it, Arthur names his daughter, “Eliz. Jones”, commonly accepted by some other researchers to be the daughter of Prudence, rather than Susanna.  However, that is not proven and, frankly, it is mere guesswork to suggest that he had two separate daughters named Elizabeth by two separate wives.  I suggest that Elizabeth had married John Browder I in 1715, the year Arthur Jones wrote his Will, but soon after said Will was written.  That is absolutely perfect to fit marrying John Browder I, and having her first child, Arthur, later in 1715. 

Plus, Arthur specifically uses her maiden name of “Jones” here, while the only other daughter he calls by a surname is Mary Cook, all the others simply being referred to by their given names.  This could signify that Elizabeth was about to be married, but had not yet been married.  At the very least, the mention of Elizabeth in her father’s Will proves she was still alive, contrary to what other researchers think. 

Overseers of said Will were James Sammon and Giles Underhill.  Witnesses were Jones Williams, William Bridges, and William Beech.  From Surry County Will Book 7, p. 16:

In the Name of God, Amen.  I ARTHUR JONES being very ill but in perfect sence being Desirous to make this my last Will and Testament.  First I give my Sole to God Almighty, my body to the Ground to be Decently buried.

Item  I give to my Daughter Mary Cook one Shilling.

Item  I give to my Daughter Sarah a parcel of Land lying between Joseph’s Swamp and Nottoway River Beginning at Doby’s Lower Corner tree so running on Doby’s Line to a red oak Corner tree thence Southerlyward to the River and down the River to a marked tree so running down the River to the fishing place and down the bottom to a mark and then Southerlyward to the River and down the River to the mouth of Joseph’s and a Cow and Calf to her and her heirs forever.

Item  I give to my Daughter Jane a parcel of Land beginning at Doby’s Corner pine to Southerly to the River and a Cow and Calf and two pewter Dishes to her and her heirs forever.

Item   I give to my Daughter Mary all the rest of my Land after her Mother’s Decease to her and her heirs forever.

Item  I give to my Daughter Prudence my Negro boy Dick provided that old John Freeman will acknowledge the land to his son Henry where he now Liveth to him and his heirs forever after so done I give the Negro to them and their heirs forever.

Item  I give to my Daughter ELIZ JONES my Negro boy Darby to her and her heirs forever and a Cow and Calf and two Pewter Dishes.

Item  I give to my Loving Wife Prudence all the rest of my Estate real and Personal with two Negroes named Lew and Robert to her and her heirs forever.

Now after all I make my Loving Wife Prudence my Executrix first to pay the Debts then the Legacys of this my Last Will and Testament. . . .

The known children of Arthur and Susannah Jones were Mary (b. c1682), Elizabeth (b. c1685), and Arthur (c1691-aft. 1762).  All children were born in Isle of Wight County, Virginia. 

The known children of Arthur and Prudence Jones were Prudence (c1698-c1760/m. 1717), and Sarah (b. c1699).  There is no basis for positing any other children for Prudence, including one named Elizabeth, as far as I can tell.  All children were probably born in Surry County, Virginia. 

After going over as many records as reasonably possible, I have no doubt left that Elizabeth did indeed marry John Browder I, and that this is the very reason that one finds no further record of Elizabeth Jones after 1715.  Following her marriage to John Browder, she, naturally, would have always been referred to as Elizabeth Browder.  And, as Arthur’s Will was written in January of 1715, Elizabeth Jones and John Browder I could easily have been married shortly afterward and still have had their first child, Arthur, later that same year. 

Elizabeth’s death prior to 1715 is simply conjecture, mainly based on a misreading of Arthur’s Will.  The fact that Arthur refers to Prudence as Mary’s “mother” is meaningless, since she was, effectively, Mary’s mother since Susanna had died.  Who else would be referred to as Mary’s “mother” in such a Will?  Arthur was not calling Prudence Mary’s natural mother, but her effective mother.  And, besides that, we, again, have proof that Mary, daughter of Susanna, still lived, regardless of what some seem to think. 

Mary Jones (b. c1682), daughter of Arthur and Susannah Jones, married Thomas Cooke, Sr. (c1677-November 22, 1736), son of William Cooke and Joane Roper, on February 17, 1702 in Isle of Wight County, Virginia (Isle of Wight Will & Deed Book I, p. 383/Chapman, p. 11).  Thomas was born in, and died in, Isle of Wight County. 

Boddie (p. 649) also mentions a deed made between “Thomas Cooke and Mary his wife, dau. of Arthur Jones” and Robert Sherrer, for 1200 pounds of tobacco, in which 50 acres of land where Robert Sherrer resided, located on the main Blackwater, was purchased.  This transaction took place on February 17, 1702, and was witnessed by John Brett, Richard Holliman and Thomas Harris (IOWCDB 1/no page reference provided).  So, with this land deed, the union between Thomas Cooke and Mary Jones, daughter of Arthur Jones, is proven. 

The known children of Thomas and Mary Cooke were Thomas, Jones, Sarah, Susanna, Benjamin, John, Rebecca, and Arthur.  Thomas Cooke, Sr. names these children in his Isle of Wight County Will, dated January 21, 1735. 

Arthur Jones, son of Arthur and Susannah Jones, married Mary Jordan (c1700-aft. 1762) about 1720.  Their known children were Arthur (b. 1722), and James (1725-c1780). 

Prudence Jones, daughter of Arthur and Prudence Jones, married Henry Freeman (c1690-1753), son of John Freeman, Sr.  The known children of Henry and Prudence Freeman were Josiah (c1720-1774), Henry (c1722-1776), Joel (b. c1724), Jones, Arthur (c1718-1752), and Amy (b. c1729). 

Now, to trace the Jones family descent, it begins with an unknown Jones who was born prior to 1506 and resided at Britton, Glouchestershire, England.  He had three known sons, John (bef. 1523-1584), unknown (b. bef. 1523), and Jones (bef. 1523-cMay, 1584).

John Jones (d. 1584), son of unknown Jones, was born at and died at Britton, Gloucestershire, England.  He wrote a Will in May of 1584 and refers in it to a lease of land he had in Oldland.  He married Maryann prior to 1540.  Their known children were Edmund (bef. 1540-aft. July 2, 1575), Anne, Thomas “the older” (b. bef. 1565), Thomas “the younger”, Joan/Jane, William, Anthony, Elizabeth, Julius, and John.

Nothing really seems to be known of Thomas Jones “the younger” except that he is the father of Robert Jones (bef. 1557-cNovember 1604) of Epikings Hall, according to John Jones’ Will.  Robert and his brother, John, would have inherited in the stead of their father, Thomas, if he died before his father, John. 

Robert Jones, son of Thomas Jones “the younger”, married Marjory (d. aft. November 1, 1604).  The known children of Robert and Marjory Jones were Arthur I (b. bef. 1574), and Rowland (bef. 1574-bef. November 1604). 

The Will of Robert Jones, written in November of 1604, provided mainly to his grandchildren, the children of Arthur Jones I, which was to be divided between them after Robert’s widow died. Apparently, Arthur was Robert’s only living child at the time of the writing of his Will, because Rowland had died.  Robert is buried at Oldland, England. 

Arthur Jones I married Eleanor Pitman on July 4, 1591 at Hanham, England.  Their known children were Martha, Mary (d. 1607), Edmund (b. c1594), Arthur II (b. c July 1597), Ffrydeswede (a female/b. c April 1602), Sara (b. c June 1604), and Henry (b. c November 1606).  All children were born at Hanham, England. 

Arthur Jones II married Joren Pychar on April 7, 1619 at Hanham, England.  The children of Arthur Jones II and Joren Pychar were Arthur III (c1624-1692), Edmund/Edward (b. c1626), Samuel (b. c1628-September 1631), Samuel (c1632-bef. November 16, 1699), and Richard (c1633-bef. August 29, 1692). 

Edmund/Edward Jones, son of Arthur Jones II and Joren Pychar, owned land in Isle of Wight County, Virginia by October 12, 1653.  It is interesting to note an instance in which Quakers were meeting, in 1663, at William Yarett’s home, located on Pagan River in Isle of Wight County.  It was illegal, according to Virginia law, to practice the Quaker religion or for Quakers to meet in that time period.  The meeting had been found out, and William Yarrett, along with Edward Jones [Sr.], were arrested and held for some time afterward in the house of the Sheriff because they attended this meeting (Boddie, p. 115/Weeks, p. 26). 

So, Edward Jones appears to have at least dabbled in this religion.  It is also known that Edward Jones, Sr., as well as Edward Jones, Jr., both took part in Bacon’s Rebellion, along with others such as Robert Flake (misspelled as Flacke), Samuel Cooke, John Harris, and John Clarke (Boddie, pp. 160-61). 

It is unknown, however, who Edmund Jones married.  Still, besides Lt. Arthur Jones he certainly had two known sons, Edward (bef. 1665-1722), Richard (c1666-c1721), as well as one daughter, Sara (c1667-c1726).  Edward Jones, Jr. married Deborah Exum (Boddie, p. 452). 

Henry King (d. c1669) was a son of John King (d. bef. May 14, 1655) and Jane.  Some say that Henry’s name was actually William, but it was more likely William Henry King. 

Henry King married Gulian Ruth Connor (c1636, England-c1678) about 1656 in Surry County, Virginia.  Gulian was transported to Virginia on January 24, 1656 by William “Caulfill” (Caulfield). 

Gulian died in Isle of Wight County, Virginia at only about forty-two years of age.  Prior to her death, since Mr. King had died early, she married again to (2) Darby Stantlin, and later to (3) Mathew Waikley. 

Boddie (p. 172) mentions several persons called “viewers of tobacco” for Isle of Wight County during the years 1639-40, which included Henry King, covering the area “from Castle Creek to the Alps”, along with William Lawson, Justinian Cooper, and William Ellis.  He further mentions (p. 539) a deed in which Maj. John Bond sold Thomas Harris a corn mill standing at the head of Pagan Creek on January 13, 1663.  This deed was witnessed by Henry King and Alice Phillips.  And Henry King, along with Alice Phillips, also witnessed a deed, dated October 20, 1664, in which Thomas Harris and Alice, his wife, sold Maj. John Bond 190 acres of land, located at the head of Pagan Creek (Boddie, p. 539-40/IOWCW&DB 1). 

Also, in 1665, Henry King agreed with Phil. Hanniford that, after Thomas Woodward measured 500 acres from the creek to the property of Mr. Ayers, that Phil. Hanniford could run a plot between Woodward and Henry King above the said branch.  This was witnessed by Robert Flake and John Harris.  And, on September 2, 1665, Henry King had 100 acres of land which he allowed Frances England to have in exchange for 100 of England’s land.  Robert Flake and Thomas Woodward witnessed this deed (Boddie, p. 543/IOWCW&DB 1). 

And on June 4, 1666, Robert Flake gave John Proctor, son of Ambrose Proctor, Sr. some property.  This was witnessed by Martin Luther and Henry King (Boddie, pp. 544-45/IOWCW&DB 1).  Finally, on April 18, 1668, Henry King was granted 1,000 acres of land located on the first swamp of the Blackwater, 750 acres of this having formerly been granted to John Sweet on September 25, 1643 (Boddie, p. 688). 

In his Will, dated March 2, 1668, and probated on May 3, 1669, Henry King left legacies to his daughters, Susanna and Elizabeth, of 900 acres of land, which had been given to him by his deceased father, and which had been confirmed by his mother, Elizabeth, since deceased.  He also names his wife, Ruth, to whom he gave 100 acres of land, located next to the property of Mr. England.  And he gave legacies to his Parish for the maintenance of a free school; to his servant, John Russell; and to his two daughters “to be brought up to learning.”  Witnesses were Edward Poynter and John Holly (IOWCW&AB 1, p. 61/Chapman, p. 10). 

The Will of Darby Stantlin, dated April 25, 1670, and recorded on October 25, 1670, left legacies to his wife, Gulian, whom he states was “sick at the present time.”  He stated that, should she die, that his friend Mathew Waikley was to remain on his plantation until Henry King [her son] “becomes of age.”  Also, “[i]n case of wife’s death and of her son Henry King” his plantation was to go to Mathew Waikley.  His Will was witnessed by Nicolas Cobb, William Cole, and Richard Sharpe. 

Bequests to his son, Henry King, were acknowledged in open court by Gulian Stantlin, and confirmed by Mathew Waikley, “her new husband” (IOWCW&AB 1, p. 90/Chapman, p. 14). 

Henry and Gulian King, as can be seen here, had at least one son, who was probably their only son, Henry (b. c1656/58).  Since he was orphaned as a child, he chose Robert Flake to be his guardian on February 10, 1672.  He later married (1) Katherine Clarke, daughter of John Clarke (d. 1717) and Mary Flake of Surry County, Virginia, in 1684 (Surry County [small] Deed Book 2, p. 13/Chapman, p. 29).  Henry King later married (2) Sarah Relick (or Williams, as some believe).  Boddie (p. 521) mentions the following:

Whereas pat. was granted 19 April 1648 to John King and Lawrence Ward for 500 acres at head [of] Pagan Creek, John King being dead said LAWRENCE WARD conveys unto King’s wife MRS. ELIZABETH KING, widow.  14 May 1655.  Teste, Thomas Davis. 

So, we know that John King, father of Henry King, lived to at least April 19, 1648, and that he was deceased by May 14, 1655.  We know, then, that, at the time of his death, he had a wife named Elizabeth.  Whether she was a second wife, or other researchers are incorrect, or her name was, perhaps, Elizabeth Jane, we may never know.

The known children of John and Elizabeth Browder, several of whom are known to have been baptized by the Rev. George Robertson, were Arthur (c1715-c1738), Edmund (b. c1716), John II (1717-c1814), Richard (c1719-c1807), William (July 7, 1723-c1763), George (Feb. 5, 1731-c1819), Anne (b. Feb. 13, 1733), and Joseph (February 2, 1741-March 1801), and possibly others. 

It is interesting to note that, among these children, for the first time, as far as is known in the Browder family, we have the names Arthur, Richard, Joseph, and Susanna.  These names correlate to names of children of Lt. Arthur Jones.  The other names, John, William, Edmund and George were already in the Browder family.  

As we will see, the names Arthur, Richard, Joseph, Frederick, Margaret, and Jones (as a given name) appear EXCLUSIVELY within the family lines descended directly from John and Elizabeth Browder.  This shows a direct correlation to the Jones family from which they likely descend.  It should also be noted that, had they been descended from Peter Jones, as famous as he was, one would expect some descendants to have been named Peter.  But I have never yet found a Peter Browder, for example. 

One should also note that NO descendant, as far as is known, of John and Elizabeth Browder was named Prudence, so the likelihood is increased that Elizabeth was the daughter of Susanna King rather than Prudence Baldwin.  That is another reason that I favor the belief that the first Elizabeth Jones lived past childhood, despite what some researchers suggest.

Now, it should also be noted that there are some gaps between these children, most notably between the years 1719 and 1723, 1723 and 1731, as well as 1733 and 1741.  It is entirely possible that this couple had more children during these year gaps, and might even have had more after 1741, even though Elizabeth would have been about 54 years of age by that time.  I would find the latter unlikely, however.  

Finally, it seems obvious to me that John Browder I died about 1757. The reason for this is that on Dec. 3, 1757, a John Browder, a Richard Browder, and a “Wm. Browder” are all listed in the Dublin County, Ireland Court of Chancery Bill Books 1757-1759, Vol. 35, p. 119. It is a good possibility that it might pertain to our John Browder, Richard Browder, and William Browder, all sons of John Browder I. In fact, it is highly unlikely that the references could be to anyone else other than the three brothers because, even though other possible relatives are listed in said records, (1) these three have the correct surname spelling for our family and (2) are all referenced in the very same record on the same page. The question here is just what said record pertained to and whether the brothers had to return to Ireland in order to deal with it, afterward returning back home.

An additional question would be what might have taken place around this year for them to be referenced in such records. I can only suggest that it might have taken that long for any part of their father’s estate, if any existed, to finally be settled in Ireland. It is entirely possible that only the three brothers made the trip but represented all of the other siblings in so doing. Could it possibly be that it was because of such a trip that later generations remembered three Browder brothers arriving in Virginia from Ireland? In such a case, they simply forgot what the trip was about and relegated it to immigration.

Please recall that Arthur Browder was deceased and his brother, Edmund Browder, died about this time also, so he was probably too ill to make the trip with the other three. George and Anne were living, but George may not have wanted to make the trip, and Anne would have been represented by someone else in any case. That left Joseph, who was likely considered too young to make the trip.

Regardless of the circumstances, it is clear from the record title that it concerned land. This seems to be a clear indication that John B. Browder I died about this time, leaving some land in Ireland as part of his estate which had to be dealt with.
